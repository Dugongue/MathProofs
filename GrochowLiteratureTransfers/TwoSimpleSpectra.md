# Compact tensor isomorphism with two simple mode spectra

Let $A,B$ be three-tensors over $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. In a specified pair of modes suppose the two flattening Gram matrices at each endpoint have simple spectrum. Then exact compact tensor isomorphism reduces to a system of signs over $\mathbb F_2$ in the real case, or an integer lattice test of phase equations in the complex case. The third mode can have arbitrary multiplicities, including an arbitrarily large nullspace. For rational real inputs the decision algorithm is deterministic polynomial bit time. For complex inputs the exact decision algorithm uses a polynomial number of algebraic operations and comparisons after algebraic spectral decomposition in the exact arithmetic model. The complex assertion gives no bit bound for products across many spectral fields. In either setting the proof recovers a transporter when the tests pass.

Chizewer, Everett, Mithal and Qiao’s 2026 algorithm uses simple spectra in every mode to reduce tensor isomorphism to a torus orbit. Remark 2.1 identifies the remaining full unitary action in a repeated eigenspace. Here a second Gram calculation eliminates the entire third-mode unitary action at once. The surviving torus acts on a four-index matrix with explicit integer weights. A second algorithm below uses just one simple marginal when a family of intrinsic Gram matrices has scalar commutant. These give deterministic tractable target families for the orthogonal and unitary versions of Part III, Open Question 1.8. [Random tensor isomorphism, Theorem 2.1 and Remark 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27128v1#S2) · [Part III, Open Question 1.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.03135v2#S1.SS5)

## Eliminating the unrestricted mode

By permuting the roles of the modes assume the specified pair is modes 1 and 2. Compare the corresponding ordered spectra and reject if either differs. For each tensor take unit eigenvectors in increasing spectral order in the two specified modes. An isomorphism must carry corresponding eigenlines to each other because flattening Gram matrices transform by unitary conjugation on their own mode. In these coordinates write $x_{ij},y_{ij}$ for the vectors in the third mode. Isomorphism is equivalent to
$$
y_{ij}=a_i b_j W x_{ij}
$$
for one unitary (or orthogonal) $W$ and phases $a_i,b_j$ of modulus one (signs in the real case). Define $G_{ij,kl}=\langle x_{ij},x_{kl}\rangle$ and $H_{ij,kl}=\langle y_{ij},y_{kl}\rangle$, with the inner product linear in its second argument. The exact condition on the phases is
$$
H_{ij,kl}=\overline{a_i b_j}\,a_k b_l\,G_{ij,kl}.
\tag{1}
$$
Necessity is immediate from unitarity. For sufficiency, the family $(\overline{a_i b_j}y_{ij})$ has the same Gram matrix as $(x_{ij})$. Equality of Gram matrices gives equality of squared norms of all corresponding linear combinations, so the correspondence on generators gives a well-defined linear isometry between their spans, with inverse the reverse correspondence. Both ambient spaces have the same dimension. Extend the isometry by taking orthonormal bases of their equal-dimensional orthogonal complements and matching them. This yields the desired $W$ without any assumption on the third flattening.

## Solving the phases

Zero entries of $G,H$ must agree, and all matching nonzero entries must have equal modulus. For each such position take the unit label $H_{ij,kl}/G_{ij,kl}$ and a row with weight $-e_i-e'_j+e_k+e'_l$. Here $e_i,e'_j$ are the standard basis vectors of the two phase blocks. Rows can vanish, in which case their label must be $1$.

In the real case each label is a sign. Use $1\mapsto0$ and $-1\mapsto1$ and solve the resulting linear system over $\mathbb F_2$. For rational real tensors, the initial eigenvalues are real algebraic numbers of degree bounded by the respective mode dimensions, with polynomial heights and isolation bit length from the characteristic polynomials. Each needed Gram entry uses at most four eigenvectors, and comparing the paired entries uses at most eight. For an individual eigenline, its vectors are obtained by solving over the field generated by its eigenvalue and taking one square root to normalize. Choose the sign deterministically, for example by a positive first nonzero coordinate. For any entry comparison the compositum degree is bounded by the product of a constant number of polynomial degrees. The heights and expression sizes for these bounded-degree calculations have polynomial bit bounds, using exact linear algebra, real algebraic number operations and root isolation. Thus each comparison of zeros or of $H$ with $\pm G$ can be done in polynomial bit time without building the field generated by the full eigenbases. Together with binary elimination this gives the stated decision bound. A witness can be expressed with algebraic arithmetic and square roots via the extension above.

Over $\mathbb C$, form the integer matrix $M$ of weights. Unimodular row operations combine phase labels by the same products and inverses, and unimodular column operations are invertible monomial changes of phase variables. Compute Smith normal form with transformations. A nonzero diagonal entry poses a single power equation on the unit circle, which is always solvable by taking a root. A zero row requires its transformed label to be $1$. Equivalently, all integer relations among the weight rows must yield product $1$ among their labels. Smith transformations have polynomial bit complexity, and all label products use binary powering. This proves the exact-arithmetic bound. All roots used for construction are complex algebraic for algebraic inputs, and every root of a unit complex number has modulus one. [Kannan–Bachem, Polynomial algorithms for Smith and Hermite normal forms](https://doi.org/10.1137/0208040)


## One simple mode can suffice

There is a second polynomial-time real test on a different, checkable family. Assume the first mode has simple spectrum, and write $A_j$ for the matrix slices in the original coordinates. Let $L_A$ be the first-mode flattening Gram matrix, $r$ the first-mode dimension, and define matrices on mode 2 by
$
K_t(A)=\sum_{j,k=1}^r (L_A^t)_{kj} A_jA_k^*,
\qquad 0\le t<r.
$
Define $K_t(B)$ in the same way. Suppose the common matrix commutant of the $K_t(A)$ consists only of scalar matrices. This condition is checked by linear equations. This second test has the same real bit bound and complex exact-arithmetic bound, even if both remaining marginal spectra have repetitions.

To prove it, diagonalize $L_A$ conceptually and let $X_i$ be the slices in the eigenbasis, with ordered eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. The displayed formula becomes $\sum_i\lambda_i^t X_iX_i^*$. Thus for any tensor isomorphism its second-mode map conjugates each $K_t(A)$ to $K_t(B)$. The distinct eigenvalues also make the Vandermonde matrix invertible, so these weighted moments determine the individual slice Gram matrices. The rational real $K_t$ can be computed directly, before using algebraic eigenvectors.

Find the space of solutions of $T K_t(A)=K_t(B)T$ by linear algebra. If it is zero, reject. If $T$ is a nonzero solution, $T^*T$ commutes with all $K_t(A)$, since the matrices on both sides are Hermitian. Hence it is a positive scalar times the identity. Dividing $T$ by the positive square root of that scalar produces a unitary or orthogonal map $V$. Every possible second-mode map is $V$ times a scalar phase because the quotient of two solutions commutes with all $K_t(A)$. Absorb that common scalar into the first-mode phases. For rational real input, $T$ may be chosen rational and then $V$ uses just one additional algebraic square root.

Apply $V^{-1}$ to the second mode of $B$. In ordered first-mode eigenbases the remaining constraints are now $y_{ij}=a_i W x_{ij}$. The same third-mode Gram test and phase solver apply, with rows $-e_i+e_k$. The eigenvector and entry size bounds still hold because each comparison uses a constant number of algebraic eigenvectors and the normalization of $T$. The calculation gives a checkable sufficient condition for reducing the unknown action to phases on just one mode.

For example, take $X_i=u_i e_i^T$ in a $3\times2\times5$ real tensor, with $u_1=(20,0)$, $u_2=(9,20)$ and $u_3=(12,-15)$. The Frobenius norms squared $400,481,369$ are distinct and the slices are orthogonal. The second marginal is $625 I_2$ and the third has nullity two. The $u_i u_i^T$ have only scalar common commutants, since commuting with $u_1u_1^T$ makes a matrix diagonal and the off-diagonal entries of $u_2u_2^T$ force its diagonal entries to agree.

The Gram-preservation lemma is kernel checked in Lean against Mathlib’s Gram matrix definition. The complete argument above is a written proof. It concerns the compact action and gives a test on this family, with the unrestricted reduction from general-linear TI to compact TI still open.
