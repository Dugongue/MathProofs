# Optimal faithful permutation actions of nilpotent groups and conjugacy witnesses for affine constituents

## 1. Minimum faithful actions for every nilpotent Cayley-table group

Given the Cayley table of any finite nilpotent group, a deterministic logspace-uniform NC algorithm computes the minimum faithful permutation degree and constructs an action attaining it. All primes, exponents and nilpotency classes are allowed. This gives a restricted solution to the minimum faithful permutation degree question proposed by Kantor and Luks. In particular it covers all finite 2-groups and removes the earlier restriction on the central quotient of the Sylow 2-subgroup. [Kantor–Luks, Section 13](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~kantor/PAPERS/quotient.pdf) [Das–Thakkar, Cayley-table complexity](https://doi.org/10.1137/24M1685353)

### Largest subgroup excluding a central element

Let \(P\) be a finite \(p\)-group and let \(z\in Z(P)\) have order \(p\). Search for a normal elementary abelian subgroup \(E\) of order \(p^2\) containing \(z\), by trying \(E=\langle z,x\rangle\) for all elements \(x\) of order \(p\) outside \(\langle z\rangle\). If one is found, put \(T=C_P(E)\). The conjugation action of \(P\) fixes \(z\) and acts trivially on \(E/\langle z\rangle\), since \(\operatorname{Aut}(C_p)\) has order prime to \(p\). Its image lies in the group of unipotent matrices fixing the line pointwise, so \([P:T]\leq p\).

For a subgroup \(L\) excluding \(z\), either \(L\cap E=1\) or \(L\cap E\) is a line \(R\ne\langle z\rangle\). In the second case, \(L\) normalizes \(R\) and centralizes it because \(L\) is a \(p\)-group. It centralizes \(z\), hence \(L\leq T\). In the first case choose any line \(R\ne\langle z\rangle\) of \(E\). The subgroup \((L\cap T)R\) excludes \(z\), intersects \(E\) exactly in \(R\), and has order \(p|L\cap T|\geq |L|\). Thus some largest avoiding subgroup lies in \(T\) and includes one of the \(p\) lines \(R\ne\langle z\rangle\). For each such \(R\), recurse in \(T/R\) on the order-\(p\) central element \(zR\), lift the answer by inverse image, and retain a largest lift. Every child has order at most \(|P|/p\), so the recursion tree has at most \(1+p+\cdots+p^{\lfloor\log_p |P|\rfloor}\leq 2|P|\) nodes.

### Terminal groups

If there is no normal elementary abelian subgroup of order \(p^2\) containing \(z\), there is no such subgroup anywhere in \(P\). Otherwise an omitted \(E_0\) gives the normal elementary abelian group \(E_0\langle z\rangle\) with a \(P\)-invariant line modulo \(\langle z\rangle\), since any \(p\)-group acting on a nonzero \(\mathbb F_p\)-space has nonzero fixed vectors by orbit counting. The inverse image of that line gives the excluded subgroup.

In this terminal case \(P\) has a cyclic subgroup \(A\) containing \(Z(P)\), with index one at odd \(p\) and at most two at \(p=2\). This is the part of the classical normal-rank-one classification used here. It also has a short proof. A maximal abelian normal subgroup \(A\) is self-centralizing: otherwise the nontrivial normal subgroup \(C_P(A)/A\) of \(P/A\) contains a central subgroup of order \(p\), lifting to a larger abelian normal subgroup. Also \(A\) is cyclic: if \(A[p]\) had dimension at least two, the \(P\)-action would have an invariant two-dimensional subspace by taking a fixed line, then a fixed line in the quotient. Write \(A=\langle a\rangle\) of order \(p^e\), so \(P/A\) embeds in the unit group modulo \(p^e\). [Normal-rank-one classification, Theorem 4.6](https://web.mat.bham.ac.uk/D.A.Craven/docs/lectures/pgroups.pdf)

If this image is nontrivial for odd \(p\), it contains \(r=1+p^{e-1}\) of order \(p\), since the principal units are cyclic of order \(p^{e-1}\). If the image has order greater than two at \(p=2\), it meets the cyclic subgroup of units congruent to one modulo four, so \(e\geq3\) and it contains that subgroup's involution \(r=1+2^{e-1}\). These unit group facts follow by powering \(1+p\) and \(5\), respectively. In either case choose \(g\) inducing \(r\), so \(g^p=a^m\). Since \(g\) centralizes \(g^p\), one has \(p\mid m\). The sum \(1+r+\cdots+r^{p-1}\) has \(p\)-valuation one, since it is \(p\) modulo \(p^e\) for odd \(p\) and \(2+2^{e-1}\) for \(p=2\). Thus a suitable \(a^t g=x\) has order \(p\). The same power formula shows that the elements of order \(p\) in \(Ax\) are exactly \(xA[p]\). The coset is invariant under conjugation because \(P/A\) is abelian. The subgroup \(\langle x,A[p]\rangle\) is then a normal elementary abelian subgroup of order \(p^2\), a contradiction. This proves the index assertion, with \(Z(P)\subseteq A\) by self-centralization.

Find a suitable cyclic \(A\) by trying all cyclic subgroups in the table. Every subgroup excluding \(z\) meets \(A\) trivially, since any nontrivial subgroup of a cyclic \(p\)-group contains its unique order-\(p\) subgroup. Thus the optimum has order at most \([P:A]\leq2\). Trying the trivial and order-two subgroups gives an optimum.

### Coset actions and circuit complexity

Let \(S=Z(P)[p]\). A family of orbit stabilizers \(L_i\) gives a faithful action exactly when \(\bigcap_i(L_i\cap S)=1\). Indeed, the kernel is an intersection of cores, and any nontrivial normal subgroup of a \(p\)-group meets \(S\) nontrivially. If \(L\cap S\) has positive codimension \(k\), extend it to \(k\) hyperplanes having the same intersection. Replace \(L\) by its products with the hyperplanes. Each such product intersects \(S\) in its prescribed hyperplane. The sum of their indices is \(k p^{1-k}[P:L]\leq[P:L]\). A stabilizer containing \(S\) can be discarded. Thus an optimum is a minimum-weight basis in \(S^*\), with the weight of a functional equal to the least index of a subgroup meeting \(S\) in its kernel. For a hyperplane \(W\leq S\), pass to \(P/W\) and mark a generator \(z\) of \(S/W\). The largest avoiding subgroup in \(P/W\) lifts to a best stabilizer for \(W\). [Elias–Silberman–Takloo-Bighash, weighted-matroid formulation](https://doi.org/10.1080/10586458.2010.10129066)

For a finite nilpotent group perform this separately on the Sylow subgroups. The optimum degrees add: a subgroup of a direct product of Sylow subgroups is the product of its Sylow intersections, by taking prime-power components of its elements. Each orbit can be replaced by its Sylow coset actions of nontrivial degrees, inflated to the whole group. This preserves the joint kernel and costs no more than the original index, because the product of two or more integers at least two is at least their sum. Conversely the union of optimum Sylow actions is faithful. Handle the trivial group by the chosen degree convention.

For logspace-uniform NC, with \(n=|G|\), perform prime testing and extraction of Sylow subgroups simultaneously for primes up to \(n\), using element orders computed from the table by repeated squaring and parallel comparison of all possible exponents. For each marked pair, unroll the recursion above into a complete \(p\)-ary tree of depth \(\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor\), with unused branches masked. The tree has at most \(2n\) nodes. At one level all steps are NC table operations. In particular, testing an \(E\) and choosing the first passing candidate is a parallel list of equality and membership tests. The lines \(R\) are given by \(\langle xz^j\rangle\) for \(0\leq j<p\), and quotients are constructed by parallel comparison of cosets with representative compaction. The tables and marks propagate downward. Largest-order subgroup bitsets lift and are selected upward. There are \(O(\log n)\) levels in each direction.

Enumerate \(S^*\) using a nullspace basis for the linear equations \(\lambda(xy)=\lambda(x)+\lambda(y)\) over \(\mathbb F_p\) for the elements of \(S\). The space has at most \(n\) elements, so all coefficient vectors for the basis can be examined in parallel with padding and masks. Finite-field rank and the corresponding choice of row and column bases give a nullspace basis in deterministic uniform NC. Run a marked recursion for every nonzero functional in parallel. Sort the resulting weighted vectors, break ties by their input position, and select exactly the vectors whose prefix rank increases. This is the greedy minimum-weight basis rule, with all prefix ranks evaluated in parallel. Finally construct the coset permutation arrays by parallel table lookup and coset compaction, inflating each Sylow action by the other Sylow factors. The total degree is at most \(|G|\) by the regular-action bound. All search ranges, padded trees, and matrix dimensions are polynomial in the table length and constructible by a logspace uniformity machine. [Mulmuley, parallel finite-field rank](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579205)

## 2. Search-to-decision for permutation groups with affine prime-degree constituents

There is a deterministic polynomial-time Turing reduction from conjugacy search to ordinary permutation-group conjugacy decision for input groups whose nontrivial point orbits have sizes two or a common odd prime \(p\), with every size-\(p\) constituent conjugate into \(\mathrm{AGL}_1(\mathbb F_p)\). The prime may vary with the input. The algorithm returns a conjugating permutation on the original domain. In particular this holds for all permutation groups whose point orbits have size at most three. This is a nonabelian case of the search-to-decision question of Allender–Grochow–van Melkebeek–Moore–Morgan. [Permutation-group conjugacy search question](https://arxiv.org/html/1710.09806)

Use the previous orbit-marked reduction to find a complete orbit matching that extends to a conjugator. That reduction works for arbitrary groups by attaching decorated orbit copies with distinct prime labels and querying ordinary conjugacy. It remains to construct a conjugator respecting the chosen matching in deterministic polynomial time. [Orbit-marked reduction](orbit-marking-lemma.md#encoding-distinguished-point-orbits)

On each size-\(p\) orbit the local image is transitive and has a unique Sylow \(p\)-subgroup, regular cyclic of order \(p\), by the affine hypothesis. Enumerate the local group, of order at most \(p(p-1)\), and use a generator of this subgroup and an origin to identify the orbit with \(\mathbb F_p\). Each local permutation is of the form \(x\mapsto bx+a\). A conjugator respecting the orbit matching must send the translation subgroup to its counterpart, so each size-\(p\) block map is affine. On each two-point orbit any point bijection intertwines the two actions of its local group \(C_2\). Ignore fixed points until the end.

In these coordinates both groups lie in the same ambient semidirect product
\[
 X=U\rtimes M,\qquad U=\mathbb F_p^m,\qquad M=(\mathbb F_p^\times)^m\times C_2^\ell,
\]
where \(m\) is the number of size-\(p\) orbits and \(\ell\) the number of two-point orbits. The last factors act trivially on \(U\). For an input group \(K\), compute its multiplier image \(B\leq M\) and translation kernel \(A=K\cap U\). The multiplier projection acts on auxiliary disjoint copies of the nonzero field elements and the two-point sets. Compute the kernel as the pointwise stabilizer of those auxiliary points, with the faithful action on the original points retained. Its translation generators span \(A\) as an \(\mathbb F_p\)-space. The multiplier subgroups are compared by permutation-group membership in their common auxiliary action. [Permutation-group stabilizers and membership](https://doi.org/10.1145/28395.28439)

An ambient translation conjugates \(K\) to \(A\rtimes B\). In \((U/A)\rtimes B\), the image of \(K\) is the graph of a cocycle \(f:B\to U/A\). Summing \(f(bc)=f(b)+b f(c)\) over \(c\) shows that \(f(b)=(1-b)w\) for some \(w\), since \(|B|\) is prime to \(p\). To find the normalizing translation without enumerating \(B\), solve the linear equations requiring that each input generator \((a,b)\) has \(a+(1-b)t\in A\) for the unknown \(t\in U\). Existence follows from the cocycle argument. After conjugation by \(t\), the subgroup lies in \(A\rtimes B\), contains \(A\) and projects onto \(B\), hence equals it.

The normalized groups are conjugate by an affine block map exactly when their multiplier subgroups agree and a nonsingular diagonal matrix carries their translation spaces onto one another. Necessity holds because \(M\) is abelian and block multipliers act diagonally on translations. Sufficiency follows by conjugating the two split groups by that diagonal matrix. To construct it for equal-dimensional translation spaces \(A,A'\), compute the spaces
\[
 R=\{r\in\mathbb F_p^m : \operatorname{diag}(r)A\subseteq A\},\qquad
 W=\{w\in\mathbb F_p^m : \operatorname{diag}(w)A\subseteq A'\}
\]
by linear equations. The space \(R\) is a unital subalgebra for coordinatewise multiplication. Define a partition of coordinates by equality of all evaluations on \(R\). For each block, choose elements of \(R\) distinguishing it from every other block in turn, scale and translate each such element to give value one on the chosen block and zero on the other block, then multiply them. This gives the block idempotent, so \(R\) is exactly the space of tuples constant on each block.

If some invertible tuple \(u\in W\) exists, then \(W=uR\), by composing with the inverse diagonal map. The subspace of \(W\) supported on each block is therefore a line whose nonzero vectors have no zero coordinate in that block. Compute each supported subspace by linear equations, select a nonzero vector in each, and add them. Under existence this always yields an invertible tuple, and inversion checks the result. Apply the diagonal permutation, undo the normalizing translations and include the chosen orbit identifications and arbitrary matched fixed points. This returns an actual conjugator on the original domain in polynomial time with ordinary decision queries only at the orbit-matching stage.

Both results are paper proofs.
