# Recovering exactly the small-individual-degree factors of lacunary polynomials

## GP-0598

For rational lacunary input with binary exponents and an individual degree cap $d$, there is a randomized algorithm that with high probability returns precisely the irreducible factors satisfying the cap, with multiplicities, as exact evaluation programs. Its time is polynomial in the lacunary bit size and $d$. An expanded sparse output can also be recovered with bounded error in expected time polynomial in the input and actual output sizes. The deterministic completion remains open here. The algorithm has no assumed sparsity bound on the factors.

### Exact factor set in compact representation

Set $D=nd$, where $n$ is the number of variables. Every sought factor has total degree at most $D$. Apply the probabilistic bounded-total-degree factorization of Kaltofen–Koiran described in Section 1.2 of Grenet, with bound $D$. The remaining steps filter the individual degree caps and produce output in the desired representation. To specify the compact output and explain why no spurious candidate survives, the main procedure can also be read directly in Grenet's Sections 3 and 4.

Factors whose Newton polytope is a point are exactly monomial irreducibles, read from coordinate minima in the original exponent list. For one-dimensional factors, Grenet's projection onto primitive directions reduces to common univariate lacunary factors of bounded degree, including multiplicities. The directions can be taken from the differences of pairs of input exponents, a polynomial number of candidates, since a Minkowski summand's edge direction occurs in the Newton polytope of the product. In a primitive direction the univariate degree needed for a multivariate factor of total degree at most $D$ is at most $D$. Apply Lenstra's exact bounded-degree univariate factorization, then lift factors and retain the cap. Their sparse representations have at most $D+1$ terms.

For higher-dimensional factors, Grenet's multivariate gap partition with degree bounds at most $D$ yields polynomially many pieces. After removing their common monomials, each piece has individual degrees $O(D^4s^3)$ for input sparsity $s$, so their total degrees are polynomial. The multiplicity of every higher-dimensional factor of degree within the bound is the minimum of its multiplicities in these pieces, by Theorem 4.15. Factor the pieces with randomized straight-line or black-box polynomial factorization. Subtract from each list the one-dimensional factors, which may be identified by the same exact bounded-degree univariate projections of those pieces. Then retain the factor classes common to all lists, recording the minimum multiplicity and filtering for the degree caps. This is exact whenever the bounded-degree factorizations and coefficient tests succeed. [Grenet, Sections 3 and 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3570) · [Kaltofen–Trager factor evaluation programs](https://kaltofen.math.ncsu.edu/bibliography/90/KaTr90.pdf)

More explicitly, factor programs can be required to evaluate on rational points in polynomial bit time by using the black-box formulation of Kaltofen–Trager on the bounded-degree shifted pieces. Distinct candidates can be compared up to nonzero scalar using their cross-product at independently chosen sample points. Individual degrees can be found by sampling the other coordinates and interpolating in one coordinate at a time. Nonzero leading coefficients and nonproportional cross-products are detected with arbitrarily high probability using ordinary polynomial identity testing. The degree and coefficient bits of evaluating the shifted pieces satisfy the bounded-degree algorithm's input hypotheses. For the resulting irreducibles, normalized rational coefficients have polynomial bit length. For example clear denominators in a piece and choose primitive integer factors. A coefficient of an integer factor is bounded by its Mahler measure times the product of the binomial upper bounds in the individual degrees. Every other integer factor has Mahler measure at least one, so the measure of the chosen factor is at most that of the piece, which is at most its coefficient norm. Normalizing by the value at a nonzero small rational sample or by a leading coefficient preserves polynomial bit lengths. These bounds also give polynomial bit lengths on every rational evaluation of polynomial encoding size.

### Expanding just the factors that are returned

For a compact factor, choose distinct small rational primes $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ and query at $(p_1^j,\ldots,p_n^j)$. The resulting sequence is a sum of pure exponentials, one per monomial, with pairwise distinct bases $\prod_i p_i^{a_i}$. A sparsity bound $k$ gives deterministic reconstruction from the first $2k+1$ values when the actual sparsity is at most $k$. Factor the minimal linear recurrence polynomial over the rationals, decode every positive integral root into the exponent vector by the known primes, and recover the coefficients by the Vandermonde system. This is the sparse interpolation method of Ben-Or and Tiwari. Degrees and all value lengths are polynomial in $k$ and the original parameters. [Ben-Or–Tiwari interpolation](https://doi.org/10.1145/62212.62241)

Double $k$ from one, verifying every candidate against the returned exact program via polynomial identity testing. Reject candidates whose exponent vectors are outside the known total-degree bound, or that fail the recurrence and reconstruction checks. Once $k$ reaches actual sparsity, the procedure yields the exact expansion. Abort a stage if a factor-program evaluation or a stage computation exceeds its proven polynomial bit budget. A correct factor program and a correct stage always meet these budgets. Cap the doubling at the number of monomials of total degree at most $D$. The cap and the worst-case work below it are at most singly exponential in a polynomial in the input and numerical degree bound. Taking error probabilities smaller than its reciprocal by polynomial amplification bounds the contribution of failed factor or equality tests to expected running time. On correct runs only polynomial work in actual output is spent. This gives the claimed expected output-sensitive bound.

The current deterministic low-individual-degree candidate construction addresses a stronger derandomization requirement. The present result specifies the exact randomized output guarantee. [Current deterministic candidate-list context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27293)
