# Sharp local–global tensor isomorphism at diagonal rank six

Call a tensor of format \(n\times n\times n\) geometrically diagonal if it is equivalent over an algebraic closure to \(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i\otimes e_i\otimes e_i\). We prove a complete classification of the first failures of local–global ordinary tensor isomorphism in this class.

Over a number field, geometrically diagonal tensors of side at most five that are isomorphic at almost all places are isomorphic over the number field. At side six, there are exactly two types of nonisomorphic pairs that can be isomorphic at all places. Their common splitting groups are the Klein four group and the dihedral group of order eight. In each type, the two tensors are locally isomorphic everywhere exactly when every decomposition subgroup of the common splitting field is cyclic. Both types occur over \(\mathbb Q\). They admit integral multiplication-table representatives isomorphic modulo every positive integer, while their rational tensors are nonisomorphic.

The proofs use the known transitive Gassmann rigidity through degree six and the known Klein four Burnside relation. The contribution is the tensor interpretation, the classification of all nontransitive degree-six obstructions including the dihedral case, and the resulting local and extension-field criteria. [Lin–Shinder–Zimmermann, Proposition 2.5 and Examples 2.7, 2.14](https://api.algebraicgeometry.nl/Article/20075/2023-6-024.pdf)

## 1. The finite étale object carried by a diagonal tensor

For every field \(F\), finite étale \(F\)-algebras of dimension \(n\), up to isomorphism, correspond to ordinary \(F\)-tensor orbits within the geometrically diagonal orbit. The map sends an algebra to its multiplication tensor. It commutes with field extension.

First, the ordinary three-factor transporter between multiplication tensors of two finite-dimensional unital associative algebras is an isotopy. Write it as \(P(x)Q(y)=W(xy)\), where all three maps are invertible. Write \(a=P(1)\), \(b=Q(1)\). Substituting the unit shows that right multiplication by \(b\) and left multiplication by \(a\) are invertible. These elements are units. For example \(ac=1\) for some \(c\), and injectivity of left multiplication by \(a\) applied to \(ca-1\) gives \(ca=1\). Then
\[
\phi(x)=a^{-1}W(x)b^{-1}
\]
is an algebra isomorphism: substitution gives \(P(x)=a\phi(x)\), \(Q(x)=\phi(x)b\), and \(\phi(xy)=\phi(x)\phi(y)\). Conversely any algebra isomorphism and units \(a,b\) specify the isotopy in this way. These formulas work on test rings for finite free algebras. [Multiplication-tensor isotopy and degeneration](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04253)

The stabilizer of the multiplication tensor of \(F^n\) is consequently \((\mathbb G_m^n)^2\rtimes S_n\), with the same permutation on the two unit groups. It is smooth and the geometric orbit is homogeneous under \(\operatorname{GL}_n^3\). Twisting the homogeneous orbit, its rational orbits are the kernel of the cohomology map from the stabilizer to \(\operatorname{GL}_n^3\); the latter has trivial first cohomology by Hilbert 90. One can take the cocycle on a separable closure, because the geometric transporter is a nonempty smooth torsor and admits a separable point. The section of the semidirect product gives a lift of every \(S_n\)-cocycle. The torus occurring above that cocycle is a product of two copies of \(\operatorname{Res}_{E/F}\mathbb G_m\), where \(E\) is the finite étale algebra of the \(n\)-element Galois set of the cocycle. Its first cohomology is trivial by Hilbert 90 on the factors. Hence the \(S_n\)-cocycle class has exactly one lift class. This proves the stated correspondence, and the tensor attached to a lift along the section is exactly the multiplication tensor of \(E\).

In particular, the test at any completion or extension field is an isomorphism test of the restricted finite Galois sets. At a good prime the decomposition image is generated by a Frobenius element. Two tensors in this class are isomorphic there exactly when the two Frobenius permutations have the same cycle type. Thus for any pair over a number field the density of good isomorphism primes is the fraction of elements of the common splitting group with equal cycle types in the two actions, by Chebotarev. Isomorphism at almost all places implies equality of the two permutation characters. Conversely, equality of the characters implies equality of cycle types for every group element, by applying the character to its powers, hence local agreement at all unramified places. [Chebotarev and arithmetic equivalence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.01956)

## 2. Classification of the degree-six equal characters

Let \(G\) be a finite group and \(X,Y\) two six-element \(G\)-sets with the same permutation character. The kernels agree since they consist of the elements fixing all six points. Divide by this common kernel and assume both actions faithful. We use the published results that a pair of equal-character sets of degree at most five is isomorphic, and the same holds for a transitive pair of degree six. It remains to treat nontransitive pairs of degree six.

The possible nonisomorphic pairs, up to swapping and identifying the faithful group, are exactly these two:
- \(G=C_2\times C_2\). One set is the union of its three distinct transitive two-element quotients, and the other consists of the regular four-element set and two fixed points.
- \(G=D_8=\langle r,s:r^4=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle\). Define \(\lambda_V(r)=\lambda_E(r)=-1\), \(\lambda_V(s)=1\), \(\lambda_E(s)=-1\). One set is \(G/\langle s\rangle\) together with the two-element quotient of character \(1+\lambda_E\), and the other is \(G/\langle rs\rangle\) together with the two-element quotient of character \(1+\lambda_V\).

Here is an exhaustive argument. Equal characters imply equal numbers of orbits. If there are at least five orbits the possibilities are immediately isomorphic. With four orbits the patterns are \(3+1+1+1\) and \(2+2+1+1\). Only the first can have an element of order three in its faithful group. For each common pattern, cancel the fixed points and apply degree-at-most-five rigidity.

With three orbits the patterns are \(4+1+1\), \(3+2+1\), and \(2+2+2\). The middle pattern cannot be paired with the last, by the element of order three. If it were paired with the first, the group would embed in both \(S_4\) and \(S_3\times C_2\), with order divisible by four and three. It would be \(S_3\times C_2\), which has an element of order six and does not embed in \(S_4\). The first and last patterns can agree only for the Klein four group, since the faithful action on the four-element orbit must be an elementary abelian two-group acting regularly. Comparing the four linear characters forces the three two-element quotients to be distinct. This gives the first displayed pair. Common patterns with a fixed point reduce to smaller degree, and a common \(2+2+2\) pattern is determined by its three quadratic characters with multiplicities.

With two orbits the patterns are \(5+1\), \(4+2\), and \(3+3\). The first has an element of order five and neither other pattern does. A pairing of \(4+2\) with \(3+3\) would give a group of order divisible by twelve and dividing both \(48\) and \(36\), so of order twelve. The faithful action on the two three-element orbits embeds it in \(S_3\times S_3\). Each projected transitive image has order three or six. An order-three image gives a kernel of order four inside the other \(S_3\), which is impossible. If both images have order six, each kernel would be a normal subgroup of order two in the other \(S_3\), also impossible. A common \(5+1\) pattern reduces to smaller degree. A common \(3+3\) pattern is determined by the two rational irreducible two-dimensional summands in its permutation character. Each transitive three-element character is the trivial character plus one such irreducible, and each character determines the corresponding three-element set.

For a common \(4+2\) pattern, let \(K\) be the quotient acting faithfully on the four-point orbit, and let \(\chi\) be the nontrivial linear character of the two-point orbit. The transitive subgroups of \(S_4\) are \(C_4,V_4,D_8,A_4,S_4\). This list follows by order, with order four giving regular actions, order eight a Sylow subgroup, and order twelve the unique index-two subgroup. The reduced four-point character for \(A_4,S_4\) is irreducible of dimension three by two-transitivity, so its character and the two-point character can both be recovered. If \(K=V_4\), the faithful total group is elementary abelian of rank two or three and the four-point character is the sum of the characters in a rank-two subspace of its dual group. In rank two this subspace is the whole dual. In rank three the remaining character \(\chi\) is outside the subspace, so its three nonzero elements are the unique three-element subset of nonzero constituents summing to zero. Thus the four-point and the two-point characters are determined in this case as well.

For \(K=C_4\) or \(D_8\), the four-point character has the form \(1+\lambda+\psi\), where \(\lambda\) is nontrivial linear and \(\psi\) is a faithful two-dimensional rational irreducible of \(K\). For \(C_4\) this is the pair of primitive fourth-root characters together, and for \(D_8\) it is the usual square representation. The character \(\psi\) of the full group is unique among the constituents of dimension two, and its kernel determines the quotient \(K\). Hence the \(C_4\) and \(D_8\) cases cannot be paired. For \(C_4\) the four-point action is regular on this quotient and determined. For \(D_8\) there are two faithful four-point actions, the cosets of the two reflection classes, and their respective linear terms are \(\lambda_V,\lambda_E\). The total character determines the unordered pair \(\{\lambda,\chi\}\). If \(\lambda\) agrees then the four-point character and the two-point character each agree. If it differs, the linear terms must be swapped. In this case both linear terms factor through the \(D_8\) quotient and faithfulness of the total action forces \(G=D_8\). This is exactly the second displayed pair. In all cases in which individual transitive characters have been recovered, degree-at-most-five Gassmann rigidity recovers their orbits.

Both displayed pairs are nonisomorphic and have equal characters. For \(V_4\) each nonidentity element has cycle type \(1^2 2^2\) on both sets. For \(D_8\) both characters are \(2+\lambda_V+\lambda_E+\psi\), and the four-point orbits have nonconjugate stabilizers. This completes the classification.

## 3. The decomposition subgroups that detect the difference

For both displayed pairs the restrictions are isomorphic on every cyclic subgroup, because equality of characters on all powers gives the cycle type of a generator. For the \(V_4\) pair the only noncyclic subgroup is the full group, which distinguishes the pair.

For \(D_8\) the two proper noncyclic subgroups are \(K_V=\langle r^2,s\rangle\) and \(K_E=\langle r^2,rs\rangle\). On \(K_V\) the vertex four-point orbit breaks into two two-point orbits whose point stabilizers are the two reflection lines in \(K_V\). The additional quadratic of character \(1+\lambda_E\) has the remaining order-two subgroup as stabilizer. The first restricted set therefore has pattern \(2+2+2\). The edge four-point orbit restricts to a regular \(K_V\)-set because its stabilizers are all outside \(K_V\), and the quadratic of character \(1+\lambda_V\) becomes two fixed points. This gives pattern \(4+1+1\) for the other set. The same argument with the roles reversed applies to \(K_E\). The full group distinguishes the pair already. Thus a subgroup fails to distinguish the pair exactly when it is cyclic.

It follows that for any minimal counterexample over a number field, its common splitting extension has one of the two displayed group actions and is locally cyclic at every place. Conversely such a locally cyclic extension with these actions gives a counterexample. For any extension field \(F\) of the ground number field in a common overfield with the Galois splitting field \(\Omega\), the tensors are isomorphic over \(F\) precisely if \(\operatorname{Gal}(\Omega/(\Omega\cap F))\) is cyclic. Indeed it identifies with the image of the absolute Galois group of \(F\) on \(\Omega\), by restriction from the finite Galois compositum \(\Omega F/F\).

## 4. Both patterns over the rationals

For \(V_4\), let \(\Omega=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{13},\sqrt{17})\). The algebra pair is
\[
A=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{13})\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{17})\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{221}),
\qquad B=\Omega\times\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q.
\]
This is the known Klein four Gassmann construction with specific radicands ensuring local cyclicity. At 13 the other radicand 17 is a square, since \(17\equiv 4\bmod 13\). At 17 the radicand 13 is a square, since \(8^2\equiv13\bmod17\). At 2, 17 is a square, and at the real place both radicands are positive. At every other prime the compositum is unramified, with cyclic decomposition group. Thus the two multiplication tensors are isomorphic over every completion and nonisomorphic over \(\mathbb Q\). They become isomorphic over an extension \(F/\mathbb Q\) exactly when it contains at least one of the three quadratic subfields of \(\Omega\).

For \(D_8\), let \(\alpha^4=17\) with \(\alpha>0\), and let \(\Omega=\mathbb Q(\alpha,i)\). The real quartic has degree four by Eisenstein and adjoining \(i\) doubles it. Choose \(r(\alpha)=i\alpha,\ r(i)=i\), and let \(s\) be complex conjugation. These give the dihedral group. The quartics fixed by the reflection subgroups \(\langle s\rangle,\langle rs\rangle\) are generated by \(\alpha\) and \(\beta=(1+i)\alpha\), respectively. Indeed \(rs(\beta)=\beta\), and \(\beta^4=-68\) is also Eisenstein at 17. The kernels of \(\lambda_V,\lambda_E\) fix \(\alpha^2\) and \(i\alpha^2\), respectively. The corresponding algebra pair is
\[
A'=\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]{17})\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-17}),
\qquad
B'=\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]{-68})\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{17}).
\]
At 17, \(i\) lies in \(\mathbb Q_{17}\) and the splitting field is a cyclic quartic extension obtained by adjoining \(\alpha\). At 2, 17 has a fourth root in \(\mathbb Q_2\). To see this, substituting \(1+4u\) reduces the equation to \(u+6u^2+16u^3+16u^4=1\), which has a simple root modulo two. Hence the splitting field at 2 is contained in \(\mathbb Q_2(i)\), and its decomposition group is cyclic. At every other finite prime the polynomial discriminant of \(x^4-17\) is a unit, so the splitting field is unramified. At infinity the decomposition group has order two. This proves that these tensors, too, are everywhere locally isomorphic and globally nonisomorphic. The maximal cyclic subgroups are \(\langle r\rangle\) and the four reflection subgroups. Thus over an extension \(F/\mathbb Q\) this pair is isomorphic exactly when \(F\) contains \(\mathbb Q(i)\) or one of the four reflection-fixed quartic fields in \(\Omega\). In particular its quadratic isomorphism field is unique, in contrast to the Klein four pair.

## 5. Integral representatives and the modulus test

The multiplication tensors can be written directly in power bases of the displayed field factors and by concatenating the product bases, with cross-factor products zero. For integral representatives preserving comparison at every modulus, choose a \(\mathbb Z\)-basis of the maximal order of each étale algebra. An isomorphism over \(\mathbb Q_p\) identifies the local maximal orders because it preserves the subrings of elements integral over \(\mathbb Z_p\). Therefore these integral tables are isomorphic over every \(\mathbb Z_p\). They give a simultaneous witness over the product of the \(\mathbb Z_p\), and reduce to ordinary tensor witnesses over \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\) for every \(m\ge1\). Their rational multiplication tensors remain nonisomorphic.

For the Klein four example one explicit integral basis uses the quadratic generators \(x=(1+\sqrt{13})/2\), \(y=(1+\sqrt{17})/2\), \(z=(1+\sqrt{221})/2\), satisfying \(x^2=x+3,\ y^2=y+4,\ z^2=z+55\). The biquadratic maximal order has basis \(1,x,y,xy\) because the two quadratic discriminants 13 and 17 are coprime. Equivalently, this tensor-product order is maximal at 13 because the second factor splits there, at 17 because the first splits there, and at all other primes because both are unramified étale. The two extra factors of the maximal order of \(B\) are scalar copies of \(\mathbb Z\).

The dihedral quartics also have explicit maximal-order bases:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\mathbb Q(\alpha):&1,\ \alpha,\ (1+\alpha^2)/2,\ (1+\alpha)(1+\alpha^2)/4,\\
\mathbb Q(\beta):&1,\ \beta,\ w,\ \beta w/2,\qquad
                     w=(\beta^2+2\beta+2)/4 .
\end{array}
\]
At every odd prime the displayed entries are integral because their denominators are powers of two. At \(2\), choose the root \(a\in1+4\mathbb Z_2\) of \(a^4=17\). The roots \(\pm a\) of the first quartic give divisibility of \((1+\alpha)(1+\alpha^2)\) by \(4\), and at the roots \(\pm ia\) the factor \(1+\alpha^2=1-a^2\) is divisible by \(8\). This verifies integrality of the first basis at \(2\). For the second quartic take \(\beta=(1+i)a\). Then \(w=(1+a)/2+i(a^2+a)/2\equiv1+i\bmod 2\mathbb Z_2[i]\). At \(\beta=-(1+i)a\), both coefficients of \(w=(1-a)/2+i(a^2-a)/2\) are even. Complex conjugation checks the other two roots. Hence \(w\) is divisible by \(1+i\) in each local factor, and \(\beta w/2\) is integral as well.

At \(2\) the first quartic algebra is \(\mathbb Q_2^2\times\mathbb Q_2(i)\), of maximal-order discriminant valuation \(2\). The second is \(\mathbb Q_2(i)^2\), of valuation \(4\), since its roots have \(2\)-adic valuation \(1/2\) and all lie in \(\mathbb Q_2(i)\). The power-basis discriminants from \(x^4-17\) and \(x^4+68\) have \(2\)-adic valuations \(8\) and \(14\), respectively. Thus the required indices of the power orders are \(8\) and \(32\) at \(2\), exactly the determinants of the displayed basis enlargements. At \(17\) the two quartic polynomials are Eisenstein and the extensions are totally and tamely ramified, so their discriminant valuations are \(3\), already those of the power bases. All remaining primes are absent from the polynomial discriminants. This proves maximality of the two bases. Together with the standard quadratic bases this supplies explicit integral tables for the dihedral pair. An exact arithmetic check verified all 16 products in each displayed quartic basis and obtained discriminants \(-19652\) and \(78608\).

All assertions here concern ordinary three-factor isomorphism on the geometrically diagonal class. In particular side six is sharp in that class. The proof gives no smaller-side assertion for other geometric tensor orbits. This is a written proof.
