# Low-space isomorphism of Fitting-free groups

Let \(G,H\) be finite groups given by valid Cayley tables, with \(G\) Fitting-free and \(H\) arbitrary. Fitting-free means that the solvable radical is trivial. Write \(N=\max(2,|G|,|H|)\), let \(m_S\) be the multiplicity of the simple type \(S\) in \(\operatorname{Soc}(G)\), and set
\[
P(G)=\prod_S m_S!.
\]
The empty product is one.

1. Isomorphism decision, output of an isomorphism when one exists, and exact counting of isomorphisms take deterministic \(O(\log N+\log P(G))\) workspace and \(P(G)N^{O(1)}\) time. In particular all Fitting-free Cayley tables admit an \(O(\log N\log\log(N+2))\)-space algorithm, and the three tasks take logarithmic space when \(P(G)\le N^c\) for fixed \(c\). A sufficient promise is \(m_S\le |S|^c\) for every \(S\).
2. Independently, when \(G/\operatorname{Soc}(G)\) can be generated by a fixed number \(d\) of elements, all three tasks take deterministic logarithmic space even for unrestricted multiplicities.

The bounds concern the standard space model with read-only input and write-only output. Both are scoped results on the deterministic logspace question of Grochow, Johnson and Levet. [Problem 10.1 and Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19777#S10)

## 1. Cayley-table access and the socle

For any logspace-decidable generating set \(D\) in a table group \(X\), membership in \(\langle D\rangle\) is undirected reachability from the identity. An edge is right multiplication by an element of \(D\) or its inverse. Test the adjacency between two labels by table lookup and the given membership predicate, allowing a scan of \(D\) if needed. Reingold's theorem applies to this virtual graph because it has at most \(|X|\) vertices and its adjacency entries are computable in logarithmic space. The same works for product groups and subgroups with logspace-decidable membership by using paired labels or filtering the ambient domain. Identity, inverse, multiplication, enumeration and comparisons of table labels have logspace implementations by scanning. The applications below use a fixed number of nested logspace subroutines. [Reingold, undirected connectivity in log-space](https://doi.org/10.1145/1391289.1391291)

The normal closure \(C_X(x)\) of one element \(x\) has a logspace membership predicate by using its conjugates as generators. Every nontrivial minimal normal subgroup \(M\) is \(C_X(x)\) for each nonidentity \(x\in M\). Conversely a nontrivial \(C_X(x)\) is minimal precisely when \(C_X(y)=C_X(x)\) for every nonidentity \(y\in C_X(x)\). Test equality by a scan of all labels. Use the least nonidentity label in a minimal normal subgroup as its unique name. The test for Fitting-freeness rejects exactly when one such subgroup is abelian. A nontrivial solvable normal subgroup contains a minimal nontrivial normal subgroup of the ambient group, and a solvable minimal normal subgroup is abelian by its characteristic derived series. The converse follows immediately. Reject unequal orders or an \(H\) that fails this test.

The socle \(S_X=\operatorname{Soc}(X)\) is generated by the union of the minimal normal subgroups, so it also has a logspace membership predicate. For a Fitting-free group it is a direct product of nonabelian simple groups. Distinct minimal normals of \(X\) intersect trivially and commute, and each is a direct product of nonabelian simple groups by the structure of finite minimal normal subgroups. Independence from a product of other minimal normals follows from their trivial centers: an intersection with that product lies in the center of the remaining minimal normal subgroup. The individual simple factors are exactly the minimal normals of \(S_X\) as a group in its own right, which can be listed by the same algorithm on the virtual subgroup. These have disjoint nonidentity elements. In particular one can find the unique factor containing any given nonidentity factor element. Conjugation by \(X\) permutes these factors. The centralizer of \(S_X\) in \(X\) is trivial since its intersection with \(S_X\) is their trivial center and any nontrivial normal subgroup of \(X\) contains a minimal normal subgroup of \(X\). The trivial group is included, with socle 1 and no factors.

For each factor \(A\le S_X\), select the lexicographically first ordered generating pair \(a_1,a_2\) by scanning pairs and using generated-subgroup membership. Nonabelian finite simple groups are two-generated. To check and evaluate a proposed isomorphism \(\psi:A\to B\) between two factors, form the subgroup of \(A\times B\) generated by \((a_1,b_1),(a_2,b_2)\) for the proposed image pair \(b_1,b_2\in B\). Membership is logarithmic-space reachability. The subgroup must project bijectively to both factors, tested by checking for exactly one partner for every element in each factor. It is then the graph of the unique desired isomorphism, and its graph allows evaluation and inverse evaluation by a scan. Enumerating all image pairs decides factor isomorphism in logarithmic space. [Finite simple-group two-generation, historical account and references](https://seis.bristol.ac.uk/~tb13602/docs/BGH_spread_final.pdf)

For later use write \(\iota_X:X\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(S_X)\) for the faithful conjugation embedding above. For an isomorphism \(\phi:S_G\to S_H\), one has an extension \(G\to H\) precisely when
\[
\phi\,\iota_G(G)\,\phi^{-1}=\iota_H(H).
\tag{1}
\]
Necessity follows by restricting an isomorphism. For sufficiency, conjugate the embedded groups by \(\phi\) and use the two inverse embeddings onto their images. This gives a unique extension whose restriction to \(S_G\) is \(\phi\), since \(\iota_X\) restricts on \(S_X\) to its inner-automorphism map. This use of the socle embedding is standard in Fitting-free isomorphism algorithms. [Grochow–Johnson–Levet, §7](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19777#S7)

## 2. Enumerating simple-factor permutations in small space

Match multiplicities of simple types in the two socles using the factor isomorphism test in Section 1. To order types consistently between groups, use the least named factor of each type in \(G\) to name the type, then list its copies on each side in table-label order. All operations use scans and counters, without storing the lists. The product of factorials can be computed and stored in \(O(\log N+\log P(G))\) space. Enumerate the type-preserving factor bijections by a single integer \(0\le u<P(G)\), with one mixed-radix block of radix \(m_S!\) per type. For a type with multiplicity \(m\), use its usual factorial digits to specify successive insertions of \(j=1,\ldots,m\) at positions \(p_j\in\{1,\ldots,j\}\). To compute the image of factor index \(i\), start at \(p_i\), and for \(j>i\) increase the position by one exactly when \(p_j\) is at or before it. The required digits are available by recomputing products, division and remainders in the allowed space. This computes one factor match at a time without storing a permutation array.

Let \(\sigma\) be a factor bijection. Every tuple of images for the first generators of the source factors is represented by one element of \(S_H\), and the same holds for the second generators. Namely, for \(v_1,v_2\in S_H\), use their respective components in the target factor \(\sigma(A)\) as the two images for \(A\). Conversely every assignment of image pairs to all factors comes from unique \(v_1,v_2\). The component of \(v\in S_H\) in a factor \(B\) is computed by scanning \(b\in B\) for equality of conjugation by \(b\) and by \(v\) on the chosen two generators of \(B\). The other components commute with \(B\), so the sought \(b\) exists and is unique by centerlessness. Enumerate \(v_1,v_2\) by two labels in \(H\). If every local image pair defines an isomorphism, the factors assemble to one \(\phi:S_G\to S_H\). Each possible \(\phi\) is enumerated once by its \((u,v_1,v_2)\).

To test (1), scan \(g\in G\) and look for \(h\in H\) with
\[
\phi(gag^{-1})=h\phi(a)h^{-1}
\]
for both chosen generators \(a\) of every simple factor. To evaluate the left side, find the factor containing \(gag^{-1}\) and evaluate its local isomorphism by Section 1. The identities test each pair of automorphisms on a generating set of \(S_G\), since the union of the factor pairs generates the product. The resulting inclusion of embedded groups is equality by the equal orders. The unique \(h\) for each \(g\) is the value of the corresponding extension. Output a witness by keeping the first successful triple and scanning \(g\) and \(h\) again. Count successful triples to count all isomorphisms. The counter fits since their total is at most \(P(G)|S_H|^2\). The work per triple is polynomial in the table sizes, proving the stated bounds.

There are at most \(\log_2 N\) factors, so \(\log P(G)\le O(\log N\log\log(N+2))\). If \(m_S\le |S|^c\) then
\[
P(G)\le\prod_S m_S^{m_S}\le\prod_S |S|^{c m_S}=|S_G|^c.
\]
This permits unbounded multiplicities of growing simple types and arbitrary conjugation permutations of the factors within each type.


## 3. Bounded generation of the socle quotient

For fixed \(d\), find one ordered tuple \(g_1,\ldots,g_d\) such that \(G=\langle S_G,g_1,\ldots,g_d\rangle\) by the reachability test. Pad with identities if needed. On the stated promise such a tuple exists. Fix it and enumerate all tuples \(h_1,\ldots,h_d\) in \(H\). For one such tuple we want the socle isomorphisms satisfying the named equations
\[
\phi\circ\iota_G(g_i)=\iota_H(h_i)\circ\phi\qquad(1\le i\le d).
\tag{2}
\]
Each solution satisfies (1), because it sends the conjugation image of \(S_G\) to that of \(S_H\) and sends each of its remaining generators into \(\iota_H(H)\). Inclusion is equality by equal orders. Conversely the restriction of a group isomorphism satisfies (2) for precisely one tuple, its values on the \(g_i\). This uniqueness also follows from the faithful conjugation embedding.

Compute the orbits on the simple factors under the named automorphisms on each side. On the factor names join each \(A\) to its conjugate by the corresponding element and its inverse. The conjugate factor is found by conjugating a nonidentity element of \(A\) and testing factor membership. These orbit membership queries are again undirected reachability. Use the least factor name to index each orbit. Call an orbit product the direct product of the factors in one such orbit, equipped with the ordered list of the \(d\) specified conjugation automorphisms restricted to it. A solution of (2) must map each orbit product to an isomorphic orbit product, intertwining all \(d\) automorphisms in order. Conversely any such bijective matching of orbit products, with an intertwiner on each, assembles to a solution of (2).

Here is a logarithmic-space test of equivariant isomorphism between two orbit products, whether they are on the same or opposite sides. Denote their underlying ambient groups by \(X,Y\), their lists of acting elements by \(a_1,\dots,a_d\) and \(b_1,\dots,b_d\), and the orbits by \(\mathcal O,\mathcal O'\). Fix the first factor \(A\) in \(\mathcal O\), enumerate a possible image factor \(B\) in \(\mathcal O'\), and enumerate a local isomorphism \(\psi:A\to B\) by the two-generator test in Section 1. Let \(U\) and \(V\) be the unions of nonidentity elements of the individual factors in \(\mathcal O\) and \(\mathcal O'\), respectively. On \(U\times V\) take edges for simultaneous conjugation by \((a_i,b_i)\) and its inverse. Define a relation \(R\subseteq U\times V\) as the set reachable from the pairs \((x,\psi(x))\) for nonidentity \(x\in A\). Test membership by scanning these starting pairs and using Reingold's algorithm. Test that \(R\) is the graph of a bijection by scanning for a unique partner for each \(u\in U\) and each \(v\in V\).

If an equivariant isomorphism extending \(\psi\) exists, its restriction to \(U\) contains \(R\). It equals \(R\) because each source factor is reachable from \(A\), and therefore its restriction to \(U\) is forced. Conversely suppose the bijection test passes. For any factor in \(\mathcal O\) choose a word of named generators and inverses that sends \(A\) to that factor. Conjugating \(\psi\) by the matching word transports its entire graph to \(R\), giving a full local isomorphism onto one target factor. The graph test makes this independent of the word. These target factors are distinct because inverse partners are unique, and they exhaust \(\mathcal O'\) by surjectivity of \(R\). Thus \(R\) determines a product isomorphism, and its invariance under the synchronized edges implies that this isomorphism is equivariant for each named automorphism. This proves necessity, sufficiency and uniqueness for each candidate \((B,\psi)\). In particular one can decide, evaluate and count the orbit-product intertwiners in logarithmic space. The local count has polynomial magnitude because there are at most \(N\) choices of \(B\) and at most \(N^2\) image pairs.

Equivariant isomorphism of orbit products is an equivalence relation on the disjoint union of the two lists of orbit products with their specified automorphisms. For each class, named by its least orbit name under a side-tagged order, count the number of its members on either side. This requires only counters and calls to the preceding test. A matching exists precisely when the counts agree for every class. Match equally ranked members within each class and choose the first successful \((B,\psi)\) for each matched pair. These choices can be recomputed to evaluate the resulting \(\phi\) on any factor element as its unique partner in \(R\). One can then stream the corresponding group isomorphism by using the scan from Section 2 to evaluate its value on each group element. This works in the trivial-socle case as well, where the Fitting-free groups are trivial.

For the number of solutions at a given tuple, a class having \(m\) members on each side contributes \(m!\,a^m\), where \(a\) is the order of the equivariant automorphism group of one orbit product in that class. Indeed each bijection of the orbit copies is allowed, and each ordered source-target pair in that bijection has \(a\) intertwiners by composition with any one isomorphism. Compute \(a\) by the local count with the same orbit on both sides. Multiply over classes and sum over the tested tuples \(h_1,\ldots,h_d\), giving zero to any tuple with mismatched counts. Every isomorphism contributes once by uniqueness in (2). All factors \(a\) and the multiplicities \(m\) have \(O(\log N)\) bits. Use one factor \(a\) per orbit member and factors \(1,\ldots,m\) for \(m!\). Iterated multiplication and iterated binary addition have constant-depth uniform threshold circuits, whose output bits can be evaluated one at a time in logarithmic space from this virtual input. Equivalently, a threshold gate can scan its predecessors with a counter, and the recursion depth is constant. Hence the exact output count fits the claimed workspace even if its bit length exceeds \(O(\log N)\). [Hesse–Allender–Barrington, uniform arithmetic](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-0000(02)00025-9)

There is no bound on the orbit lengths or on the multiplicities in this argument. For example it covers \(S^m\rtimes C_m\) with \(C_m\) cyclically permuting the \(m\) factors of a fixed nonabelian simple group \(S\) for every \(m\). The socle is \(S^m\), because this subgroup is a minimal normal with trivial centralizer, and any other minimal normal would centralize it. The quotient is cyclic while \(P(G)=m!\).

## 4. Restricted epimorphisms in logarithmic space

There is also a consequence for epimorphisms. Suppose both groups are Fitting-free, the source \(G/S_G\) is solvable, and the target \(H\) satisfies either of the logarithmic-space hypotheses in the theorem. Then epimorphism decision, search and exact count take deterministic logarithmic space.

In fact for any epimorphism \(\pi:G\twoheadrightarrow H\), the image \(\pi(S_G)\) is a direct product of the surviving nonabelian simple factors. Its conjugation orbits on these factors split it into minimal normals of \(H\), so \(\pi(S_G)\le S_H\). Meanwhile \(H/\pi(S_G)\) is solvable as a quotient of \(G/S_G\). Every nonabelian minimal normal of \(H\) is perfect, hence has trivial image in that solvable quotient. Thus \(S_H=\pi(S_G)\). If \(A\) is the product of the surviving factors in \(S_G\), the restriction \(\pi|_A\) is injective and its image is \(S_H\). For a group element \(g\), \(\pi(g)\) centralizes \(S_H\) exactly when \(g\) centralizes \(A\), since \(A\) is normal and \(\pi|_A\) is injective. The target socle has trivial centralizer, so \(\ker\pi=C_G(A)\).

Enumerate all subsets of the simple factors invariant under conjugation by \(G\). This uses a bit mask of at most \(\log_2 N\) bits and factor membership tests. For each such subset form the centralizer of the associated product by testing commutation with the factor generator pairs, yielding a normal subgroup with logspace membership. The candidate kernels are distinct, since the intersection of \(C_G(A)\) with \(S_G\) consists precisely of the unselected factors. Compute each quotient virtually by taking the least label in each coset and reindexing via counts. Check if it is isomorphic to \(H\), applying the theorem with \(H\) as the promised input and the quotient as arbitrary. Reversing a streamed isomorphism uses a scan for a unique preimage. Compose with the quotient projection to construct the epimorphism. Each successful kernel contributes \(|\operatorname{Aut}(H)|\), so the exact total is the number of successes times the automorphism count, with the same arithmetic workspace. Earlier polynomial and parallel epimorphism results allowed unrestricted sources and Fitting-free targets. Here the additional source and target hypotheses give logarithmic space. [Earlier Fitting-free epimorphism theorem](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/GrochowOpenProblemSolutions/CAYLEY-TABLE-ALGORITHMS.md#gp049-epimorphisms-to-a-fitting-free-target)

These are written proofs using explicit Cayley-table access. The socle embedding and the use of local simple factor isomorphisms are established methods. The space bounds rely on coding all local generator images by two socle elements, and, independently, on synchronized orbit intertwiners and exact matching of their equivalence classes.
