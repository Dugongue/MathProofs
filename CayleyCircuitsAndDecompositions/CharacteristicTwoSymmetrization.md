# Deterministic matrix-tuple symmetrization in characteristic two

**Theorem.** Over any field $F$ of characteristic two, there is a deterministic algorithm using polynomially many field operations and equality tests that, given a square matrix tuple $(B_i)_{i=1}^m$, finds invertible $A,D$ with all $AB_iD$ symmetric, or reports that none exist. The same statement holds with alternating in place of symmetric, where alternating additionally requires zero diagonal. For explicitly represented finite fields $\mathbb F_{2^s}$ the bit time is polynomial in $n,m,s$.

This answers the symmetrization part of the characteristic-two question in Ivanyos and Qiao, §1.4, extending their Theorem 8. Their question also asks for a characteristic-two algorithm for tuple isometry. Symmetrization here concerns a single input tuple and independent left and right changes. [Ivanyos and Qiao, §1.4 and §4](https://arxiv.org/html/1708.03495#S1.SS4)

## 1. The computable rank objective

Write $L(B)$ for the vector space of matrices $Z$ such that all $ZB_i$ are symmetric. When testing alternating symmetrization, use the subspace additionally given by the diagonal equations. Since $AB_iD$ has the required type exactly when $D^{-t}AB_i$ has it, an invertible element of $L(B)$ is the required output. The equations for $L(B)$ are linear.

We first reduce to tuples with zero common kernel and whose images span the full output space. The input dimensions of the domain quotient by the common kernel and of the sum of the images must be equal, since this is true of symmetric matrices. If they differ, reject. Otherwise choose independent bases on the left and right so that the tuple has these active rows and columns in its leading square block and zeros elsewhere. The common kernel and image sum are intrinsic under left-right changes. In any symmetric tuple they are orthogonal complements. A left-right change to a symmetric (respectively alternating) tuple therefore induces a left-right change of the active blocks to symmetric (respectively alternating) matrices after a congruence separating the common kernel. Conversely, a change on the active blocks extends by identity on the padded blocks. The all-zero active dimension requires no search.

For the active tuple form $A_0=\{R: \exists S,\ RB_i^t=B_i^tS\text{ for all }i\}$, the algebra of the left blocks of endomorphisms of the transpose tuple. Compute the algebra and products by linear algebra. Write $L_{\mathrm{sym}}(B^t)$ for the ordinary symmetric left-multiplier space for the transpose tuple. For $Z\in L(B)$, form the right ideal
$$
I(Z)=\operatorname{span}\{Z W a:W\in L_{\mathrm{sym}}(B^t),\ a\in A_0\}\subseteq A_0 .
$$
Indeed $ZW B_i^t=ZB_i W^t=B_i^t Z^tW^t$, so $ZW\in A_0$ for all inputs.

Suppose for the correctness argument that the input is a yes-instance, and write $B_i=E C_i$ with $E$ invertible and $C_i$ all of the required type. The left endomorphism algebra of $B^t$ is then the same as that of $C$, since $B_i^t=C_i E^t$. On this algebra the involution sends $R$ to $S^t$ when $R C_i=C_i S$. The witness $S$ is unique by the zero common kernel. Transposition proves that this is an involutive anti-automorphism. Its fixed space is $L_{\mathrm{sym}}(C)$, and
$$
 L(B)E=L(C),\qquad L_{\mathrm{sym}}(B^t)=E L_{\mathrm{sym}}(C),\qquad I(Z)=(ZE) A_0 .
$$
The last equality holds because $L_{\mathrm{sym}}(C)\subseteq A_0$ and contains $1$. The space $L(C)$ contains $1$ and every $x+x^*$. This holds in the alternating case too, because $(x+x^*)C_i=xC_i+(xC_i)^t$ has zero diagonal.

**Projective rank without computing the radical.** For a right ideal $I$ of a finite-dimensional $F$-algebra $A$, compute the dimension of a right ideal $eA$ for an idempotent $e\in I$ such that $eA$ and $I$ have the same image in $A/\operatorname{rad} A$. Start with $e=0$ and compute a basis of $(1-e)I$. If every basis element is nilpotent, the latter ideal is nilpotent. To justify the test, a finite-dimensional algebra that is nonnilpotent stays nonnilpotent over an algebraic closure. In its unitization it is an ideal with nonzero image modulo the radical, since that radical is nilpotent. Projection onto a simple factor of the semisimple quotient gives a full matrix algebra as a quotient of the original nonunital algebra over the closure. The images of its basis span that quotient and cannot all be nilpotent, since nilpotent matrices have trace zero. A nilpotent right ideal maps to zero in the semisimple quotient, where each nonzero right ideal contains a nonzero idempotent. Thus when the test stops, $I\subseteq eA+\operatorname{rad} A$. Otherwise a nonnilpotent basis element yields a nonzero idempotent $f\in(1-e)I$, by the polynomial projection onto its nonzero primary part from its minimal polynomial. The polynomial can be chosen with zero constant term. We have $ef=0$. Now $f(1-e)$ is an idempotent orthogonal to $e$ on both sides and is nonzero, since $f=fe$ would imply $f^2=0$. Replace $e$ by $e+f(1-e)$. Its generated right ideal strictly grows. There are at most $\dim A$ iterations, each using linear algebra and univariate polynomial gcd. This is a constructive proof of the projective-ideal method of Ivanyos and Qiao, §4.2. [Prior method](https://arxiv.org/html/1708.03495#S4.SS2)

Let $\rho(Z)$ be the resulting dimension for $I(Z)$. It depends only on the isomorphism type of the image of $I(Z)$ in $A_0/\operatorname{rad}A_0$. Indeed $eA_0$ is projective with that image as its semisimple head, and the head determines the multiplicities of the indecomposable projective summands. Consequently, in a yes-instance $\rho$ strictly increases whenever the ranks of $ZE$ on the matrix factors of the semisimple quotient all weakly increase and at least one strictly increases.

## 2. A rank increase using three basis directions

Let $a$ be the image of the current $ZE$ in the semisimple quotient, and let $V$ be the image of $L(C)$. In a yes-instance, if $Z$ is singular then $a$ is a nonunit. Work temporarily over an algebraic closure and take the geometric semisimple quotient there. The involution survives, and $V$ still contains the identity and the images of all $x+x^*$. These conclusions follow because the radical is involution-invariant and because $x\mapsto x+x^*$ commutes with scalar extension and with passage to a quotient. Each simple factor of the original semisimple quotient has at least one geometric matrix factor above it. Ranks over the original division ring and the ranks after passing to any such geometric factor are proportional with a positive factor. Indeed over the original simple algebra present the element as a matrix over its division ring and use invertible row and column operations to bring it to a diagonal block with identities and zeros. After passage to a geometric factor these operations stay invertible, and the division-ring identity blocks have equal positive matrix sizes. This works also for inseparable centers. Rank monotonicity and a strict increase can thus be checked on the geometric factors.

On a geometric factor exchanged with another by the involution, $x+x^*$ can have arbitrary projection to that first factor. If $a$ is deficient there, some minor of size one larger than its rank has a nonzero linear coefficient along a suitable direction.

On a fixed factor $M_s$, the involution is the adjoint of an invertible symmetric form $H$. Indeed composition with transpose is inner, and involutivity gives $H^t=\lambda H$ with $\lambda^2=1$, hence $\lambda=1$ in characteristic two. Multiplying the projections of $a$ and $V$ by $H$ turns them into symmetric matrices. The resulting space contains every alternating matrix because $H(x+x^*)=Hx+(Hx)^t$. It also contains $H$.

Let $M=Ha$ on that factor and choose a basis so $M=\mathrm{diag}(M_0,0)$ with $M_0$ invertible and the zero block on the radical. The allowed directions still include all alternating matrices and the transformed nonsingular symmetric form $H$. If the radical has dimension at least two, place an alternating cross entry inside it. The minor on the old rank block plus one extra row and a different extra column has a nonzero linear coefficient in the perturbation.

For a one-dimensional radical, write the allowed direction in blocks as $\begin{psmallmatrix}B_0&v\\v^t&b\end{psmallmatrix}$. The determinant of the perturbed matrix is
$$
\det(M_0+tB_0)\bigl(tb-t^2v^t(M_0+tB_0)^{-1}v\bigr).
$$
If $H$ is nonzero on the diagonal of the radical line, use $H$ to make the linear coefficient nonzero. Otherwise the following two cases suffice.

- If $M_0$ is nonalternating, so is $M_0^{-1}$ by congruence. An alternating direction with $B_0=0$ and suitable $v$ makes the quadratic coefficient nonzero.
- If $M_0$ is alternating, it has even size, so the whole matrix size $s$ is odd. The nonsingular $H$ is therefore nonalternating, and its block $H_0$ off the radical line is nonalternating since its radical-line diagonal is zero. Use $H$ plus an alternating direction that sets $v$ freely, leaving $B_0=H_0$. The quadratic coefficient vanishes since $M_0^{-1}$ is alternating. The cubic coefficient, up to the nonzero factor $\det M_0$ and a sign, is $v^t M_0^{-1}H_0 M_0^{-1}v$. It is nonzero for a suitable $v$, since the middle form is nonalternating and the outer map is invertible.

This proves that some improved minor has a nonzero coefficient of degree at most three in the perturbation parameter. If the perturbation is expressed in a basis of $L(B)$, followed by the map $Z\mapsto ZE$ and the displayed changes of basis, this coefficient is a homogeneous polynomial of that degree in the direction coefficients. A nonzero monomial uses at most three basis indices. Restricting to those indices preserves a nonzero minor polynomial. On every other geometric factor choose a minor certifying its old rank. Their product with the improved minor is a nonzero polynomial on the chosen at most three coefficients, since each old-rank minor has nonzero constant term. Its total degree is at most $\dim A_0$, as the sum of geometric matrix sizes is at most that dimension.

Given a set of more than $\dim A_0$ distinct field scalars, a grid on the chosen at most three coordinates cannot make that product vanish everywhere. This is the elementary polynomial interpolation bound, valid also for polynomials with coefficients in an extension field. The search tries all supports of size at most three and every coefficient tuple on that grid, testing $\rho$ until it increases. It needs no hidden $E$, decomposition, radical, or extension coordinates. Start at $Z=0$. An invertible $Z$ certifies yes. If the search cannot improve $\rho$, a yes-instance was impossible by the argument above. There are at most $\dim A_0$ improvements.

## 3. The finite-field procedure and descent

Here is an alternative algorithm over an explicitly represented finite field $k$ of characteristic two, including $k=\mathbb F_2$. Regard $B$ as a representation $M=(U,V,(B_i:U\to V)_i)$ of the two-vertex directed quiver. Its dual $M^\vee$ interchanges vertices and transposes the arrows. A morphism $(a,b):M\to M^\vee$ satisfies $bB_i=B_i^t a$. Its dual has components $(b^t,a^t)$. A symmetric isomorphism is thus an isomorphism with $a=b^t$. For alternating symmetrization add the linear conditions that each $bB_i$ has zero diagonal. This is exactly the desired witness.

Decompose $M$ into indecomposable representations. Over finite fields, constructive module decomposition and module isomorphism take deterministic polynomial time in fixed characteristic. The tuple can be represented as one module by using the matrices $\begin{psmallmatrix}I&0\\0&0\end{psmallmatrix}$ of a vertex projection and the off-diagonal arrow maps on $U\oplus V$. A module summand then gives the two vertex summands, and a module intertwiner is exactly a quiver morphism. [Chistov–Ivanyos–Karpinski, module algorithms](https://doi.org/10.1145/258726.258751)

We use the following test on the multiplicities of isomorphism types.

1. A pair of distinct dual types $P,P^\vee$ must have equal multiplicities. This follows from $M\simeq M^\vee$ and Krull–Schmidt. Conversely use a hyperbolic isomorphism on each paired $P\oplus P^\vee$: its diagonal blocks are zero and its off-diagonal blocks are the double-dual identification and its transpose. It meets the alternating conditions as well.
2. On a self-dual indecomposable $P$, compute its space of symmetric or alternating morphisms $P\to P^\vee$ by linear algebra. Check each basis vector for invertibility. This is exact, since composing with any isomorphism $P^\vee\to P$ identifies the full Hom space with the local ring $\operatorname{End}(P)$. The nonunits are its radical, a linear subspace. If an invertible allowed morphism exists, use its direct sums.
3. Otherwise this type must have even multiplicity, and even multiplicity suffices via hyperbolic pairs using any isomorphism $P\simeq P^\vee$. For necessity, denote the residue field of $\operatorname{End}(P)$ by $K$. Choose one isomorphism $P\to P^\vee$, using the same identification on every copy. The resulting residue Hom space is one-dimensional over $K$. Dual transpose preserves the subspace of noninvertible morphisms of this Hom space. For every morphism $h$, the sum $h+h^\vee$ is allowed even in the alternating problem. It therefore has zero residue. Hence dual transpose acts as the identity on the residue Hom space. In any allowed self-dual form on copies of $P$, the diagonal entries have zero residue and off-diagonal entries match under transposition. The residue matrix is alternating over the field $K$, so its size must be even if it is invertible.

For the block arguments, take $\operatorname{End}(M\oplus M^\vee)$ modulo its radical. By Krull–Schmidt, this quotient is the product of matrix rings over the residue division rings of the distinct indecomposable types. Each division ring here is a finite field. An isomorphism between $M$ and $M^\vee$ has invertible maps between the respective blocks in the quotient. This justifies the diagonal and off-diagonal tests even when morphisms between different types are nonzero. The specified witnesses reconstruct an isomorphism on the raw tuple by using its decomposition bases.

We also need the fact that a tuple over a finite field $k$ can be symmetrized over an extension $F$ only if it can be symmetrized over $k$. For $E=\operatorname{End}(M\oplus M^\vee)$ its semisimple quotient is a product of matrix rings over finite separable fields. After extending scalars to $F$ this quotient is still semisimple, and the old radical extends to a nilpotent ideal. Thus the new radical is exactly the extension of the old radical. Distinct old factors never merge, even if a residue field splits. For distinct dual types an unequal pair of multiplicities still gives an unequal pair in the extended quotient, as the corresponding idempotents are still exchanged by dual transpose. For a self-dual type that failed the one-copy test, scalar extension of the linear equations preserves the zero residue of every allowed diagonal block, and scalar extension preserves the identity action of dual transpose on its residue Hom space. Over every field factor of the extended residue field the matrices are still alternating of the original size. An odd multiplicity still obstructs invertibility. Therefore every obstruction on the finite field persists over $F$.

## 4. Uniform field access and cost

For a general characteristic-two field given by field operations and equality, start with $0,1$ and the input coefficients. Close this set under addition and multiplication until it reaches $\dim A_0+1$ elements, or until it stabilizes with fewer elements. Stop adding as soon as the threshold is reached. Comparing and multiplying all pairs in a set of polynomial size, with polynomially many successive additions to the set, costs polynomially many operations. If the threshold is reached use those scalars in the three-direction search. Otherwise the stabilized finite subring of a field is a finite field. Its entire arithmetic table, including inverses, can be read by bounded scans. To obtain a polynomial-basis representation if needed, find a generator of its cyclic multiplicative group by trying the field elements. The powers of that generator give the usual basis over $\mathbb F_2$, and its minimal polynomial can be recovered by enumerating linear combinations in the field of polynomial cardinality. Apply the finite-field procedure there and use the descent statement to justify a negative answer over the ambient field. Basis changes for degenerate inputs can all be carried out in the field generated by the input entries. In the zero-dimensional case return the identity after undoing those basis changes.

Every intermediate linear algebra problem uses polynomial dimensions, every grid has constant dimension at most three, and the finite fields reached by exhaustive closure have polynomial cardinality. For an explicitly represented finite field, arithmetic is also polynomial in its representation length. The algorithm outputs actual left and right changes of basis over the original field. This is an informal proof.
