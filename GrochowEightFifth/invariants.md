# Characteristic-free field generation and a nonabelian degree gap

Let $K[V]$ be the polynomial coordinate ring of a finite-dimensional representation $V$ of a finite group $G$ over $K$. Write $\beta_{\mathrm{field}}$ for the least degree in which polynomial invariants generate the rational invariant field, $\beta_{\mathrm{sep}}$ for the least degree in which they separate orbits over an algebraically closed field or over $\mathbb R$, and $D_{\mathrm{span}}$ for the least degree in which the polynomials span $K(V)$ over $K(V)^G$. All proofs below are informal. [Degree questions and definitions](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.05626v6#S5) · [Blum-Smith and Derksen, *Generic orbits, normal bases, and generation degree for fields of rational invariants*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aim.2026.111042).

## The sharp spanning-degree bound holds in modular characteristic

For every finite group over every field, one has
$$
\boxed{\ \beta_{\mathrm{field}}(G,V)\le 2D_{\mathrm{span}}(G,V)+1.\ }
$$
The restriction on the characteristic in the published Theorem 1.1 can be dropped. Equality occurs in the modular case for each odd characteristic. [Blum-Smith and Derksen, Theorem 1.1 and §3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05650v4#S1).

### Trace matrices recover the fixed field

We use a field observation. Suppose $L/F$ is a finite separable extension of degree $n$, $L=K(u_1,\ldots,u_r)$, and $K\subseteq F$. Fix an $F$-basis $b_1,\ldots,b_n$ of $L$. Let $M\subseteq F$ be the subfield generated over $K$ by the entries of the matrices
$$
B_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}_{L/F}(b_i b_j),
\qquad
B^{(k)}_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}_{L/F}(b_i u_k b_j).
$$
The trace pairing is nondegenerate because $L/F$ is separable. In the basis $b_i$, the matrix of multiplication by $u_k$ is $B^{-1}B^{(k)}$, since the $i,j$ entry of $B$ times that multiplication matrix is $\operatorname{Tr}_{L/F}(b_i u_kb_j)$. All these matrices have entries in $M$.

The multiplication representation $L\to\operatorname{Mat}_n(F)$ is unital and injective. It therefore maps the algebra $M[u_1,\ldots,u_r]$ injectively into $\operatorname{Mat}_n(M)$. This algebra is an integral domain of finite dimension as an $M$-vector space, hence is a field. It contains $K$ and all $u_k$, so it is $L$. The resulting unital action of $L$ on $M^n$ makes $M^n$ an $L$-vector space. Consequently $[L:M]\le n=[L:F]$, and $M\subseteq F$ gives $M=F$.

For a representation take $L=K(V)$ and $F=L^G$. The effective image of $G$ is the Galois group of $L/F$. Use the coordinate functions for $u_k$ and choose the $b_i$ among polynomials of degree at most $D_{\mathrm{span}}$. For a polynomial the trace is its sum over the effective group, so traces are polynomial invariants and preserve the upper degree bound. The entries of $B$ have degree at most $2D_{\mathrm{span}}$, and those of $B^{(k)}$ have degree at most $2D_{\mathrm{span}}+1$. The observation therefore proves the boxed bound. The trace pairing argument is valid in the modular case, where the trace of $1$ can vanish.

### Sharpness in each odd modular characteristic

Let $\operatorname{char}K=p$ for an odd prime $p$. Consider the three-dimensional indecomposable representation of $C_p$ written on coordinates as
$$
x\longmapsto x,\qquad
y\longmapsto y+x,\qquad
z\longmapsto z+y+x/2.
$$
For group parameter $a\in\mathbb F_p$ the corresponding transforms are $x$, $y+ax$, and $z+ay+a^2x/2$. The Galois evaluation map for $L=K(x,y,z)$ and $F=L^{C_p}$ identifies $L\otimes_F L$ with the product of $p$ copies of $L$, with coordinates given by the $p$ embeddings of the second factor. More explicitly the target is $\prod_{a\in\mathbb F_p} L$, and $u\otimes v$ maps to $(u\,a(v))_a$. Thus the $F$-rank of a set of polynomials equals the $L$-rank of its orbit evaluation vectors. At the generic point, the evaluations of $1,x,y,z$ span $1,a,a^2$ over $L$. Products of at most $d$ such evaluations span the evaluations of polynomials in $a$ of degree at most $2d$. Conversely degree at most $d$ can produce only those evaluations. The rank is $\min(p,2d+1)$ by the Vandermonde matrix on $\mathbb F_p$. This gives $D_{\mathrm{span}}=(p-1)/2$.

Put $q=2xz-y^2$ and $t=y/x$ after inverting $x$. The action fixes $x,q$ and takes $t$ to $t+1$. Its invariant algebra after this localization is $K[x,x^{-1},q,t^p-t]$. Indeed division by the monic invariant $t^p-t$ reduces to a translation-invariant remainder of degree below $p$, which must be constant. The invariant field is generated by $x,q,N$, where $N=y^p-x^{p-1}y$ has degree $p$.

A polynomial invariant of degree $d<p$, expressed using $y=xt$ and $z=(q+x^2t^2)/(2x)$, has $t$-degree at most $2d<2p$. It is therefore $A(x,q)+B(x,q)(t^p-t)$ with Laurent coefficients in $x$. Its odd part under the substitution $y\mapsto-y$ is the polynomial $B(x,q)N/x^p$. Reducing $q$ modulo $x$ gives $-y^2$, so nonzero polynomials in $q$ remain nonzero after reduction. The $x$-valuation of a nonzero Laurent coefficient $B$ is therefore its least $x$-exponent in its expansion with coefficients in $K[q]$. Since $N$ is prime to $x$, polynomiality forces $B/x^p\in K[x,q]$ whenever $B\ne0$. The resulting odd part would have degree at least $p$. Hence all polynomial invariants of degree below $p$ lie in $K[x,x^{-1},q]$. They cannot generate the field of transcendence degree three. We obtain $\beta_{\mathrm{field}}=p=2D_{\mathrm{span}}+1$. The field generators here agree with the known analysis of modular cyclic indecomposables. [Reimers and Sezer, *Generic separation for modular invariants*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.laa.2026.06.018).

## Question 5.8. An unbounded gap on faithful absolutely irreducible nonabelian modules

For each odd prime $q$, take $G=\mathbb F_q\rtimes\mathbb F_q^\times$, acting on the augmentation representation $V$ of its natural permutation representation on $\mathbb F_q$. Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic different from $q$, this is an absolutely irreducible faithful representation of dimension $q-1$, and
$$
\beta_{\mathrm{field}}(G,V)\le9,\qquad
\beta_{\mathrm{sep}}(G,V)\ge q.
$$
The same bounds hold for the real augmentation representation. This gives an unbounded ratio on faithful irreducible nonabelian representations in characteristic zero. It also does so in characteristic two, where all these groups are modular. [Question 5.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.05626v6#S5.SS2).

### Proof

Work first over a field containing a primitive $q$-th root. There are coordinate variables $x_a$ indexed by the nonzero residues $a\in\mathbb F_q$, chosen so that the translation subgroup $C_q$ acts on $x_a$ with weight $a$ and $H=\mathbb F_q^\times$ permutes these weight lines by multipliers. The weight lines are distinct and one-dimensional. Every nonzero subrepresentation is a sum of weight lines under the translation group and must contain all lines by transitivity of the multipliers. This proves absolute irreducibility. Nontrivial translations act nontrivially, and a nonidentity multiplier permutes the weight lines nontrivially, which proves faithfulness.

The field $F=K(x_a)^{C_q}$ is generated by its invariant monomials of degrees two and three. Indeed, in the free abelian group on the weights, the positive zero-sum pairs impose $e_{-a}=-e_a$. The triples impose $e_{a+b}=e_a+e_b$ whenever $a,b,a+b$ are nonzero. Taking $e_0=0$ extends this to every pair of residues. Thus the quotient by these relations is the weight group $\mathbb F_q$, which proves generation of the invariant exponent lattice by the short relations. Ratios of their monomials then generate $F$.

Among the invariant cubics take $s_a=x_a^2x_{-2a}$ for $a\ne0$. Their exponent matrix is $2I+P$ for a permutation matrix $P$, and its integer determinant is nonzero because the complex eigenvalues of $P$ have absolute value one. Thus these monomials are algebraically independent over any base field. Here algebraic independence follows from independence of integer exponents, so it still holds if the determinant vanishes as a scalar in the field. The multipliers act regularly on the $s_a$. The evaluation matrix $(h(s_a))_{h,a}$ is generically invertible: as a polynomial in independent symbols $s_a$, its determinant becomes a nonzero permutation determinant upon setting $s_1=1$ and all other $s_a=0$. The Galois evaluation map for $F/F^H$ now shows that the $s_a$ are an $F^H$-basis of $F$. The action of $H$ on $F$ is faithful because it permutes the independent $s_a$ regularly.

Use the trace observation for $F/F^H$, with the $s_a$ as the basis and the quadratic and cubic zero-sum monomials as the field generators over $K$. The necessary traces are polynomials in the original variables of degrees at most nine. They are fixed by both the translation subgroup and the multipliers. The trace observation therefore shows that they generate $F^H=K(V)^G$.

Over an algebraically closed field a primitive-weight line forces $\beta_{\mathrm{sep}}\ge q$, since every invariant of lower degree is constant when restricted to that line and a finite group has finite orbits. Over $\mathbb R$, a conjugate pair of opposite weights gives a real two-dimensional rotation plane. Invariants of degree below $q$ restrict to polynomials in the squared radius, since after complexification only monomials with matching exponents on the opposite weights can occur. These polynomials cannot distinguish all finite orbits on a circle. The bound on $\beta_{\mathrm{field}}$ descends to the reals by the base-change invariance of the field generation degree. [Blum-Smith, Garcia, Hidalgo and Rodriguez, Lemma 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.05626v6#S2.SS1).


## Question 5.11. Computing the spanning degree by orbit evaluation

Let $G$ act effectively by linear transformations and let $n=|G|$, $r=\dim V$. There is a rank computation of $D_{\mathrm{span}}$ that uses the orbit of one generic point and retains at most $n$ evaluation columns at each stage. Over $\mathbb Q$ or a finite field, when the group elements and their actions are supplied explicitly, it gives a randomized polynomial-time computation in the size of this explicit input and the logarithm of the inverse failure tolerance. The same construction works in modular characteristic. This answers the computation of the parameter in Question 5.11 for the explicit-group encoding. [Question 5.11](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.05626v6#S5.SS2) · [Blum-Smith and Derksen, §2 on the generic orbit](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05650v4#S2.SS2).

For a point $v$ let $c_j(v)\in K^n$ have entry $(gv)_j$ indexed by $g\in G$. For the generic point $x$ work over $L=K(V)$ and start with $U_0=\operatorname{span}_L(\mathbf 1)$. Recursively compute
$$
U_{d+1}=U_d+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\operatorname{diag}(c_j(x))U_d.
$$
Coordinatewise multiplication proves by induction that $U_d$ is the space spanned by all degree-at-most-$d$ orbit evaluation vectors. The Galois evaluation map gives
$$
D_{\mathrm{span}}=\min\{d\mid\dim_L U_d=n\}.
$$
The nontrivial group elements act nontrivially, so the $n$ generic orbit points are distinct. Over $L$ one can choose a linear form taking distinct values at those points. The Vandermonde matrix on its $n$ values then proves that full rank is reached by degree $n-1$. At every step retain a basis, so the recurrence never performs a rank computation with more than $(r+1)n$ columns or more than $n$ rows.

For the numerical algorithm, sample the $r$ coordinates of $v$ independently and uniformly from a finite set $S$ of scalars in a sufficiently large field, and run the same recurrence using $c_j(v)$. Work over a finite extension if the base finite field is too small. All specialized ranks are at most generic ranks. At $d=D_{\mathrm{span}}$, choose $n$ witnessing evaluation columns of monomials of degree at most $d$. Their determinant is a nonzero polynomial of total degree at most $nd\le n(n-1)$. The probability that it vanishes at $v$ is at most $n(n-1)/|S|$ by the polynomial zero bound. On the complementary event the first degree reaching rank $n$ is the true answer. If a run reaches that rank at any degree, the degree it reports is a certified upper bound. If necessary, repeat with independent samples and take the smallest successful value. To implement the rank recursion with controlled coefficient sizes, store selected evaluated monomial columns themselves as bases and use elimination only to select an independent subset. Their degrees stay at most $n-1$, so for rational input they have bit lengths polynomial in the explicit matrix input and the sample bit length. Extension-field evaluation similarly has arithmetic cost polynomial in the extension degree. This establishes the stated polynomial bound for the explicit encoding.

## A sharp composite-order refinement of Proposition 4.4

Let $C_n$ have a faithful nonmodular representation with exactly two distinct nontrivial and noninverse weights. If $n$ is odd, then
$$
\beta_{\mathrm{field}}\le(n+3)/2.
$$
If $n$ is even, then $\beta_{\mathrm{field}}\le n/2+1$, with exactly one exception up to scaling and weight interchange. The exception is the class of $\{1,n/2-1\}$ for $4\mid n$ and $n\ge8$, whose degree is $n/2+2$. For odd $n\ge5$ the bound is attained by $\{1,2\}$. For even $n\ge4$ the bound outside the exception is attained by $\{1,n/2\}$. The counterexample in the exceptional class was established previously. Here the new statement is the universal sharp bound and the exclusion of all other exceptions. [Original Proposition 4.4 and the two-weight identity in Proposition 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.05626v6#S4.SS1).

### Proof

The two-weight identity $\beta_{\mathrm{field}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{field}}$ reduces upper bounds to finding two linearly independent nonnegative exponent vectors. If neither weight is primitive, each axis has a pure invariant whose degree is the order of the corresponding weight, at most $n/2$. The two axes suffice. Otherwise scale a primitive weight to $1$, and write the other as its representative $b\in[2,n-2]$. The exponent lattice is given by $x+by=0\bmod n$ and has index $n$.

If $b>n/2$, put $x_0=n-b\ge2$, $j=\lceil n/x_0\rceil$. The independent invariant vectors $(x_0,1)$ and $(jx_0-n,j)$ have degrees at most $\max(x_0+1,x_0+(n-1)/x_0)$, since $jx_0-n\le x_0-1$ and $j\le (n+x_0-1)/x_0$. For odd $n$, the convex function $x_0+(n-1)/x_0$ has the value $(n+3)/2$ at both endpoints of $[2,(n-1)/2]$. For even $n$, $x_0\le n/2-1$. The endpoints of $[2,n/2-1]$ give values $(n+3)/2$ and $n/2+1+1/(n/2-1)$, both smaller than $n/2+2$. Thus integer degrees are at most $n/2+1$ in the even case.

Suppose next that $b\le n/2$ and put $n=qb+r$, $0\le r<b$. If $q\ge3$, $(r,q)$ and $(r+b,q-1)$ give an invariant basis with maximum degree $q+b+r-1$. The inequality $(q+1)b\ge n+1$ bounds this above by
$$
q+\frac{2(n+1)}{q+1}-2.
$$
This is convex in $q$. The lower endpoint $q=3$ gives $(n+3)/2$. At the upper endpoint $q=(n-1)/2$ for odd $n$ it has the same value, and at $q=n/2$ for even $n$ its value is strictly below $n/2+2$. This proves the desired bounds in this case.

For $q=2$, first suppose $b$ is a nonunit. If $r>0$, use $(r,2)$ and the pure $y$-axis invariant of degree at most $n/2$. Here $r+2\le(n+4)/3$. If $r=0$, then $b=n/2$ and the vector $(b,1)$ works together with $(0,2)$. Finally suppose $b$ is a unit. Interchanging weights and scaling replaces $b$ by $b^{-1}$. Unless its quotient $q'$ is also two, one of the earlier cases applies. When $q=q'=2$, write $r'=n-2b^{-1}$ with the inverse represented in $[1,n-1]$. The two invariant vectors $(r,2)$ and $(2,r')$ have degrees at most $(n+4)/3$. They are independent unless $rr'=4$. Since both remainders have the parity of $n$, the only possible dependence is $r=r'=2$ at even $n$. Then $b=b^{-1}=n/2-1$, whose invertibility is equivalent to $4\mid n$. The distinct-weight condition excludes $n=4$. This is precisely the exceptional pair, for which the basis $(2,2),(n/2+1,1)$ and the parity lower bound give degree $n/2+2$.

For odd $n$ and weights $\{1,2\}$, a relation strictly below degree $(n+3)/2$ satisfies $x+2y=n$ if it is nonzero, since its sum $x+2y$ is below $2n$. Its degree is $n-y$, so below the bound one must have the unique value $y=(n-1)/2$, giving rank one. For $n$ even and weights $\{1,n/2\}$, every relation below degree $n/2+1$ has $x=0$: for odd $y$ a nonnegative $x$ must be at least $n/2$, and for even $y$ a positive $x$ must be at least $n$. The two-row upper bound from the axis and $(n/2,1)$ is then sharp. This completes the refinement.
