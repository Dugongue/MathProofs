# Undirected squared matching counts have linear counting width in odd characteristic

Fix an odd prime `p`. There are simple bipartite graphs of equal order `n` that Duplicator cannot distinguish with `Ω_p(n)` pebbles and that have different squares of their perfect-matching counts modulo `p`. This answers the undirected counting-width question in Section 7.3. It implies the permanent lower bound on the smaller input domain of symmetric zero-one matrices. [Dawar–Wilsenach, Symmetric Arithmetic Circuits, Sections 7.2, 7.3 and 8](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v021a014/v021a014.pdf)

Start with a connected simple cubic bipartite expander `Γ` on `v` vertices. Such graphs form a sequence with sizes doubling and edge-expansion bounded below. A perfect matching of `Γ` exists by regularity and Hall's theorem, and its complement is a spanning cycle factor `F`. The graph is 2-connected. Its edges decompose into three perfect matchings, so each edge belongs to a cycle, and a cut vertex of degree three would imply a bridge. [Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava, Interlacing Families I, Theorem 5.5](https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/spielman/PAPERS/Marcus_Spielman_SrivastavaIFI.pdf)

Use the two Dawar–Wilsenach CFI graphs `X_0,X_1`. For each base edge `e` there are two bit vertices `e_0,e_1`. Each base vertex `w` has one balance vertex and four inner vertices indexed by its incident bit triples of the prescribed parity. All parities are even in `X_0`, and one parity is odd in `X_1`. Each inner vertex is adjacent to its balance vertex and the three corresponding bit vertices. These graphs are bipartite, with inner vertices on one side.

## A weighted count with nonzero sum and difference

Fix a direction on each cycle of `F`. In both graphs give weight `t_e` to the incidences from either bit vertex of `e∈F` to the inner fiber at its chosen head. Give weight one to all other incidences. Let `U_b(t)` denote the weighted perfect-matching count. The projection of a matching records how many of its two bit vertices are matched at each endpoint of each base edge. All matchings of a given projection have the same weight. Section 7.2, Lemma 7.5, matches the counts of every nonuniform projection between the two graphs. Every uniform matching has weight `prod_{e∈F} t_e`. By their unweighted gap formula,

```text
U_0(t) − U_1(t) = ± 2^(3v/2+1) prod_{e∈F} t_e.
```

In particular this difference is nonzero modulo `p` at every sign assignment.

In `U_0+U_1`, reduce the powers `t_e^2` to one. The constant term of the resulting multilinear polynomial counts matchings nonuniform on every factor edge. The balance vertex takes one of the four inner vertices at each base vertex. Hence its two incident factor edges must contribute two bit vertices in total. The possibilities of zero or four are excluded since the third edge can contribute at most two and there are only three remaining inner vertices. Thus each vertex takes both bits from exactly one factor edge and neither bit from the other. The choices consistently orient each factor cycle, with two possibilities per cycle. Each complementary matching edge splits its bits, with two independent choices.

For a fixed cycle orientation and choice of split bits, the local count is exactly four at every vertex. Projection of the four allowed parity triples onto the doubled edge and the singleton edge gives all four pairs. The singleton bit has two possible inner partners, one in each of the doubled edge's two groups. After choosing it, the doubled edge's bit in that group has one choice and its other bit has two. The balance vertex takes the last partner. Thus the constant coefficient is `2^(1+c+v/2) 4^v`, where `c` counts the cycles and the extra factor of two accounts for both CFI graphs. It is nonzero modulo `p`. This coefficient is the average of the polynomial on all sign assignments since `p` is odd. Some sign assignment therefore makes `U_0+U_1` nonzero, and this assignment also has nonzero difference. Their squared values differ.

## Recovering ordinary unweighted simple graphs

Replace a positive edge by one length-three path and a negative edge by `p−1` internally disjoint length-three paths. Give each path new internal vertices. The new graphs are simple and bipartite. If an original edge is unused, all its internal vertices have a unique matching using their internal pairs. If an endpoint takes an edge along a path, its interior vertex's other possible partner is forced to take the other endpoint on that same path. Thus using the edge contributes exactly the number of paths, and leaving it unused contributes one. The new unweighted matching counts reduce modulo `p` to `U_b` on the selected signs. The two new graphs have the same number of vertices because every base incidence has the same multiplicity on both sides and each local fiber has four inner labels. They have `O(pv)` vertices each.

## Counting indistinguishability

The usual CFI edge-flip strategy extends to the path subdivisions as follows. Keep corresponding original balance, inner, and bit fibers. Each new fiber is labeled by an incidence (including a balance incidence), a path number, and one of the two internal positions on that path. Its four vertices are indexed by the four corresponding inner parity triples. The path number range is the same in the two graphs.

Let `k=floor(c_0 v)` for a sufficiently small fixed positive `c_0`. A pebble on any balance or inner fiber, or on a path fiber rooted at base vertex `w`, blocks `w`. A pebble on an edge-bit fiber blocks both endpoints of its base edge. Deleting the at most `2k` blocked vertices leaves a unique component with more than `v/2` vertices. Otherwise a union of components of size between `v/4` and `v/2` would have all its boundary edges in the blocker set, contradicting uniform edge expansion and the degree-three boundary cap `6k`. Such a union can be made by accumulating components smaller than `v/4` if no single component is already in the requisite interval. Large components are nested as the blocker set grows because any two subsets of more than half the vertices intersect.

Maintain a global vector of edge flips with the correct local parity change away from one defect in that large component. The zero vector initially puts the defect at the twisted vertex. Map pebbled bit labels by their corresponding global flips. Map pebbled inner and path-fiber labels by the triple of incident flips at their base vertex. Every pebbled fiber lies outside the defect. These maps preserve all adjacencies of pebbled vertices. An internal path edge checks a role and equality of local triples. An incidence to an original bit vertex checks the corresponding coordinate of the triple, shifted by the same global flip in both fibers. Balance incidences and matches to the original inner vertex are preserved by the fiber labels.

After a pebble is removed, for every possible new fiber choose a new defect in the large component after adding its blocker. Move the old defect there along a path in the current large component by toggling the flip vector on path edges. This changes no remaining pebble image. Use the modified vector to define the bijection on that particular new fiber. The maps made independently for each possible new fiber combine into a single global bijection. Whichever vertex Spoiler chooses, its fiber's modified vector witnesses the next position. Thus Duplicator wins with `k` pebbles. This argument uses the usual CFI defect move and records the extra path fibers that preserve it. [Dawar–Richerby, The Power of Counting Logics on Restricted Classes of Finite Structures](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~ad260/papers/counting-treewidth.pdf)

For every sufficiently large even target order `n`, choose a doubling-sequence base size `v=Ω_p(n)` such that the resulting order is at most `n`. Pad with disjoint edges to order `n`. This preserves the matching count and the same pebble lower bound. Thus the squared matching parameter has linear counting width when measured on graphs with at most `n` vertices, or on exactly `n` vertices for even `n`. The permanent of either undirected bipartite adjacency matrix is the square of its matching count, by its two off-diagonal blocks. The published support-and-counting transfer then gives the square-symmetric circuit lower bound even for symmetric zero-one inputs. The proof is informal.

# The determinant symmetry boundary above simultaneous alternating permutations

In characteristic zero, for every `n≥5`, consider the determinant-preserving permutations of the `n²` matrix entry labels. Among subgroups containing the simultaneous action `ΔA_n`, every subgroup is either contained in the simultaneous symmetric action together with transpose or contains independent alternating row and column actions. The first group range has the polynomial-size determinant circuit of Dawar–Wilsenach. The second has their exponential orbit-size lower bound. This settles the symmetry boundary within the stated overgroup range. [Dawar–Wilsenach, Symmetric Arithmetic Circuits, Theorem 4.1 and Section 8](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v021a014/v021a014.pdf) [Dawar–Wilsenach, Lower Bounds for Symmetric Circuits for the Determinant](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.10986)

Write `D_n={(a,b)∈S_n×S_n : sgn(a)=sgn(b)}`. The full entry-permutation group of the determinant is `D_n ⋊ <τ>`, where `τ` is transpose and swaps the two coordinates of a pair. This is the entry-label symmetry group described by Dawar–Wilsenach. Let `J=H∩(A_n×A_n)`, normal in any subgroup `H` under consideration.

If `J` strictly contains `ΔA_n`, take `(a,b)∈J` with `a≠b`. Multiplying by `(b^(-1),b^(-1))` yields a nonidentity element of the first factor. Its conjugates by `ΔA_n` generate that whole factor by the simplicity of `A_n`. Together with `ΔA_n` this yields `A_n×A_n` in `H`.

If `J=ΔA_n`, then every element of `H` normalizes `ΔA_n`. A pair `(a,b)` does so precisely when the conjugations by `a` and `b` agree on `A_n`. Its ratio is then in the centralizer of `A_n` in `S_n`. The centralizer is trivial, since an element commuting with all three-cycles fixes every three-element set setwise, and these sets separate the points. Transpose centralizes `ΔA_n` and so leaves the same test. Hence `H` lies in `ΔS_n × <τ>`.

There are exactly ten possible overgroups before taking conjugacy classes. In the easy range they are the inverse images of the five subgroups of the Klein four quotient `(ΔS_n × <τ>)/ΔA_n`. In the hard range they are the inverse images of the five subgroups of `(D_n ⋊ <τ>)/(A_n×A_n)`, another Klein four group, with quotient coordinates sign and transpose. This classification is of subgroups containing `ΔA_n`. The proof is informal.

# A depth-three reduction preserving matrix symmetry

Over a characteristic-zero field, a polynomial of degree `d` in an `n × n` matrix that has a reduced matrix-symmetric circuit of support size at most `k`, with `k≥2` and `n≥2k+1`, has a matrix-symmetric circuit of depth at most three and orbit size `n^O(sqrt(kd))`. Constant polynomials use a constant gate. If the input is presented as a sum of `M` bounded-treewidth homomorphism polynomials, the construction uses `M n^O(sqrt(kd)) poly(d)` gates and wires. This answers the depth-three branch of the question in Section 1.3. The previous depth-four construction does not give this three-layer form. [Dawar–Dwivedi–Pago–Seppelt, Depth Hierarchies for Symmetric Algebraic Circuits, Section 1.3 and Theorem 3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.23541#S1.SS3)

## Separators and homogeneous factors

By their Theorem 3.5 the polynomial is a linear combination of bipartition-respecting homomorphism polynomials of patterns of treewidth at most `k−1`. Their degrees are their edge counts with multiplicity. Discard degrees exceeding `d` by taking homogeneous components of the identity. Remove isolated vertices, absorbing their factors of `n` into the coefficient. Fix one remaining nonconstant pattern `F` with `e≤d` edges.

Set `B=ceil(sqrt(kd))`. Root a tree decomposition of `F` with at most `k` vertices in each bag. Assign each edge occurrence to the highest bag containing its endpoints. The bags containing both endpoints form a connected subtree, so the assignment exists and is unique. Give bags the resulting edge weights. From leaves to root accumulate each bag's own weight and the residual weights of its children, marking and resetting whenever the total exceeds `B`. Each mark consumes at least `B+1` distinct units, so there are at most `e/(B+1)` marks. Each residual tree component has weight at most `B`. Put in `S` all vertices of the marked bags, whence `|S|≤k e/(B+1)`.

Every vertex outside `S` occurs only within one residual tree component. An edge incident to it is assigned within that tree component. Thus each component of `F−S` has at most `B` edge occurrences incident with it, counting the edges to `S` as well. For a given assignment `a` of the separator labels, the corresponding product for `hom(F)` has one homogeneous factor per component. This factor sums the corresponding local monomials over all assignments of the component. It also has one degree-one factor for each internal edge of `S`. Group these factors into buckets with total degree at most `B` by closing a bucket whenever the next factor would overflow it. The sum of each pair of neighboring complete buckets then exceeds `B`, so the number `m` of buckets is at most `2e/B+1`. Each resulting factor `Q_j` is a sum of tagged monomials. Its bucket uses at most `2B` nonseparator vertices because every such vertex is incident to one of the bucket's at most `B` edges. Thus `Q_j` has at most `n^(2B)` monomial occurrences, and each tagged monomial's orbit is determined by at most `|S|+2B` host labels. Under permutations of rows and columns the separator assignments and the local label assignments are relabeled, while all bucket and edge tags are fixed.

## From these factors to three unweighted layers

For `r≥1`, use the subset-sum version of polarization

```text
prod_{i=1}^r u_i = (1/r!) sum_{J⊆[r]} (−1)^(r−|J|) (sum_{i∈J} u_i)^r.
```

It follows by expansion, because alternating over subsets cancels each term missing any index. Homogenize the bucket factors to degree `B` using a single extra invariant variable, which will finally be set to one. Apply the identity first to the product of the `m` bucket factors. The subset tags are fixed under permutations. For each resulting power of a sum of bucket factors, apply it again to the individual degree-`B` tagged monomials in the sum. This writes the inner sum as `1/B!` times a sum of signed `B`th powers of unweighted linear forms `ell_s`. The signs attach only to tags. Over an extension adjoining a primitive `2B`th root of unity, absorb the signs, giving a sum `Q=sum_s L_s^B`, with each `L_s=a_s ell_s` and `a_s` a root of unity. The factor `1/B!` contributes only an overall scalar. Every `ell_s` is an unweighted sum of variable occurrences, including the homogenizing variable. [Gupta–Kamath–Kayal–Saptharishi, Arithmetic Circuits: A Chasm at Depth 3](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/140957123)

For each nonnegative integer `z`, put `P_z(t)=prod_s (1−t^B L_s^B)^z`. The coefficient of `t^(Bm)` is a polynomial in `z` of degree at most `m`. By the binomial expansion its leading coefficient is `(−1)^m Q^m/m!`. In detail, to obtain this coefficient sum over nonnegative integers `r_s` adding to `m`. The leading coefficient of `binom(z,r_s)` is `1/r_s!`, so the multinomial formula gives the asserted power. Thus `Q^m` is a linear combination of coefficients of `P_z(t)` for `z=0,...,m`, each of which in turn is a linear combination of scalar evaluations in `t` by interpolation. These evaluations use only fixed scalar tags.

For a scalar `t` each factor `1−t^B L_s^B` splits as `prod_{ζ^B=1}(1−ζ t a_s ell_s)`. When `t≠0`, rewrite each affine factor as `(−ζ t a_s)(ell_s−1/(ζ t a_s))`. Put all scalar factors and the interpolation coefficients into the product gate as one constant. The bottom factors now use only unweighted sums of variables and a constant input, with parallel wires when necessary. At `t=0` the product is constant. Setting the homogenizing variable to one retains the three layers, namely sum, product, sum from top to bottom. The only variable labels in a bottom sum come from one bucket monomial. The middle product collectively includes all its tagged local assignments, so its permutation orbit is determined by its separator assignment.

## Base field, orbits, and size

For each fixed pattern descend from its cyclotomic extension using a primitive element `θ` of degree at most `2B` over the base field. Represent the circuit constants as polynomials of degree less than `2B` in `θ`; use a formal indeterminate `u` in its place. The resulting polynomial in `u` is interpreted under `u=θ`. A base-field-linear functional sending one to one is the identity on the intended base-field coefficients. Composing it with univariate evaluation at `θ` gives a linear functional on the bounded-degree polynomials in `u` used here. It is a linear combination of evaluations at base-field scalars by interpolation. Make one copy for each such evaluation and absorb its coefficient into the middle product constants. This preserves the three layers and the gate labels' equivariance. Each block uses a cyclotomic field for that pattern's `B`, so no joint extension across blocks is required.

All separator assignments, host local assignments, and coefficient calculations can be kept as tagged occurrences. Under the selected matrix action a middle gate is determined by its pattern and scalar tags and at most `|S|` host labels. A bottom gate is determined by at most `|S|+2B` host labels. The output and scalar tags are fixed. To control the full automorphism orbits, identify equal input labels and merge gates bottom-up whenever the operation label and multiset of already merged children agree. This is equivariant under the selected action, preserves depth and circuit values, and gives a reduced circuit. By Lemma 2.3 its extension of each input permutation is unique, so its full orbit is the image of a selected orbit. Therefore the orbit bound is `n^O(|S|+B)=n^O(sqrt(kd))`.

For size, there are at most `n^|S|` separator assignments and `2^m` initial polarization summands per assignment. An inner Waring list has at most `m n^(2B) 2^B` tagged linear forms. For each integer `z≤m`, the degree in `t` and the number of affine factors after splitting are bounded by `B m` times this list length. Descent interpolation uses at most a polynomial multiple in `B` of the same number of factors. Together with the bottom fan-in bound `B+1`, these factors give `n^O(|S|+B) 2^O(B+m) poly(d)` total size per supplied pattern. This is `n^O(sqrt(kd)) poly(d)` by the chosen `B`. The use of Theorem 3.5 alone may supply many pattern terms for an arbitrary circuit. Its universal guarantee here is in orbit size. The proof is informal.
