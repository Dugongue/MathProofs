# Linear constraints on ordinary tensor-isomorphism answers

## Theorem 1. Uniform closure under linear span programs

Fix any finite field \(F\) for the tensor entries. Let \(q_i=(T_i,U_i)\) be ordinary tensor-isomorphism queries. Let \(M\in(\mathbb Z/m)^{r\times N}\) and \(b\in(\mathbb Z/m)^r\) be given, and let \(m\ge2\) be written in unary (in particular, \(m\) can be fixed). One ordinary tensor-isomorphism instance over \(F\) tests whether
\[
 M z=b,\qquad z_i=0\ \text{whenever }q_i\text{ is a NO instance}.
\]
If \(q_i\) is a YES instance, any \(z_i\in\mathbb Z/m\) is permitted. The reduction is deterministic, uniform, and polynomial in all explicit inputs, including \(m\). In particular, the field of the span program may be \(\mathbb F_2\) while the tensor field is \(\mathbb F_3\). There is no rank bound.

More generally, a polynomial list of linear equations with variables in explicitly presented finite abelian groups, arbitrary specified linear maps between those groups, and some variables forced to zero when a given ordinary TI query fails, has the same reduction. Each group's elements and operations must have size polynomial in the explicit input. The cyclic case has an implementation with groups computed directly from \(m\).

### Two elementary encodings

**Objects.** Associate to a positive-format tensor \(T\) an associative unital triangular algebra \(A(T)\) with vertex idempotents indexed \(0,1,2\), off-diagonal radical corners \(X,Y,Z\) on \(01,12,02\), and only nonzero corner-to-corner radical product \(X\times Y\to Z\) equal to the bimap of \(T\). All products of three radical elements vanish. Its ordinary algebra isomorphism type is exactly the ordinary tensor type. An isomorphism induces a permutation of primitive quotient idempotents. Lifted idempotents of the same quotient classes can be aligned by conjugation by the unit \(\sum_i f_i e_i\), congruent to \(1\) modulo the radical. The directed corner graph is then invariant, and the arrow sequence \(0\to1\to2\) forces each index. The remaining conditions are exactly the bimap transporter equations, and every transporter extends blockwise. The center is \(F\): commuting with the vertex idempotents kills the off-diagonal corners, and the nonzero adjacent corners equate the three scalar entries. Hence the primitive central idempotents of any product of these objects are exactly its factor identities.

Formats with a zero mode or incompatible shapes can be replaced by fixed valid-format YES and NO pairs when decided on those grounds. This includes precisely the usual finite encoding conventions for empty arrays.

**Additive groups.** For a finite explicit abelian group \(D\), let \(E_D\) have basis \(e_x\) for \(x\in D\). Include its coordinatewise multiplication \(e_x e_y=\mathbf1_{x=y} e_x\), and linear operators \(e_x\mapsto e_{x+h}\) for a generating list \(h\) of \(D\). Isomorphisms of these marked structures are exactly shifts \(e_x\mapsto e_{x+a}\). Indeed the product forces a permutation, the operators force equivariance for all translations, and an equivariant permutation is determined by the image of \(e_0\). This works even when \(\operatorname{char}F\) divides \(|D|\).

### Switches and equations

For query \(q_i=(T_i,U_i)\) over cyclic \(D=\mathbb Z/m\), create the product of \(m\) algebras with \(A(T_i)\) in position 0 and \(A(U_i)\) in every other position. Attach \(E_D\) by the linear map to this product that sends \(e_x\) to the identity of its \(x\)-th factor. A system automorphism induces a shift by \(z_i\) on \(E_D\). If the query is false, a nonzero shift would carry \(A(T_i)\) to a nonisomorphic factor, so \(z_i=0\). If the query is true, every shift extends to the product by choosing an algebra isomorphism for each required factor map. Use a separate product and space for each query. The same switch works with \(D\) an arbitrary group from the abelian version and its unique distinguished position 0. Trivial groups require no switch. Variables without associated queries use the group encoding alone and allow every shift.

For a homomorphism \(h:D\to D'\), the fixed map \(e_x\mapsto e_{h(x)}\) between separate group spaces ties their shifts by \(a'=h(a)\). For three separate group spaces of the same type \(D\), the fixed bilinear map \((e_x,e_y)\mapsto e_{x+y}\) ties the output shift to the sum of the two input shifts. Repeated occurrences of a shift space can either use the same named space or be linked to a copy by the identity. For each linear equation combine its terms with an explicit addition chain using fresh group spaces. A marked vector on its endpoint is \(e_0\) in the source system and \(e_b\) in the target, where \(b\) is that equation's required right hand side. Empty sums use a fixed shift 0, marked identically at their start. All other tensors are the same in the two systems. It follows that their isomorphisms give exactly the solutions in the theorem on the marked shifts. Every shift solution extends, because the product-factor maps are independent except for the idempotent links, and all other conditions on group spaces were explicitly listed.

### Packing the system

All objects described above are an explicit tensor system with named spaces, tensors of order at most three, and shared or dual occurrences. Here is a size-independent way to apply the fixed-component theorem. Make a master copy of every named space plus one marked master line. For every tensor component create three private occurrence spaces, padding missing slots by copies of the line. Use a tag space with a coordinatewise product and an ordered shift (the shift sends the last tag to zero). Their joint stabilizer fixes all tags. On the direct sum of all master and occurrence spaces, use one tag-indexed projector tensor to enforce each direct summand; use one tag-indexed linear link tensor to identify every positive occurrence map with its master; use one tag-indexed bilinear scalar pairing to impose the inverse transpose on a dual occurrence; mark the master line vector. Finally sum the padded components into their private ordered occurrence triples. This gives seven tensors on two spaces, with sizes polynomial in the explicit original system. Restriction and extension of these seven-tensor isomorphisms preserve each original component and all shared maps. Convert the fixed seven-component system into ordinary three-tensor isomorphism by the fixed-component tensor-system theorem. [Chen, Grochow, Qiao, Tang and Zhang, Theorem 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.03135v2#S4).

## Theorem 2. Arbitrary-rank aggregation in the negative world

Let \(D_i\) be explicit finite abelian sign groups of exponents dividing a common \(m\). For each independent transporter problem \(i\) let \(C_i\subseteq D_i\) be its attainable character values. The image of a nonempty transporter coset under a character is a coset of a subgroup, and a failed transporter query gives the empty set. Let \(h_i:D_i\to D\) be group homomorphisms and pose
\[
 c_i\in C_i\quad\text{for every }i,\qquad \sum_i h_i(c_i)=b.
\]
Suppose each candidate test \(v\in C_i\) has a polynomial-time many-one compiler to ordinary TI, uniformly over the list. Then the whole constrained problem has a single deterministic ordinary-TI query, with no restriction on the rank of \(D\) when it is given as a list of explicit group coordinates. The arithmetic cost includes the explicit domain \(\sum_i |D_i|\) and \(m\). In the fixed ternary application \(D_i=(\mathbb Z/2)^3\) and arbitrary rank means ordinary binary matrices of input-variable dimensions; one does not enumerate the full target group.

For each \(v\in D_i\), give the candidate test its own switch \(z_{iv}\in\mathbb Z/m\). Enforce \(\sum_v z_{iv}=1\) in \(\mathbb Z/m\) for each \(i\), and \(\sum_{i,v} z_{iv} h_i(v)=b\). If \(C_i\) is empty the first equation is impossible. Otherwise write \(C_i=c+S\). With all unavailable switches zero, \(\sum_v z_{iv}v=c+\sum_v z_{iv}(v-c)\in C_i\). This identity is independent of the choice of integer lifts modulo \(m\). Conversely choose the switch of a single desired \(c_i\) to be \(1\) and the others zero. Theorem 1 implements all these equations together. For fixed ternary inputs this replaces both the logarithmic-rank restriction and the rigidity assumption in earlier independent-profile aggregation.

Write \(H(F)\) for the deterministic polynomial-time many-one reduction from fixed-field SL tensor isomorphism to ordinary TI over \(F\). For any fixed finite field of size greater than two, \(H(F)\) is equivalent to a single ordinary-TI constraint compiler for arbitrary lists of independent three-tensor determinant profiles and arbitrary affine equations on their sign coordinates. Choose a cyclic generator of \(F^\times\) as part of the fixed field. For \(H(F)\Rightarrow\) aggregation, compile each of the constant \((|F|-1)^3\) local candidate tests by \(H(F)\) after the appropriate diagonal reflection, and apply the theorem. For the converse take one comparison and prescribe all three determinants to be one. If only certain input subclasses have available candidate compilers, the unconditional aggregate theorem still applies to all of those subclasses, even when the full fixed-field reduction fails.

This theorem also applies to the relative norm profiles over explicit extensions of a fixed field wherever the earlier uniform norm implication supplies the local tests. It requires independence of the original transporters. Witnesses that must coincide in different input comparisons do not in general form the Cartesian product used in the proof.

## Other consequences with explicit decoders

1. Take mod-two incidence columns of a graph, one per edge, with any ordinary TI pair attached to that edge. After discarding false edges, the specified vertices \(s,t\) are connected exactly when their incidence sum is in the retained span. A path gives the sum. Conversely the number of odd vertices in each component of any selected edge set is even, so \(s,t\) must be in the same component. Connectivity and arbitrary conjunctions of requested same-component conditions therefore have ordinary many-one compilers. The same proof allows a polynomial explicit family of targets sharing the same availability bits by using separate switches for each target.
2. For a gain-labeled undirected graph with gains in a fixed finite group \(G\), form its explicit lifted undirected graph on \((v,g)\). Each edge of gain \(h\) links \((v,g)\) to \((w,gh)\). Attach its ordinary comparison to every lift. Reachability from \((s,1)\) to \((t,a)\) tests existence of a walk of gain \(a\) using true edges, even for nonabelian \(G\). The cost includes \(|G|\). This is distinct from solving nonabelian equations on independent shifts.
3. For any explicitly represented matrix over a fixed prime field, membership of any polynomial list of target columns in the span of selected columns is a single comparison. In particular, if an input supplies a basis of the full column span, a single comparison determines whether the selected columns still span it. Such ordinary tests compose with the usual balanced positive Boolean tests.
4. The switch works without assuming a yes/no oracle when the availability conditions are ordinary tensor isomorphism instances. By contrast, when the inputs are SL instances, an application that replaces scalar signs by switches still needs a compiler for each local availability condition. Thus closure under these linear decoders cannot by itself settle the unresolved atomic case.

All proofs in this chapter are written proofs. The finite audit script checks the switch and affine-coset mechanisms separately.

## Complementary query pairs and exact branching-program counts

A **complementary pair** for a bit is two explicit ordinary TI queries, exactly one of which is YES on the input domain. Ordinary TI is strong enough to read these bits via a single comparison that counts paths in a supplied polynomial-size directed acyclic branching program. Let the edge labels be tests of bits or their negations, with constants permitted. Count paths from a source \(s\) of indegree zero to a distinguished terminal \(t\), along edges whose labels are true.

For any modulus \(m\) as in Theorem 1, give each vertex an unrestricted variable \(u_v\), and each edge \(e\) two variables \(p_e,q_e\). Use the test for the edge's label to enable \(p_e\), and its complementary test to enable \(q_e\). Impose
\[
 u_s=1,\qquad u_{\mathrm{tail}(e)}=p_e+q_e,\qquad
 u_v=\sum_{e: \mathrm{head}(e)=v}p_e\quad(v\ne s).
\]
Exactly one channel is available, so \(p_e\) is forced to be \(u_{\mathrm{tail}(e)}\) on true edges and zero on false edges. Topological induction shows that \(u_t\) is the number of true-edge paths modulo \(m\). Fixing \(u_t\) to a requested residue is an additional linear equation. Integer edge weights can be included by multiplying the corresponding summands by their fixed residues. A graph with several terminal outputs or different required residues is handled by multiple linear constraints.

The exact unweighted path count fits in \(1+|E|\) bits when \(|E|\) counts edges with multiplicity; the edge set determines each path in an acyclic graph. Choose enough distinct polynomial-size primes so their product exceeds \(2^{|E|}+|b|\) for a supplied integer target \(b\), and for each prime test the residue of \(b\). Such a list is constructible deterministically in polynomial time by trial division and, for example, the elementary order-of-growth estimates for the number of primes. One can avoid prime estimates completely: use all moduli from \(2\) through \(M=2|E|+2\operatorname{bits}(|b|)+8\). Then \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,M)\) is divisible by \(\binom{M}{\lfloor M/2\rfloor}\), because each prime's valuation in any binomial coefficient is at most the highest power exponent of that prime below \(M\), by the floor formula for factorial valuations. This binomial coefficient is at least \(2^M/(M+1)\), which suffices. A balanced conjunction of residue tests therefore checks exact equality. A balanced disjunction over a residue mismatch checks exact non-equality, by enumerating each modulus's polynomially many residues. For polynomial-bit integer edge weights, the same argument uses a polynomial replacement for \(|E|\) bounding the count's bit length.

This yields a single ordinary comparison for postprocessing complementary query bits by deterministic logspace, by parity-logspace, or by modular-logspace computation at any fixed modulus. Use a time-unrolled configuration graph with polynomially many vertices, with transitions guarded by the queried input bits or their complements. Its path count or designated modulus gives the computation. If \(\overline{\mathrm{TI}}\le_m^P\mathrm{TI}\), ordinary queries provide both rails globally. Consequently the negative world together with that complement hypothesis excludes arbitrary polynomial nonadaptive lists of ordinary queries followed by uniformly constructible \(\oplus\mathrm L\) decoders or exact comparisons of integer path counts in explicitly presented branching programs. A deterministic logspace decoder is included. Without the complement hypothesis the theorem still applies when both rails can be constructed and their complementarity is proved for the inputs in use.

For a different consequence that needs no complementary pairs, attach a query to every edge of an explicit graph and use the all-ones incidence target over \(\mathbb F_2\). This tests whether the subgraph of available edges has a spanning subgraph of odd degree at every vertex, equivalently whether every connected component of the available-edge graph has even order. The resulting decoder includes the standard odd-factor function. It has linear-size monotone span programs yet superpolynomial monotone Boolean circuit size on the bipartite graphs in the cited lower bound. Hence the new closure genuinely goes beyond arbitrary polynomial-size monotone Boolean circuits used solely as decoders on the query bits. This is a statement about the abstract decoder, and places no such lower bound on general TI maps with access to tensor entries. [Babai, Gál and Wigderson, §§2.1–2.5](https://www.math.ias.edu/~avi/PUBLICATIONS/ABSTRACT/bgw99.pdf).

Finally, the coset hypothesis in Theorem 2 is essential to this exact construction. The nonaffine profile \(\{01,10,11\}\) permits odd selection of all three entries, yielding \(00\), although that value is absent. Arbitrarily prescribed local nonaffine relations therefore cannot be fed into this compiler by the same replacement.
