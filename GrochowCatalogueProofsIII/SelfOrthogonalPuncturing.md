# GP-0338 · Binary puncturing to a self-orthogonal code

**Theorem.** For a binary linear code, deciding whether at most $k$ coordinate punctures make the code Euclidean self-orthogonal is NP-complete and is equivalent under parameterized many-one reductions to binary $k$-VectorSum. The reduction from minimum-weight syndrome decoding multiplies its optimum by exactly $15$ on feasible syndrome instances. The puncturing optimization has no PTAS unless P = NP. The same hardness holds for allowing the monomial orbit, since a binary monomial is just a coordinate permutation, and for input allowing arbitrary fields, since binary codes are valid inputs.

If $M$ is a binary generator matrix with columns $v_j$, a puncturing set $D$ works if and only if
$$
\sum_{j\in D} v_jv_j^{\mathsf T}=\sum_j v_jv_j^{\mathsf T}.
$$
The right side is the full Gram matrix, and a row span is self-orthogonal exactly when its generator Gram is zero, even if the supplied rows are dependent. Reading only the upper triangular entries gives an immediate reverse reduction to binary $k$-VectorSum with unchanged parameter.

The forward reduction uses $15$ positions indexed by $z\in\mathbb F_2^4\setminus\{0\}$, here numbered by their binary values with the first bit least significant. On these positions introduce five private rows
$$
x_1,\ x_2,\ x_3,\ x_4,\ q=x_1x_2+x_3x_4,
$$
where $x_i(z)$ is the corresponding bit. Their entries and all their pairwise pointwise products have even weight. Indeed each nonconstant square-free monomial of degree at most $3$ has even sum over the nonzero points. The only apparently higher-degree private product is $q^2=q$ as a binary function. The row vectors and their pairwise products span the full $14$-dimensional space of even vectors. Their span contains the four linear monomials and all six quadratic monomials. The products $q x_i$, after subtracting quadratic terms, give precisely the four independent cubic monomials. These $14$ monomials are independent on the nonzero points because they also vanish at zero, and square-free monomials are independent as functions on the full binary cube. Consequently if deletions have zero Gram on these private entries, their indicator on the $15$ positions is constant.

Use three additional test patterns $h_1,h_2,h_3$ supported on $\{1,4,5\}$, $\{2,8,10\}$, and $\{1,5,6,8,10\}$, respectively. Each has even dot product with each private row, as can be checked by applying the indicated bit functions on these sets. Their weights are odd and their pairwise intersections have even cardinality. Thus $f=h_1+h_2$ and $g=h_2+h_3$ have even weight, are orthogonal to the private rows, and satisfy $f\cdot g=1$.

For a syndrome vector $a\in\mathbb F_2^m$, adjoin $m+1$ top rows to the block, with value $f(z)e_0+g(z)a$ in column $z$, where $a$ is identified with the final $m$ entries. The block Gram has all private and cross coordinates zero and top matrix
$$
B(a)=e_0a^{\mathsf T}+ae_0^{\mathsf T}.
$$
The map $a\mapsto B(a)$ is linear and injective. For each vector $a_j$ of the syndrome instance put a separate such block in the generator matrix, sharing the top rows and giving each block its own private rows. The private zero-Gram equations force every feasible deletion to take all or none of each block.

Given syndrome target $y$ and $n$ selectable blocks, a protected correction block gives the full Gram the desired top value $B(y)$. Specifically set $s=y+\sum_j a_j$ and take $7^h$ disjoint copies of the $15$-block for $s$ with common top rows, where $h$ is least with $7^h>n$. Arrange the copies as the leaves of a complete $7$-ary tree of height $h$. At each internal node add three private rows supported below that node. Label its seven children by the nonzero binary $3$-vectors. The new private rows are the three label bits, constant on every position below their respective child. All affected child subtrees contain an odd number of positions. Each row and row-pair product for one node has even total, since the nonzero $3$-bit points have four ones in each bit and two ones in each pair. Rows at unrelated nodes have disjoint support. An ancestor row is constant on a descendant support and all descendant rows have even weight, so their products have even weight as well. A node row also has zero dot product with every bottom private row or top row, because on each bottom block it is constant and all the involved rows have even weight. The correction Gram is $B(s)$ because $7^h$ is odd.

On a feasible deletion, the $15$-position constraints first force each leaf block to be deleted all or none. Induct up the tree. At each node the sums on deletions imposed by its three coordinates and three pairwise products force the seven constant child indicators to agree. The six square-free monomials of degree one or two on the seven nonzero binary $3$-vectors are independent even vectors, hence span their even subspace. Odd subtree sizes ensure these are exactly the six equations on the seven indicators. The whole correction block can therefore be deleted only at cost $15\cdot7^h>15n$. The matrix has polynomial dimensions since $7^h\le 7\max(1,n)$.

For a budget $k\le n$, there is a feasible deletion of at most $15k$ if and only if some set of at most $k$ vectors $a_j$ sums to $y$. Indeed the protected block stays, and the injectivity of $B$ equates exactly the desired syndrome. If the syndrome instance is feasible, taking all vectors of some solution costs at most $15n$, so the optimum is exactly fifteen times its syndrome optimum. A parameter above $n$ can first be capped at $n$. Binary $k$-VectorSum is W[1]-hard, and unparameterized syndrome decoding is NP-complete. [Bhattacharyya–Gadekar–Ghoshal–Saket, binary $k$-VectorSum](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESA.2016.11) · [Berlekamp–McEliece–van Tilborg, syndrome decoding](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1978.1055873)

To see the approximation consequence directly, use the incidence vectors of triples on $3r$ elements and the all-one syndrome. A perfect matching gives optimum $r$. Any solution of size $r+d$ covers each element oddly, so a multiply covered element is covered at least three times. The total excess multiplicity is $3d$, and the total number of incidences on multiply covered elements is at most $9d/2$. Discarding all triples meeting those elements leaves a matching of size at least $r+d-9d/2=r-7d/2$. Petrank's hard perfect-completeness gap, in which the maximum matching is at most $(1-\epsilon)r$ in the negative case for an absolute $\epsilon>0$, therefore forces $d\ge 2\epsilon r/7$. If the syndrome is infeasible we can identify that case by Gaussian elimination, since positive instances always have a feasible syndrome. On the remaining inputs the factor-$15$ reduction preserves this constant gap.
