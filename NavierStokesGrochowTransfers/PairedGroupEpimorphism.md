# Paired cuts for central-radical group epimorphisms

Let $G,H$ be Cayley-table groups whose solvable radicals are central and elementary abelian and whose radical quotients are direct products of nonabelian simple groups. Put $N=|G|+|H|$. Write $D=(D_i)_{i\in S}$ and $B=(B_j)_{j\in T}$ for their respective arrangements of simple-factor centers within their perfect parts, as defined below. When both perfect centers are nontrivial they must have the same prime $p$ for a positive answer. In the trivial-center cases we can use the prime of the nontrivial perfect center, or $p=2$ if both are trivial.

For a subspace arrangement $X$ in its total span, write $w(X)$ for its minimum rooted binary tree width and $c(X)$ for its maximum cut dimension over all subsets. The cut dimension is the dimension of the intersection of the spans of the two sides. The width of a rooted binary tree is the maximum cut dimension over its node leaf sets. The empty arrangement has both parameters zero. Write $\delta$ for the source perfect-center dimension minus the target perfect-center dimension. A negative $\delta$ rules out an epimorphism. For $\delta\ge 0$, set

$$b=\min\{w(D)\min(c(B),w(D)+\delta),\; w(B)\min(c(D),w(B)+\delta)\}.$$

**Theorem.** Decision, construction, and exact counting of epimorphisms $G\twoheadrightarrow H$ take deterministic $p^{2b}\operatorname{poly}(N)$ time. In particular all three are polynomial in the Cayley-table size when $b=O(\log_p N)$. The parameters, needed trees, and certificates are computed from the tables. The criterion permits both tree widths, both center dimensions, the dimension difference, and simple-factor multiplicities to grow. It gives a sufficient case of Grochow–Srivastava–Thakkar Open Question 3(5), catalogued as G08. [Published question](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07429v1#S7)

The recursion takes its local data from the source cut and the complementary target cut simultaneously. Its gluing equation is the exact residual-valued specialization of the child-to-parent cancellation in Alpöge–Buckmaster's `SignedTree.nodeInput` and `signedTreeLowerBound`. The groups, finite-field front spaces, and state count are new mathematical ingredients of the transfer. The proof of the group algorithm is informal. [NS signed-tree input](https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes/blob/ad53419de079eb8f95f60313473374624a348546/lean/Breakdown/CertCell/StatementDefs.lean#L1269) · [NS cancellation](https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes/blob/ad53419de079eb8f95f60313473374624a348546/lean/Breakdown/CertCell/CertTreeLowerBound.lean#L34)

## 1. From group tables to local epimorphisms and a single linear relation test

For either input $J$, $Z(J)$ is its radical. In $J/Z(J)=\prod_i S_i$, the simple factors are exactly the minimal nontrivial normal subgroups. A normal subgroup is a product of factors because the commutator with an element supported on one coordinate isolates every nontrivial coordinate of a normal subgroup. This proves that normal closures of individual quotient elements, restricted to the inclusion-minimal nontrivial closures, find all factors. All computations use polynomially many table operations.

Write $U_i$ for the inverse image of $S_i$ and $E_i=[U_i,U_i]$. Then $U_i=E_iZ(J)$, since $S_i$ is perfect, and consequently $E_i$ is perfect. For distinct $i,i'$, the commutator $[U_i,U_{i'}]$ lies in the center. For each fixed element of $U_{i'}$, taking its commutator gives a homomorphism on $U_i$ with central image. It kills $E_i$. Hence the $E_i$ commute pairwise, $J'=\prod_i E_i$ is perfect, and $A_J=J'\cap Z(J)=\sum_i Z(E_i)$. The last identity follows by taking any product from $J'$ with trivial image in the quotient and testing its independent simple coordinates. A linear complement $C_J$ of $A_J$ in $Z(J)$ gives $J=J'\times C_J$.

Each epimorphism sends $G'$ onto $H'$. Its restriction from $C_G$ to $H$ must land in $B_{\rm tot}\times C_H$, where $B_{\rm tot}=Z(H')$, and must map onto the second factor. Conversely such a choice gives an epimorphism when combined with the onto map on perfect parts. Thus the full count is

$$\#\operatorname{Epi}(G',H')\,\#\operatorname{Hom}(C_G,B_{\rm tot})\,\#\operatorname{Epi}(C_G,C_H).$$

The last two numbers and witnesses follow from linear algebra, with the trivial-group and distinct-prime cases immediate. For the first factor, let $E_i$ and $F_j$ denote the perfect factors of $G'$ and $H'$ and let their centers be $D_i$ and $B_j$. Put $A=Z(G')$, $B_{\rm tot}=Z(H')$. The quotient by the center forces an injection of target factors into source factors. In an onto map between products of centerless simple groups, the images of the individual source factors in each target factor are commuting normal subgroups, so exactly one is nontrivial and it gives an isomorphism. The same source cannot be used twice because its image in those two factors would be diagonal.

The restriction on a matched $E_i$ has image in $F_jZ(H')$. Since $E_i$ is perfect it lands in $F_j$, and an image covering $F_j/Z(F_j)$ equals $F_j$, again by perfection. An unmatched $E_i$ has central image and so maps to the identity. For each match, form the list of local epimorphisms $\theta_i:E_i\twoheadrightarrow F_j$ inducing an isomorphism of the simple quotients. A finite nonabelian simple group is two-generated. A lift of a generating pair to its perfect central extension generates that extension because the subgroup generated by the pair and the center is the whole extension. Enumerate source generator pairs to find one and enumerate all image pairs in $F_j$. For each pair, propagation along the two generators and their inverses specifies the only possible map, and checking the tables verifies the map, surjectivity, and the quotient condition. This costs polynomial table time per matched pair. [Two-generated finite simple groups](https://arxiv.org/abs/0711.2817)

Write $a_i:D_i\to B_j$ for the map on the center obtained from a matched local epimorphism, and $a_i=0$ for an unmatched source. All local maps descend to a unique epimorphism $G'\to H'$ exactly when

$$\sum_{i\in S}a_i(r_i)=0\quad\text{for every }r=(r_i)_i\in R=\ker\left(\bigoplus_{i\in S}D_i\longrightarrow A,\quad (d_i)\longmapsto\sum_i d_i\right).$$

To prove sufficiency, first map the product of the $E_i$ by the local maps. Its kernel of multiplication onto $G'$ lies in the product of the $D_i$, by the distinct quotient coordinates, and is exactly $R$ there. The linear condition kills this kernel. The local images are the $F_j$ and generate $H'$, so the descended map is onto. Necessity follows by restriction. Each distinct injection and tuple of local maps gives a distinct epimorphism because they can be recovered from its restrictions. These reductions agree with the earlier factor-center approach, which kept a map with only one constrained boundary. [Earlier local criterion and one-sided state bounds](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/6b091c10bce8f1f3fca282e739f4bbb1790615b3/FactorCenterEpimorphisms/PROOF.md)

## 2. The exact front condition

For $I\subseteq S$ and $Y\subseteq T$ write

$$L_I=\left(\sum_{i\in I}D_i\right)\cap\left(\sum_{i\notin I}D_i\right),\qquad K_Y=\left(\sum_{j\in Y}B_j\right)\cap\left(\sum_{j\notin Y}B_j\right).$$

Define $q_I:R\to L_I$ by $q_I(r)=\sum_{i\in I}r_i$. This is surjective: write any $x\in L_I$ as a sum on $I$ and its negative as a sum on the complement, then join the two lists. In particular $q_S=0$ and $K_T=0$.

A global solution determines a linear $\alpha:A\to B_{\rm tot}$ by the relation test. On a source subset $I$ that uses precisely the target subset $Y$, the partial sum of local center maps on $R$ is $\alpha q_I$. The image of $\alpha$ on the span from $I$ lies in the span from $Y$, and its image on the complementary span lies in the complementary target span. Thus $\alpha(L_I)\subseteq K_Y$. Its partial sum is represented by the **unique** front $M:L_I\to K_Y$ with partial sum $Mq_I$.

There is a further constraint on paired cuts. Each matched local map takes its center onto the corresponding target factor center, by surjectivity and injectivity of the induced quotient map. Therefore $\alpha$ sends the two source-side spans onto the two corresponding target-side spans. If their dimensions drop by $r_I$ and $r_{S\setminus I}$, respectively, these are dimensions of intersections with $\ker\alpha$ and each lies in $[0,\delta]$. The total span drops in dimension by $\delta$. The dimension formula for sums and intersections gives

$$\dim L_I-\dim K_Y=r_I+r_{S\setminus I}-\delta.$$

Thus $|\dim L_I-\dim K_Y|\le\delta$. The algorithm discards any subset pair failing this bound. Every complete solution still appears, so the pruning preserves the exact root count.

The node condition for a split $(I,Y)=(I_1,Y_1)\sqcup(I_2,Y_2)$ is the equality in $\operatorname{Hom}(R,B_{\rm tot})$

$$M q_I=M_1q_{I_1}+M_2q_{I_2}.$$

At a leaf with source $i$, the equation is $M q_{\{i\}}=a_i\operatorname{pr}_i$. It has a unique solution if it has any. Test each equation by solving on a basis of $R$ and checking membership in $K_Y$. Equivalently, check that the right hand side kills $\ker q_I$ and lies in $K_Y$, then take its quotient map. Addition of these equations over the tree uses the exact cancellation identity from the NS transfer: each nonroot residual $M_\nu q_{I_\nu}$ occurs once on the left and once as a child on the right. The surviving equality at the root is $0=\sum_i a_i\operatorname{pr}_i$. This is exactly the complete descent condition from Section 1. Conversely, any global solution supplies all the node equations by restriction. Therefore this compilation of the local equations is both sound and complete on any rooted binary tree partitioning the assignments.

## 3. Algorithms

Find all subset cut dimensions and projection matrices by Gaussian elimination. Find a minimum-width source tree by dynamic programming on its subsets. For a singleton the optimal value is its cut dimension. At a larger subset the optimal value is the maximum of its cut dimension and the least, over two nonempty child sets partitioning it, of the maximum of the two optimal child widths. The recursion takes at most $3^{|S|}\operatorname{poly}(N)$ time. Apply the same algorithm on the target. Both $|S|$ and $|T|$ are at most $\log_2 N$, so all these subset computations cost polynomial table time.

For a fixed source tree, list achievable fronts for each source node $I$ and each possible target subset $Y$. At a leaf try the omission and every local match. For a parent, split the target set into disjoint child sets and solve the node equation for each pair of stored child fronts. The count associated to a front is the sum of products of its two child counts, with one contribution per local map at a leaf. A choice of partial injection and maps fixes its child sets and front values uniquely, since the source tree is fixed. Thus this is exact counting, including when several distinct local maps give the same matrix. At the root accept only the state using all target factors. An accepting entry recovers a witness by following retained choices. Its count is $\#\operatorname{Epi}(G',H')$. For a pair surviving the dimension test, $\dim L_I\le w(D)$ and $\dim K_Y\le\min(c(B),w(D)+\delta)$. Its front count is therefore at most $p^{w(D)\min(c(B),w(D)+\delta)}$.

Alternatively fix a target tree, and for each target node $Y$ vary the subset $I$ of used source factors. A leaf tries one match. At the root allow any $I$ and omit the other sources. The same equations on the original full $R$ already incorporate these omitted zero maps. Although $q_I$ can be nonzero at this root, $K_T=0$ forces its front to be zero, so the same cancellation proves the relation test. The same fixed-tree uniqueness proves exact counts, now bounded by $p^{w(B)\min(c(D),w(B)+\delta)}$ fronts per node and source subset. In the case of an empty target tree there is just the trivial perfect-part map. Taking the smaller exponent gives the claimed time, because there are at most $3^{|S|+|T|}$ subset assignments at a merge and $p^{2b}$ pairs of fronts. Counts have polynomial bit length, bounded above by the number $|H|^{|G|}$ of all maps. Multiplying by the abelian counts completes the algorithm on the original groups.

There is also an adaptive decision and search variant. For a supplied budget $b_0$, store Boolean entries for pairs $(I,Y)$ with $\dim L_I\dim K_Y\le b_0$ and vary both binary splits. The root tests whether an epimorphism admits some tree of such paired cuts and returns a witness when it does, in $3^{|S|+|T|}p^{2b_0}\operatorname{poly}(N)$ time. The leaf conditions and root check are the same as for the source tree. Deduplicating across tree choices is unnecessary for decision. The exact-count theorem above uses a fixed tree.

## 4. Families beyond the single-boundary regimes

Over $\mathbb F_2$, the elementary example groups come from $P=\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$, which is perfect with center of order two and quotient $A_5$. For every spanning list $(x_i)_{i=1}^k$ in a binary space $A$, quotient $P^k$ by the central kernel of the linear map $\mathbb F_2^k\to A$ sending its standard basis to the $x_i$. The resulting group is perfect and its factor-center arrangement consists of the lines $\langle x_i\rangle$, or zero when $x_i=0$. Its order lies in $[60^k,120^k]$.

For integer $m\ge 4$, use the list of all $e_u+e_v$ with $1\le u<v\le m$ as one block on the binary even-weight space of dimension $m-1$. Adjoin $2m^2$ independent private coordinate lines in the source and $m^2$ in the target. Use the same complete-graph block in both. Every cut intersection lies in the complete-graph block since every private line is independent of all other lines. Therefore $c(D),c(B)\le m-1$ and $b\le(m-1)^2=O(\log_2 N)$. Both widths nevertheless grow linearly in $m$. In any binary tree select a node whose cut divides the graph edges into two sets each of size at least one third of all graph edges. Such a node exists by descending through a child with more than two thirds of the graph-edge leaves until none exists, then taking the larger child. For a subgraph whose binary incidence-column rank is $r$, every connected component on $s$ vertices contributes $s-1$ to the rank, as follows by taking a spanning tree and summing along paths. The number of edges is at most $\binom{r+1}{2}$ because summing $\binom{s}{2}$ over components is maximized by concentrating their rank in one component. Consequently each side of the selected cut has rank at least $m/\sqrt 3-O(1)$ inside the graph block. Their sum is the full even-weight space, and their intersection has dimension at least $(2/\sqrt 3-1)m-O(1)$. Private coordinates do not change that intersection.

In these examples the target center and the source–target dimension difference grow quadratically in $m$, the sole simple type has unbounded multiplicity, and the center prime stays two. Omitting the excess private source factors gives an epimorphism. Perturbing the two graph blocks to arbitrary binary lists on their respective $O(m)$-dimensional core spaces, keeping $\Theta(m^2)$ independent private lines, preserves the stated polynomial bound, with the algorithm deciding whether the resulting pairs admit epimorphisms.

The dimension-drop pruning gives a second regime where even the products $w(D)c(B)$ and $w(B)c(D)$ overestimate the required state count. Keep the complete-graph blocks, and on both sides append $m^2$ independent coordinate lines each listed twice. Add $m$ independent lines listed once only on the source side. Now $c(D),c(B)\ge m^2$ by splitting the two copies of every doubled line across a cut. A tree that groups each doubled line into sibling leaves and keeps the complete-graph block in one subtree has width at most $m-1$. The preceding core balancing argument still gives $w(D),w(B)=\Omega(m)$. Here $\delta=m$, $\log_2 N=\Theta(m^2)$, and the pruned exponent $b$ is $O(m^2)$. Both unpruned products are $\Omega(m^3)$. Omitting the $m$ private source factors again gives an epimorphism, and the theorem applies to both positive and negative inputs obeying the dimension bound.

These regimes go beyond fixed one-sided branchwidth. The unrestricted question remains open.
