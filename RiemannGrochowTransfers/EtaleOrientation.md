# Determinants of étale multiplication tensors

A multiplication tensor of a finite-dimensional algebra is the bilinear map $(x,y)\mapsto xy$. An isotope is obtained by changing bases independently on the two inputs and the output. All vector spaces below have given ordered bases. A transporter from $T$ to $T'$ is a triple of invertible maps $(P,Q,U)$ satisfying $U T(x,y)=T'(Px,Qy)$. A special-linear transporter has determinant one in each mode.

## Theorem 1. Deterministic decision over variable finite fields

Given two bilinear tensors over an explicitly represented finite field $\mathbb F_q$, one can recognize whether each is an isotope of a nonzero finite étale algebra. On that locus, one can decide whether a transporter has any prescribed determinant triple. Both operations take deterministic time polynomial in the input size, including $\log q$. The algorithm uses field arithmetic and linear algebra, with no finite-field polynomial factorization or construction of an isomorphism between field factors.

### Recovering the algebras

For a tensor $T:V_1\times V_2\to W$, solve the linear equations for pairs $(X,Y)$ of mode endomorphisms with $T(Xx,y)=T(x,Yy)$. Their projections $B_1,B_2$ are unital subalgebras of the endomorphism rings, since the pairs are closed under $(X,Y)(X',Y')=(XX',Y'Y)$. For multiplication in a unital associative algebra, setting each argument to $1$ identifies the projected algebras with right and left multiplication, respectively. Consequently, for an étale isotope all three dimensions are equal to some $d>0$, both projected algebras are commutative and reduced of dimension $d$, and each projected algebra acts faithfully on its mode.

Check these conditions. In a commutative algebra over $\mathbb F_q$, reducedness is equivalent to bijectivity of the $q$-power map. Its matrix is obtained by repeated powering of basis elements. A faithful $d$-dimensional module for a $d$-dimensional étale algebra is regular. Indeed, every field factor acts on a nonzero summand and each such summand has dimension over that field at least one. Equality of dimensions makes every summand one-dimensional over its factor.

A cyclic vector for either projected algebra $B$ can be found without factoring $B$. For each vector $v_i$ of the given mode basis, compute the ideal $K_i=\ker(B\to V,\ b\mapsto b v_i)$ and its annihilator by linear algebra. The idempotent $e_i$ that is zero on $K_i$ and one on its complementary factors is the $\operatorname{Ann}(K_i)$ part of the decomposition $1\in K_i\oplus\operatorname{Ann}(K_i)$. The idempotents $f_i=e_i\prod_{j<i}(1-e_j)$ have disjoint support and sum to $1$, because the action is faithful. The vector $\sum_i f_i v_i$ has a nonzero coordinate on every field factor, so it generates the regular module. One may compute $f_i$ without iterative expressions by projecting the unit to the ideal of factors on which $v_i$ is the first basis vector with nonzero coordinate. This ideal is $\operatorname{Ann}(K_i)\cap\bigcap_{j<i}K_j$, and the projection uses its annihilator as above.

Choose cyclic vectors $\alpha\in V_1$ and $\beta\in V_2$. Define $J=T(-,\beta)$ and $L=T(\alpha,-)$ into $W$. Check that they are invertible and set $x\star y=T(J^{-1}x,L^{-1}y)$. In hidden multiplication coordinates where $T(Px,Qy)=U(xy)$, the cyclic vectors correspond to units $a,b$. Then $J(Px)=U(xb)$ and $L(Qy)=U(ay)$. Thus $z\mapsto U(zab)$ is an isomorphism from the hidden algebra to $(W,\star)$. Conversely, if the recovered product is unital, commutative, associative and reduced, $(J,L,I)$ is a tensor isomorphism from $T$ to its multiplication. These verifications use the basis and establish exact recognition. The construction refines the earlier normalization for semisimple isotopes. [Earlier normalization](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/tree/main/AlpogeTISL), [Brooksbank and Luks on module isomorphism](https://ix.cs.uoregon.edu/~luks/ModIso.pdf).

### Field degrees without factorization

Let $A$ be the recovered algebra and $\sigma(x)=x^q$. Let $I_k$ be the ideal generated by the vectors $\sigma^k(b_i)-b_i$ for a basis $(b_i)$ of $A$. It is the span of the products of these vectors with basis elements. A field factor $\mathbb F_{q^e}$ contributes zero to $I_k$ if $e\mid k$. Otherwise some basis vector has nonzero difference there and the factor contributes the entire factor to $I_k$. Hence $\operatorname{Ann}(I_k)$ is the ideal of factors with degrees dividing $k$. Intersect it with the annihilator of the sum of the corresponding ideals for proper divisors of $k$. The result is the ideal of factors of degree exactly $k$. Its dimension divided by $k$ gives their multiplicity. Running over $k=1,\ldots,d$ recovers the multiset of degrees, which decides ordinary tensor isomorphism by the calculation below.

### The determinant triples

For multiplication tables $A,A'$ every transporter has the form
$$
(P,Q,U)=(L_a\phi,L_b\phi,L_{ab}\phi)
$$
with $a,b\in A'^\times$ and a unital algebra isomorphism $\phi:A\to A'$. Indeed $a=P(1), b=Q(1)$, the equations $U(x)=bP(x)=aQ(x)$ make $a,b$ units, and $\phi=(ab)^{-1}U$ is a unital algebra isomorphism by substituting into the transporter equation. Conversely the displayed maps satisfy that equation.

Write $J',L'$ for the target normalization, $\lambda_J=\det J/\det J'$ and $\lambda_L=\det L/\det L'$. Multiplication on a field factor has determinant its field norm. Finite-field norms are surjective, so $a$ and $b$ can independently have any prescribed nonzero multiplication determinants. It follows that a triple $(s,t,w)\in(\mathbb F_q^\times)^3$ occurs for the original tensors exactly when some algebra isomorphism of the recovered tables has determinant
$$
r=\frac{st}{\lambda_J\lambda_L w}.
\tag{1}
$$
To see this, the three determinants before eliminating the unit choices are $(\lambda_J u r,\lambda_L v r,uvr)$. The first two equations determine $u,v$, and the last one is then equivalent to (1).

Suppose the degree multisets agree. The form $B_A(x,y)=\operatorname{Tr}(L_{xy})$ is nonsingular since $A$ is étale, and every algebra isomorphism preserves it. The determinant of any algebra isomorphism therefore satisfies
$$
r^2=\frac{\det B_A}{\det B_{A'}}.
\tag{2}
$$
In characteristic two squaring is injective, so this condition is sufficient. In odd characteristic, an even-degree factor has Frobenius of determinant $-1$ by a normal basis. Two copies of the same odd-degree factor can be exchanged with determinant $-1$. These cases make both signs in (2) attainable. In the remaining case all factor degrees are odd and distinct. Every algebra automorphism has determinant $1$, since it acts separately on the factors by powers of Frobenius. The following computation finds the attainable sign directly.

### Moore determinants determine the sign

Let $E/\mathbb F_q$ be a field of odd degree $e$ and $b=(b_1,\dots,b_e)$ an ordered basis. Compute
$$
\Delta_E(b)=\det_E(b_j^{q^i})_{0\le i<e,\ 1\le j\le e}.
$$
This determinant is nonzero. For a basis of powers of a primitive element, the matrix is a Vandermonde matrix on its distinct conjugates. For another basis the matrix is obtained by right multiplication by an invertible $\mathbb F_q$-matrix. Applying Frobenius cyclically permutes the rows, so $\Delta_E(b)$ lies in $\mathbb F_q^\times$ when $e$ is odd. For an isomorphism $\phi:E\to E'$ with matrix $P$ in bases $b,c$, applying $\phi$ entrywise yields the Moore matrix of $c$ times $P$. As $\phi$ fixes the base field, its determinant in those bases is $\Delta_E(b)/\Delta_{E'}(c)$. The base-field element is extracted by expressing it in the one-dimensional span of the unit. The computation takes polynomial time without constructing $\phi$.

Use the degree ideals to choose bases for the individual odd-degree factors and concatenate them in increasing degree order. Multiply their Moore ratios and compose with the two basis changes to obtain the unique determinant in the original bases. Together with (1) and (2) this proves Theorem 1, including the special-linear case $(s,t,w)=(1,1,1)$. The Moore matrix computation works for every field size and odd extension degree, including extensions whose degree is divisible by the characteristic. [Moore determinant](https://encyclopediaofmath.org/index.php?title=Moore_determinant).

## Theorem 2. Small inert primes for cyclic fields

Assume Dirichlet GRH. Let $E/\mathbb Q$ be finite cyclic and $B$ a positive integer divisible by the absolute field discriminant and any primes to be excluded. There is an absolute effective constant $C$ such that a prime in $(X/2,X)$ is inert in $E$ and coprime to $B$ whenever $X\ge C\log^2(2B)$. The constant has no factor depending separately on the field degree. The assertion includes the degree-one case, where inert means the residue degree is one.

Write $G=\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)$, $d=|G|$, and $\delta=\varphi(d)/d$. The indicator $g$ of generators of $G$ has a Fourier coefficient at a character $\chi$ of order $m$ given by $\widehat{g}(\chi)=\delta\mu(m)/\varphi(m)$. This is the Ramanujan sum formula. To verify it, expand the indicator of residues $a\bmod d$ coprime to $d$ as $\sum_{k\mid(a,d)}\mu(k)$. Summing $\chi(a)$ over multiples of each $k$ gives zero unless $m\mid k$, in which case it gives $d/k$. Dividing by $d$ and expanding the resulting sum for squarefree $m$ gives $\delta\mu(m)/\varphi(m)$. Nonsquarefree $m$ gives zero.

Fix a nonnegative twice continuously differentiable weight $v$ supported in $[1/2,1]$, positive inside it. For a primitive Dirichlet character of conductor $f_\chi$ the smoothed explicit formula over prime powers has main term $X\int v$ for the trivial character and error $O_v(\sqrt X\log(2 f_\chi))$. A zero $\rho$ contributes $X^\rho\widehat v(\rho)$, where $\widehat v$ is the Mellin transform. The estimate follows from $|X^\rho|=\sqrt X$, decay $O_v((1+|\Im\rho|)^{-2})$, and the unit-height zero count $O(\log(2 f_\chi(2+|\Im\rho|)))$. Removing higher prime powers costs $O_v(\sqrt X)$. To combine the characters, evaluate at ordinary rational primes outside the conductor $f$ of the field and replace primitive inducing values with the characters of $G$. We can remove primes dividing $B$ from each primitive character sum before combining the characters, at cost $O_v(\log(2B))$ per character. This gives
$$
\sum_{p\nmid B}g(\operatorname{Frob}_p)\log p\,v(p/X)
=\delta X\int v+O_v\left(\sqrt X\sum_\chi|\widehat g(\chi)|\log(2 f_\chi)+\log(2B)\sum_\chi|\widehat g(\chi)|\right).
$$
Each coefficient has absolute value at most $\delta$, hence $\sum_\chi|\widehat g(\chi)|\le\delta d$.

Indeed the conductor–discriminant formula gives $\sum_\chi\log f_\chi=\log|\operatorname{disc}E|$, and every nontrivial conductor is at least $3$. Thus $d\le1+\log B/\log3$ and
$$
\sum_\chi|\widehat g(\chi)|\log(2 f_\chi)
\le \delta \bigl(d\log 2+\log|\operatorname{disc} E|\bigr)
\ll\delta\log(2B).
$$
The exclusion cost above is $O_v(\delta\log^2(2B))$. Taking $C$ sufficiently large makes the main term dominate, and a generator of $G$ is precisely Frobenius at an inert prime. This gives a degree-free, deterministic bounded scan using inertness tests on multiplication tables. [Character-weighted explicit formula](https://www.ams.org/mcom/1996-65-216/S0025-5718-96-00763-6/S0025-5718-96-00763-6.pdf), [conductor budget for the earlier abelian prime signatures](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/AlpogeGrochowFourEngines/AbelianPrimeSignatures.md).

## Theorem 3. Modular recovery of rational special-linear transport

Assume Dirichlet GRH. On the rational multiplication-isotope locus where every field factor is cyclic of odd degree and there is at least one $\mathbb Q$ factor, special-linear tensor isomorphism has a deterministic polynomial-time decision from rational linear algebra, trace discriminants and modular records. It also decides every prescribed triple of nonzero rational determinants. If the ordinary isomorphism test succeeds, one may recover the determinant of a field isomorphism using one separately chosen inert prime for each matched component. No prime needs to be inert in all components at once. After the rational computations below, every prime used is bounded by $C\log^2(2B)$ for an explicitly computable exceptional product $B$ of polynomial bit length.

Normalize as in Theorem 1. For a commutative projected algebra over $\mathbb Q$, test reducedness by the nondegeneracy of the trace pairing. Over characteristic zero the radical of this pairing is the nilradical. Construct the rational primitive idempotents by the algorithm of Lenstra and Silverberg. Over $\mathbb Q$ the cyclic-vector construction can use the projection formula from Theorem 1 to keep bit lengths polynomial. Decide whether the recovered algebras have the same field types by the component-marked abelian signature test with its order-discriminant cutoff. Since the promised factors are cyclic they meet its abelian hypothesis. A $\mathbb Q$ factor makes the multiplication determinant surjective onto $\mathbb Q^\times$, so (1) remains the exact target determinant for an algebra isomorphism. [Lenstra and Silverberg](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.08843), [earlier marked signature proof](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/AlpogeGrochowFourEngines/AbelianPrimeSignatures.md).

Every automorphism of an odd-degree cyclic field has determinant one, using a normal basis. Thus both signs for the total determinant occur if identical field factors repeat, by interchanging two copies. The trace equation (2) then suffices. In the remaining case match the field factors by their isomorphism types and choose corresponding orders of the factors and ordered bases on both sides. For a matched pair, the trace ratio gives the square of the determinant of its unique orientation. Extract one of its two rational roots $r_i$ by taking integer square roots on the reduced numerator and denominator. They are squares because the isomorphism already exists. Each factor isomorphism maps the maximal order to the maximal order. Away from the discriminants of the chosen orders, the chosen order lattices are equal to the maximal-order lattices over $\mathbb Z_p$, because the order index squared divides the discriminant. Include in $B$ the order discriminants, $2$, the denominators of the basis changes to those orders, their nonzero determinant numerators, and the nonzero numerators and denominators of the $r_i$. All have polynomial bit length by rational linear algebra and integer square-root bounds. Equivalently, work in order bases throughout each component and include the basis changes only when assembling the total determinant.

For each matched field pair use Theorem 2 to find an inert prime $p\nmid B$. At such a prime the component isomorphism reduces to an isomorphism of finite fields of odd degree. Its determinant is given by the Moore ratio of Theorem 1 in the reduced bases. As $p$ is odd and $r_i$ is a unit at $p$, the two rational sign choices have distinct reductions. This identifies the rational sign exactly. The product of the recovered determinants, composed with the accumulated changes of basis, gives the unique determinant of an algebra isomorphism. Equation (1) gives the claimed decision. The promised class allows arbitrary repeated cyclic field factors and composita of noncyclic Galois group.

## Example. Every good reduction can lose the determinant sign

Take the compositum of the cyclic cubic subfields of $\mathbb Q(\zeta_7)$ and $\mathbb Q(\zeta_{13})$. The two fields have coprime conductors and are disjoint, so the Galois group is $C_3^2$. Let $A$ be $\mathbb Q$ times the product of all four distinct cubic subfields of that compositum, and choose the product of their maximal orders. The isomorphism determinant group of $A$ is trivial, since each field factor has odd degree and the factors are pairwise nonisomorphic. In the ordered product basis take $S=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)$. The tensors $T(x,y)=xy$ and $T'(x,y)=S(xy)$ are ordinary-isomorphic over $\mathbb Q$ and special-linear-inequivalent. In fact the reference determinant triple is $(1,1,-1)$, while an automorphism of determinant $-1$ would be necessary to cancel it by the multiplication-transporter formula.

At any prime unramified in the compositum, the Frobenius element lies in at least one of the four order-three kernels. The corresponding cubic factor splits completely, giving three equal degree-one field factors in the reduced algebra. Exchanging two of them gives an automorphism of determinant $-1$ in odd characteristic. Multiplication by units can then cancel all three determinants. In characteristic two $S$ already has determinant one. Thus the unmarked reductions are special-linear-isomorphic at every good prime. Separately choosing one inert prime per factor, as in Theorem 3, keeps the information that the unmarked reductions lose.
