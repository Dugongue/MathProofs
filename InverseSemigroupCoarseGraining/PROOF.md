# Exact counting for targets with an inverse semigroup term

Let \(S,B\) be explicit finite nonempty algebras of the same fixed finite signature of bounded arity. Suppose some binary term with constants from \(B\) defines an inverse semigroup multiplication on \(B\). Then for fixed \(B\), one can count all surjective homomorphisms \(S\to B\) and construct one when the count is positive in deterministic polynomial time in the source table length. Uniformly, with \(m=|S|\) and \(b=|B|\), the bound is \(b^{O(b^2)}(m+1)^{O(b)}\) for \(b\geq 2\). Only the multiplication needs to be a term.

An inverse semigroup has associative multiplication, a unique inverse \(x^{-1}\) for each \(x\) with \(xx^{-1}x=x\) and \(x^{-1}xx^{-1}=x^{-1}\), and commuting idempotents. Its idempotents \(E\) form a meet-semilattice under multiplication. The proof combines the principal-filter enumeration from the earlier elementary coarse-graining proof and the logarithmic quasigroup generation argument from *Four algorithms and reductions around Grochow's questions*. The combination applies to a class that need not admit either of those two terms on the whole target.

## 1. Finding the term and recovering the idempotent coordinates

One can find a multiplication term by closing the two projections and the \(b\) constant binary tables on \(B\) under pointwise application of the basic operations. The resulting at most \(b^{b^2}\) tables are exactly the binary term operations with named constants. Store circuits for their construction and test the multiplication axioms on each table. The singleton case is immediate. If \(m<b\) return zero.

Enumerate one candidate source preimage for each of the \(b\) named target elements. For this choice, all terms with these constants have interpretations on the source. Put \(\omega=b!\). Every element \(y\) of a finite semigroup on \(b\) points has \(y^\omega\) idempotent. The powers enter a periodic part with index at most \(b\) and period at most \(b\), so \(\omega\) is past the index and is divisible by the period.

For \(x\in B\), put \(d(x)=x^{-1}x\) and \(r(x)=xx^{-1}\). In the semilattice order on \(E\) these are given by

\[
d(x)=\max_{a\in B}(ax)^\omega,\qquad
r(x)=\max_{a\in B}(xa)^\omega .
\]

For the second equality the range idempotent of \(xy\) is \(xyy^{-1}x^{-1}\le xx^{-1}\). This follows by multiplying it by \(xx^{-1}\). Repeated application gives the bound for all positive powers of \(xa\), and an idempotent equals its own range idempotent. Taking \(a=x^{-1}\) attains \(xx^{-1}\). The domain equality follows by reversing the products. Thus the right sides use only multiplication terms and named constants.

Form the subset \(D\) of \(S\) generated under the chosen multiplication by all values of all the terms \((ax)^\omega,(xa)^\omega\) on \(S\). Under any compatible homomorphism \(D\) maps to \(E\). Enumerate all multiplication-preserving maps \(D\to E\) as follows. For each \(e\in E\) the inverse image of its principal upset is a subset \(U\) satisfying

\[
u*v\in U\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\in U\ \text{and}\ v\in U.
\]

Here \(*\) is the interpreted operation on \(D\), which can be arbitrary. Any nonempty \(U\) of this kind is the least subset obeying these implications that contains a single point. Multiply all its elements by a fixed parenthesized fold. The fold belongs to \(U\) by closure, and the backward implications recover each element from the fold. Conversely the least such subset for any seed is computed by iterative implication propagation. Enumerating all seeds and the empty set therefore lists all possible upset preimages, with duplicates removable by equality of subsets. Choose one preimage per \(e\), and decode the image of each point using the membership vector in principal upsets. Distinct elements of \(E\) have distinct vectors. Check decoding and multiplication preservation. At most \((m+1)^{|E|}\) lists survive before these checks.

For each map on \(D\), the two maximum formulas determine \(d(h(x)),r(h(x))\) for every \(x\in S\) using the images of the listed term values. Reject if a required maximum fails to exist. This partitions \(S\) into the candidate preimages of the domain-range classes of \(B\).

## 2. Recovering maps on individual classes

For every nonempty class \(B_{e,f}=\{x:d(x)=e,\ r(x)=f\}\) choose one \(c\in B_{e,f}\). The ternary expression \(x c^{-1}y\) defines a group law on \(B_{e,f}\) with identity \(c\). Indeed \(x\mapsto x c^{-1}\) bijects the class with the group of elements having both domain and range \(f\), with inverse \(z\mapsto zc\). Multiplication under this bijection pulls back to the displayed law. Its interpretation on the source uses the already chosen preimage of the named element \(c^{-1}\).

The matching source subset must be closed under this interpreted binary operation for a compatible map to exist. Also nonempty target classes must have nonempty source preimage for a surjection. Check these requirements and reject if they fail. To enumerate compatible maps on a source subset, quotient by the congruence generated by left and right cancellation requirements. Concretely whenever a translation in the current quotient identifies two distinct points, merge them and take congruence closure for this binary operation. Each step is forced for any map to the target group and reduces the size. At termination the quotient is a finite quasigroup. A nonempty product-closed subset of a finite quasigroup is itself a quasigroup. If the subset is proper, adjoining one point from outside at least doubles its generated size, because the left translate by that point is disjoint from the subset. To see disjointness, fix a right factor in the subset and use the bijectivity of its right translation both within the subset and on the whole quasigroup. Thus the quotient has at most \(1+\lfloor\log_2 m\rfloor\) generators. Choosing their images and propagating products enumerates all the maps, with at most \(|B_{e,f}|^{1+\lfloor\log_2 m\rfloor}\) choices for the class.

Combine the class maps and check every original basic operation, consistency on \(D\), the chosen pins, and surjectivity. Count each map only under the pins that are least in a fixed source ordering for their respective target values. Within those pins the map on \(D\) and the generator images on every class are already determined by the map, so this counts it once. Using \(\sum_{e,f}\log_2|B_{e,f}|\le b\), with the sum over nonempty classes, gives the stated bound. There are only polynomially many output bits even for an arbitrary explicit target, since the count is bounded by \(b^m\).

## 3. Consequence for cellular automata

For a radius-one cellular automaton of source alphabet size \(a\) and block length \(N\), the block algebra has \(m=a^N\) elements and one ternary operation. Target surjective coarse-grainings are precisely surjective homomorphisms of these algebras. This gives exact counting and witness construction in single-exponential time in \(N\) for fixed alphabets, and an EXP upper bound for unary \(N\) with an explicit variable target of this type. This extends the target classes handled for Song–Grochow's fixed-block problem. [Song–Grochow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12153) · [Barto–DeMeo–Mottet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04958)

A nontrivial finite abelian group with an absorbing zero, with multiplication as its only basic operation, illustrates the combination. A term genuinely involving both variables maps any input pair with a zero entry to zero, so cannot be a quasigroup law. A meet term cannot contain the zero constant since it would then be constant, and must involve both variables by commutativity and idempotence. Its restriction to the abelian group would then be an affine term with a common linear part \(L\) on both slots. Associativity forces \(L^2=L\) and idempotence forces \(2L=I\), which together imply \(L=I\) and then \(I=0\), a contradiction. The inverse semigroup argument covers this target as well as finite inverse semigroups with nontrivial domain-range classes. This proof is informal.

## 4. General decomposition by unary term values

The enumeration method works under the following broader condition on any finite target \(B\). Choose unary terms \(\rho_i\) with named constants. For each \(i\), the closure of \(\rho_i(B)\) under some binary term is required to be a meet-semilattice or a quasigroup with that operation. Partition \(B\) into classes whose membership is determined by the joint list of values of the \(\rho_i\). Each class must itself carry a meet-semilattice or quasigroup law given by some binary term with named constants. The terms may vary from class to class. The features and classes need not be homomorphisms and subalgebras for the original signature.

Under these hypotheses the argument gives deterministic polynomial-time exact counting and witness construction for a fixed target. After choosing the constant pins, close each interpreted \(\rho_i(S)\) under the corresponding binary term in the source. Any compatible surjection induces one of the maps from this closure to the specified target closure. List all candidates using the semilattice or quasigroup enumeration above. For each choice of maps, their values at the points \(\rho_i(x)\) give a class for every \(x\), or force rejection if their joint values do not occur in \(B\). Apply the same enumeration separately to each class, after checking closure under its binary term. Verify the assembled map against the original operations, all partial closure assignments, and the pins. Deduplication proceeds exactly as before.

For a uniform bound there is a choice with at most \(b-1\) unary terms. Add features that strictly refine the joint equality partition until the original joint equality partition is reached. Any dropped feature is determined by the retained ones on the target, so class membership is still determined. With bounded arity, unary and binary term tables can be saturated in \(b^{O(b^2)}\) time. Search across tuples of at most \(b-1\) unary tables and associated binary tables, across all partitions constant on joint equality classes, and across the binary table for each class. This uses \(b^{O(b^3)}\) time and recognizes the condition. Enumeration on the source then uses at most \((m+1)^{O(b^2)}\) candidates times \(b^{O(b^3)}\) overhead. Inverse semigroups meet the condition by taking the two families of power terms from §1, grouping by the recovered domain-range pair, and using the group laws of §2.
