# Central-radical group epimorphisms and binary subcode equivalence

This note gives an exact coding-theoretic formulation for one family in Grochow–Srivastava–Thakkar Open Question 3(5), a polynomial-time Cayley-table algorithm under a multiplicity condition, and a bound of $N^{O(1+\log\log N)}$ for an arbitrary source and the full target family in that question. The unqualified polynomial-time question remains open. [Open Question 3(5)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07429v1#S7).

## 1. A two-way correspondence on powers of one fixed group

Let $P=\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$, and identify its center with $\mathbb F_2$. Its central quotient $\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$ is the simple group $A_5$. The group $P$ is perfect. For example it is generated by the elementary transvections, and conjugation by $\operatorname{diag}(2,2^{-1})$ scales their parameters by $4$ or its inverse; commutators therefore give all the elementary transvections. For a binary linear code $C\subseteq\mathbb F_2^k=Z(P^k)$, put $G(C)=P^k/C$. This is perfect, has central elementary abelian radical, and its central quotient is $A_5^k$. The center is exactly the image of $Z(P^k)$, because the latter image is central and the quotient by it is centerless.

**Claim.** For $C\subseteq\mathbb F_2^k$ and $D\subseteq\mathbb F_2^\ell$, an epimorphism $G(C)\to G(D)$ exists exactly when there is an ordered selection of $\ell$ distinct source coordinates for which the projection sends $C$ into $D$.

An epimorphism takes center into center and induces an epimorphism $A_5^k\to A_5^\ell$. Each output factor is reached by exactly one input factor: any nontrivial homomorphism from $A_5$ into $A_5$ is an automorphism, and two distinct source factors have commuting images. Two target factors cannot use the same input factor because the resulting diagonal image in their product would not be surjective. This gives the ordered selection.

For any simple factor in the quotient, its preimage in $G(C)$ is the product of the image of its $P$-factor and the center. Its commutator subgroup is the image of the $P$-factor alone. Hence a deleted $P$-factor maps trivially into $G(D)$, since its image is central and it is perfect. On a retained factor, the image of $P$ covers the target factor modulo its center. Since that target factor is perfect, its image is the whole corresponding target commutator subgroup. That target is itself $P$ or $A_5$, according to whether the target factor's center was killed. In the first case the map is an isomorphism and sends the unique central involution to the unique central involution. In the second case the central involution maps to identity. Therefore the effect of the composite $P^k\to G(C)\to G(D)$ on all central coordinate vectors is precisely the claimed ordered projection followed by reduction modulo $D$. Its kernel contains $C$. Conversely, any such ordered projection of code coordinates comes from the same ordered projection $P^k\to P^\ell$, which descends to an epimorphism of the two quotients.

In particular, for $k=\ell$ this is binary subcode equivalence, the test $\pi C\subseteq D$ for some coordinate permutation. Therefore epimorphism testing under the succinct representation by the two central-kernel generator matrices is NP-complete, using binary subcode equivalence. One can also get hardness directly from binary minimum distance. For a code $D_0$ and target distance $w\ge1$, append an independent unconstrained block of $w-1$ coordinates. The new code has a word of weight exactly $w$ exactly when $D_0$ has a nonzero word of weight at most $w$. Test containment of a permutation of the span of a chosen word of weight $w$. This is subcode equivalence and reduces to the group test. A coordinate permutation provides a polynomial witness in this encoding. [Binary minimum-distance hardness](https://doi.org/10.1145/258533.258559), [subcode equivalence and permuted kernels](https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/1749.pdf).

In the Cayley-table representation, the order of $G(C)$ is $120^k/|C|$, between $60^k$ and $120^k$. Build its table in $2^{O(k)}$ time by enumerating the product and passing to central cosets. Thus a polynomial Cayley-table solution to Question 3(5) would give a $2^{O(k)}$-time algorithm for binary subcode equivalence of length $k$. Conversely, a $2^{O(k)}$-time subcode equivalence algorithm suffices for the Cayley-table subfamily just constructed, including different lengths. To recover $C$, find the minimal normal factors in the center quotient by taking normal closures of all elements and keeping inclusion-minimal nonidentity closures. In the commutator subgroup of each factor preimage, locate the central involution or record zero if that center is trivial. The linear dependencies of this list in the elementary abelian center give $C$. Do the same for $D$. Enumerate the at most $2^k$ retained coordinate subsets, project $C$, and test permutation containment in $D$. All recovery steps have polynomial Cayley-table cost.

## 2. An algorithm for the general target family

Let $G$ be arbitrary. Let $H$ have central elementary abelian solvable radical $R$, and suppose $Q=H/R$ is a direct product of nonabelian simple groups. Put $H_0=[H,H]$. Perfection of $Q$ gives $H=H_0R$; centrality of $R$ gives $H_0=[H_0,H_0]$. Since $R$ is elementary abelian, choose a linear complement $A_0$ to $H_0\cap R$ in $R$. Then $H=H_0\times A_0$, and $Z(H_0)=H_0\cap R$. These are computable by table operations.

Any pair of epimorphisms $f_0\colon G\to H_0$ and $f_1\colon G/[G,G]\to A_0$ combines to an epimorphism onto $H$. Indeed, $f_0([G,G])=H_0$, so it is possible to correct the first coordinate within $[G,G]$ while preserving the second. Conversely every epimorphism onto $H$ decomposes uniquely into such a pair. The abelian factor is decided, searched and counted by linear algebra on $G/[G,G]$ modulo the prime of $A_0$. For a domain space of dimension $a$ and target dimension $b\le a$, the number of epimorphisms is $\prod_{i=0}^{b-1}(p^a-p^i)$, obtained by choosing independent rows.

### All possible kernels for the centerless quotient

Construct a chief series of $G$ by finding a smallest nonidentity normal closure of an element in each quotient. This is a minimal normal subgroup, since any nontrivial proper normal subgroup inside it would contain an element with a smaller closure. For each nonabelian chief section let $C_i$ be the kernel of the conjugation action of $G$ on that section. For any epimorphism $G\to Q$, its kernel is an intersection of some of the $C_i$. Indeed the chief series maps to a chief series after the trivial sections are removed. Each surviving section is isomorphic to the original section, by minimal normality. The intersection over the surviving nonabelian sections contains the kernel and its image centralizes those sections. That image is solvable: at a nonabelian surviving section a nontrivial intersection would be the entire section by minimal normality, and centralizing it would then force it to be abelian. All sections of the restricted series are consequently abelian. The image is trivial because $Q$ has no solvable normal subgroups. The number of candidates before deduplication is at most $2^{\lfloor\log_2|G|\rfloor}$. The chief series, centralizers, kernel masks, and quotient tables have polynomial cost. This is the earlier MathProofs chief-factor kernel method.

### Lifts over an exact quotient map

For each obtained quotient $G/L$, test whether it is a product of the required simple factors, by finding its minimal normal subgroups and checking their simplicity and product. Enumerate all isomorphisms $G/L\to Q$ by matching isomorphic simple factors and independently specifying a map on each. For a simple factor the elementary two-generator enumeration computes all its isomorphisms in polynomial table time and finds at most the square of its order many maps. Finite nonabelian simple groups are two-generated. If $m_S$ is the multiplicity of simple type $S$ in $Q$, the total comparisons for these listings and lift tests are bounded by
$$
 N^{O(1)} \prod_S m_S!
$$
where $N$ bounds the group orders. Since $\sum_S m_S\le\log_2 N$, this is $N^{O(1+\log\log N)}$. It is polynomial whenever the product of the factorials is polynomial, for example when all multiplicities are bounded, even if the central rank grows. [Finite simple group generation](https://pages.uoregon.edu/kantor/PAPERS/gkfinal.pdf).

For each obtained map $\beta\colon G\to Q$, choose a section $s$ of $H_0\to Q$ by table lookups. Every lift is $g\mapsto u_g s(\beta(g))$ for $u_g\in Z(H_0)$. The multiplication equations are affine linear in the central variables $u_g$, with right sides given by the section's multiplication defects. Thus one obtains a lift, counts every lift, or proves none exists by Gaussian elimination. Any lift is surjective onto $H_0$: its image $I$ maps onto $Q$, hence $H_0=IZ(H_0)$, and then $[H_0,H_0]\subseteq I$. Perfection gives $I=H_0$. Each lift has exactly one quotient map, so summing the lift counts and multiplying by the independent abelian-factor count gives the exact number of epimorphisms. All arithmetic and explicit witness operations are polynomial per branch.

These are unconditional decision, search and counting bounds for the entire target family, including sources outside that family. The remaining polynomial-time clause for arbitrary repeated factors contains the length-parameter subcode equivalence problem identified in Section 1. The NP-completeness conclusion uses succinct central-kernel input, whereas the polynomial-time question concerns full multiplication tables.
