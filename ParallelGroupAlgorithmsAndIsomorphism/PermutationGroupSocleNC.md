# Computing the socle of a permutation group in parallel

**Theorem.** Given an unrestricted finite permutation group $G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(n)$ by a list of generating permutations, a deterministic uniform $\mathsf{NC}$ algorithm returns generators of $\operatorname{Soc}(G)$, the subgroup generated by all minimal nontrivial normal subgroups of $G$. It can return the abelian and nonabelian parts separately.

This answers the ordinary permutation-group case of the general socle question stated after Problem 12.4 by Levet, Srivastava and Thakkar. It also answers the Shonan Meeting 246 question about solvable primitive groups, and the stronger ordinary permutation-group question recorded immediately before it. The input and output here use permutations in the ambient faithful action. [Levet–Srivastava–Thakkar, §12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16039v4#S12) · [Shonan Meeting Report 246, printed p. 15](https://shonan.nii.ac.jp/docs/No.246.pdf#page=16)

## The normal series and its centralizers

Use the published $\mathsf{NC}$ normal-series algorithm to compute a series $1=N_0\leq N_1\leq\cdots\leq N_r=G$. Each $N_i$ is normal in $G$, $r$ is polylogarithmic, and each layer is abelian or a direct product of nonabelian simple groups. For the latter kind the algorithm supplies the preimage $T\leq N_i$ of each simple direct factor of $N_i/N_{i-1}$, given by generators. Use the standard permutation-group $\mathsf{NC}$ algorithms for order, normal-subgroup centralizers and intersections, and for the derived subgroup. The last is the normal closure of the pairwise commutators of a generating list. [Kantor–Luks–Mark, §§2.2 and 3.6, Lemmas 3.4 and 3.14](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~kantor/PAPERS/klmFINAL.pdf) · [Babai–Luks–Seress, Theorem 7.1](https://ix.cs.uoregon.edu/~luks/NC.pdf#page=9)

For each $i$, compute
$$
 C_i=N_i\cap C_G(N_{i-1}),\qquad K_i=C_i\cap N_{i-1}=Z(N_{i-1}),\qquad Q_i=C_iN_{i-1}/N_{i-1}.
$$
Both groups in the intersection defining $C_i$ are normal in $G$. The natural map $\pi_i:C_i\to Q_i$ is onto, and its kernel $K_i$ is central in $C_i$. The subgroup $Q_i$ is normal in the layer $N_i/N_{i-1}$. For any minimal normal $M$ of $G$, take the first $i$ with $M\subseteq N_i$. Minimality gives $M\cap N_{i-1}=1$, so $[M,N_{i-1}]=1$ and $M\subseteq C_i$. The restriction of $\pi_i$ to $M$ is injective.

## Nonabelian minimal normal subgroups

For each nonabelian layer and each simple direct factor $S=T/N_{i-1}$ of that layer, form $E=C_i\cap T$ and its derived subgroup $D=[E,E]$. Keep the generators of $D$ exactly when
$$
                    |D|\,|N_{i-1}|=|T|.
$$
All operations are in $\mathsf{NC}$. Indeed, $C_i$ is normal in $G$ so $T$ normalizes it, and the order test uses integers of $O(n\log n)$ bits.

To prove correctness, note that $\pi_i(E)=S\cap Q_i$ is either trivial or $S$, by simplicity and normality. In the trivial case $E\subseteq K_i$ is abelian, so the test discards it. Otherwise $D$ maps onto $S$, because $S$ is perfect. It follows that $E=DK_i$ and $D=[D,D]$ since $K_i\subseteq Z(E)$. The test says precisely that $D\to S$ is an isomorphism. Equivalently the central extension has a subgroup mapping isomorphically onto $S$: if $L\leq E$ is such a subgroup then $E=LK_i$ and $[E,E]=[L,L]=L$.

Conjugation by $G$ permutes the simple direct factors in the layer, preserving the order test and permuting the retained subgroups $D$. Two distinct retained factors commute. Their images commute, so their commutators lie in $K_i$. For a fixed element of one factor, taking a commutator with elements of the other is therefore a homomorphism into the abelian group $K_i$, which vanishes because the latter factor is perfect. The product of any set of retained subgroups is consequently a direct product, because it maps injectively to the product of the distinct image factors. Take an orbit of retained factors under $G$. Their product is a minimal normal subgroup of $G$, since any normal subgroup of a direct product of nonabelian simple groups is a subproduct and the conjugation action is transitive on the factors in the orbit. Thus all retained generators belong to the nonabelian socle.

Conversely every nonabelian minimal normal $M$ with first stage $i$ injects into the layer as a normal subgroup that is a product of nonabelian simple factors. A finite minimal normal subgroup is characteristically simple, so the image is nonabelian and contains no abelian simple factor. The layer is therefore nonabelian. Each simple direct factor in its image has an isomorphic preimage inside $M$, so the test keeps that lifted factor and the resulting factors generate $M$. Taking all retained groups at all stages gives precisely the subgroup generated by the nonabelian minimal normal subgroups of $G$.

## A normal abelian subgroup containing the abelian socle

Compute $A=\prod_{i=1}^{r}Z(C_i)$ by taking the union of the center-generating lists. Each center is normal in $G$. For $i<j$, $C_j$ centralizes $N_{j-1}$, which contains $Z(C_i)$, so $A$ is abelian. If $M$ is an abelian minimal normal subgroup with first stage $i$, its image is an abelian normal subgroup of the semisimple layer. Such subgroups are central, since their projections onto every nonabelian simple factor are trivial. It follows that $[M,C_i]\subseteq M\cap N_{i-1}=1$, so $M\subseteq Z(C_i)\subseteq A$.

Every abelian minimal normal subgroup is elementary abelian for some prime $p$. Any prime dividing the order of a permutation in $\operatorname{Sym}(n)$ is at most $n$. For each prime $p\leq n$, compute $B=A[p]=\{a\in A:a^p=1\}$ as the kernel of the homomorphism $A\to A$, $a\mapsto a^p$. This uses LIFT with the trivial quotient denominator, applied to the images of the supplied generators and the target identity. Powering uses balanced multiplication, and all primes at most $n$ can be recognized in parallel by testing trial divisors. [Kantor–Luks–Mark, Lemma 3.3](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~kantor/PAPERS/klmFINAL.pdf)

Regarding $B$ as a vector space over $\mathbb F_p$, its irreducible subspaces under the conjugation action of $G$ are precisely the minimal normal subgroups of $G$ lying in $B$. We describe both the coordinate computation and the extraction of their sum below.

### Coordinates for the conjugation module

Compute the orbits $O$ of $B$ on $[n]$ by graph connectivity under the generating permutations. The permutation group induced by $B$ on each orbit is abelian and transitive, hence regular. Indeed, an element of an abelian transitive group that fixes one point fixes every point, so the stabilizer in the induced faithful action is trivial. Write $B^O$ for this induced group. Its order is $|O|\leq n$, and the map $B\to\prod_O B^O$ is injective because the original permutation action is faithful.

For each orbit, enumerate the permutations in $B^O$ by repeated squaring of lists of distinct products, starting with the restricted generators, their inverses, and the identity. At each round multiply all pairs, mark the first copy of each duplicate, and compact the list by prefix sums. After $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ rounds the list is complete since shortest paths from the identity in a Cayley graph on at most $n$ vertices have length less than $n$. List size is at most $n$ after compaction, and one round has polynomial size and polylogarithmic depth. One may use identity padding with validity flags for smaller orbits. [Kantor–Luks–Mark, §7.2](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~kantor/PAPERS/klmFINAL.pdf)

Compute a basis of the elementary abelian group $B^O$ by retaining an element in list order exactly when it fails to belong to the subgroup generated by earlier list entries. These tests run in parallel, using reachability in the Cayley graph on the listed group elements, which has at most $n$ vertices. This yields some $d_O$ basis elements. Enumerate every coordinate vector in $\mathbb F_p^{d_O}$ and multiply the corresponding powers of basis elements to obtain a lookup table. There are exactly $|O|$ such coordinate vectors. Given a permutation in $B$, evaluate its image on the least point of $O$. Regularity identifies its restriction to $O$ uniquely, so the table gives its coordinates. Concatenating the coordinates embeds $B$ linearly in a space of dimension $\sum_O d_O\leq n\log_2 n$.

Read the images of the supplied generators of $B$ in this space. Use parallel prefix rank tests to retain a basis, remembering the corresponding permutations. To compute the conjugation action of a generating permutation of $G$, conjugate these basis permutations and read their orbit coordinates, then solve the linear systems expressing them in the $B$-basis. Inverses, products, powers, balanced prefix sums, and the indicated table searches are all deterministic uniform $\mathsf{NC}$. Use parallel rank and linear-system algorithms over $\mathbb F_p$. [Mulmuley, *A fast parallel algorithm to compute the rank of a matrix over an arbitrary field*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579205) · [Kantor–Luks–Mark, §3.4](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~kantor/PAPERS/klmFINAL.pdf)

### Extracting the sum of irreducible submodules

Let $d=\dim B$ and let $R\subseteq M_d(\mathbb F_p)$ be the unital algebra generated by the conjugation matrices. The case $d=0$ contributes nothing. In the other cases a basis of $R$ can be computed in $\mathsf{NC}$. Start with the span of the supplied matrices and the identity, then replace the current subspace by the span of all pairwise products of its basis matrices. After $j$ rounds this spans all words of length at most $2^j$, using the identity to pad shorter words. The spaces spanned by words of length at most $t$, for successive $t$, increase strictly until they give $R$, so length $d^2$ suffices. The number of rounds is $O(\log d)$, with every basis obtained by parallel rank.

Write $J=J(R)$ for the Jacobson radical. The sum of irreducible $R$-submodules of $B$ is exactly $\operatorname{Ann}_{B}(J)$. Indeed, $J$ annihilates each simple module. Conversely the space annihilated by $J$ is a module over the semisimple algebra $R/J$ and is thus a sum of simples. A subspace is invariant under the action of $G$ exactly when it is an $R$-submodule. Invariance under each generator implies invariance under its inverse by finite-dimensionality and invertibility. Once a basis for $J$ has been found, the common kernel is a linear system. Convert its basis back to permutations via products of powers of the remembered basis permutations of $B$.

Here are the details of the deterministic parallel radical computation, using the trace-coefficient idea of Cohen, Ivanyos and Wales. Put $I_0=R$. For $\ell=0,1,\dots,\lfloor\log_p d\rfloor$, form the matrix of the pairing
$$
 P_\ell(a,b)=[t^{p^\ell}]\det(I_d+t ab),\quad a,b\in I_\ell,
$$
using the current basis in each variable. Take $I_{\ell+1}$ to be the left kernel of this pairing. At this stage it is a bilinear pairing over $\mathbb F_p$, as the next argument shows. Each round needs only parallel matrix multiplication, characteristic-polynomial coefficients, and linear-system solving. One may execute $1+\lfloor\log_2 d\rfloor$ padded rounds and use threshold comparisons to stop the updates for $p^\ell>d$. [Cohen–Ivanyos–Wales, Algorithm 1 and §4.1](https://research.tue.nl/nl/publications/finding-the-radical-of-an-algebra-of-linear-transformations/) · [Berkowitz, *On computing the determinant in small parallel time using a small number of processors*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0190(84)90018-8)

For correctness extend scalars to an algebraic closure $k$ of $\mathbb F_p$. The radical extends to $J\otimes k$, since this is a nilpotent ideal and its quotient is a product of full matrix algebras over $k$ by separability of finite fields. Let $j$ index the simple factors of this split quotient, and write $a_j$ for the component of the image of $a$. Let $m_j$ be the multiplicity of its simple module in a composition series of $B\otimes k$. Each $m_j$ is positive. Otherwise the preimage of the absent factor would act as zero on every composition factor. A sufficiently long product of elements of that preimage would then act as zero on the entire module. Faithfulness would make that ideal nilpotent, despite its nonzero simple quotient. Each $m_j\leq d$. In a basis respecting the composition series, block triangularity gives
$$
 \det(I_d+t a)=\prod_j \det(I+t a_j)^{m_j}.
$$
Inductively, $I_\ell\otimes k$ is the preimage of the product of the factors with $p^\ell\mid m_j$. For $a,b$ in this preimage, the characteristic-$p$ power identity gives
$$
 [t^{p^\ell}]\det(I_d+t ab)=\sum_{v_p(m_j)=\ell}
   (m_j/p^\ell)\operatorname{tr}(a_jb_j)^{p^\ell}.
$$
Terms from other factors are constant through the required coefficient. The right side is Frobenius-semilinear in each variable over $k$ and bilinear on the original $\mathbb F_p$-space. Testing against the original field basis therefore suffices to test against every vector over $k$. Conversely the solution space over $k$ is the extension of the computed linear kernel, since after taking inverse Frobenius of the equations against those original basis vectors one obtains the same coefficients in $\mathbb F_p$. Testing against matrix units in each split component shows that the kernel removes precisely the factors with $v_p(m_j)=\ell$, by nondegeneracy of matrix trace pairing. This proves the induction. Once $p^\ell>d$, the preimage is exactly $J$.

Take the union of the resulting permutation generators over primes and the retained derived-subgroup generators over all stages. Every listed permutation belongs to the socle, and every minimal normal subgroup is generated by permutations represented in these lists. The number of independent branches is polynomial in the input size and the only explicitly repeated algebra steps have logarithmic iteration counts. All permutation arrays, coordinate matrices, and arithmetic integers have polynomial length. Thus the construction has polynomial size and polylogarithmic depth under logspace uniformity, using padded lists to keep dimensions fixed. For the DLOGTIME-uniform version, use the standard inclusion of logspace in DLOGTIME-uniform $\mathsf{NC}^2$, obtained by powering the configuration-graph adjacency matrix. A single logspace-uniform wiring predicate can thus be evaluated for every possible pair of gates in parallel by DLOGTIME-uniform circuits. Then evaluate all gates simultaneously in each of a polylogarithmic number of rounds using those wiring bits, with each gate using values from the preceding round. An acyclic circuit of depth $D$ stabilizes by round $D$, so this yields DLOGTIME-uniform circuits of polynomial size and polylogarithmic depth even when a cited algorithm uses logspace uniformity.

## Scope and provenance

The theorem uses the normal-subgroup centralizer algorithm in an actual permutation group. It makes no assumption that the same operation works in a quotient of a permutation group. The broader quotient-model problem in Problem 12.4 remains distinct. The two new assemblies above isolate the exact central extensions that contribute nonabelian factors and put the abelian factors in a single module from which its simple part can be computed. Cannon and Holt use a simple-factor derived-subgroup test on a central extension in their sequential imprimitive case. Kantor and Luks recover the abelian socle via simple submodules of a normal elementary abelian subgroup in their sequential algorithm. The construction here places these ideas over a single parallel-computable normal series, with a uniform parallel coordinate and radical computation. The parallel Fitting-free case is in the question's source. [Cannon–Holt, §5](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/16352/) · [Kantor–Luks, §9](https://pages.uoregon.edu/kantor/PAPERS/quotient.pdf) The proof is informal. A targeted priority check based on the primary references and their follow-ups did not establish earlier publication of this general ordinary-permutation result.
