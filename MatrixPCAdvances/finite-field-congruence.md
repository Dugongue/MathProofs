# Degree and sparse size for arithmetic noncongruence

Fix an odd finite field \(F=\mathbb F_Q\). For arbitrary labelled \(N\times N\) matrix tuples \(A,B\), use the equations \(XA_iX^{\mathsf T}=B_i,\ XY=YX=I\), and the field equations for all coordinates of \(X,Y\). Every noncongruent pair over \(F\) has an ordinary PC refutation of degree at most \(QN\). For each fixed \(F\), the worst-case degree is \(\Theta_F(N)\), and some instances require \(2^{\Omega_F(N)}\) sparse size. This lower bound holds even for single nonsingular symmetric matrices that become congruent over the algebraic closure. Sparse size counts nonzero monomial occurrences.

## Upper bound for an arbitrary tuple

Make the two-vertex representations with arrows \(A_i,A_i^{\mathsf T}\) and similarly for \(B\), each arrow pointing from the first vertex to the second. The duality exchanges the vertices, dualizes the spaces and swaps the two arrows for each \(i\). Each representation is canonically self-dual. Transpose and multiply the congruence lines on the right by \(Y^{\mathsf T}\), using the inverse lines, to derive the forward arrow equations for the morphism with vertex maps \(Y^{\mathsf T},X\) in degree 3. The maps \(X^{\mathsf T},Y\) give the reverse morphism, either by the same transpose pairing or by applying inverse equations to the forward arrow equations. For the reverse equations one can simply transpose the forward equations for the paired arrows, keeping degree 3. These two morphisms are inverse and adjoint to each other in the original congruence equations.

If the representations are not isomorphic, read the opposite multiplicity corners of their semisimple endomorphism quotient after a splitting extension. A missing type gives degree 3. Every remaining differing type gives a rectangular inverse system of degree at most the smaller multiplicity plus 2, by descending minors. The smaller multiplicity is at most \(N\), since an indecomposable has a nonzero space at some vertex. The resulting degree is at most \(QN\), and the proof descends by scalar extension for bounded PC spaces. Over \(F\), isomorphism of these representations is equivalent to geometric isomorphism. The finite semisimple quotient splits with the same multiplicities in each constituent factor. [Quiver degree and quotient readout](quiver-conjugacy.md)

Suppose now that an isomorphism \(\phi\) between the two representations exists over \(F\). Use the source endomorphism algebra \(E\), with involution \(*\) induced by the canonical self-duality. For a morphism between source and target, denote its adjoint by \(\dagger\). Put \(h=\phi^\dagger\phi\in E\), an invertible self-adjoint element. An isometric identification exists precisely when \(g^*g=h\) for some \(g\in E^\times\). In one direction, if \(T\) is an isometric identification take \(g=T^{-1}\phi\). In the other take \(T=\phi g^{-1}\).

This is also a degree-3 readout in PC. Linearly project the formal reverse map onto its reverse intertwiner space over \(F\), writing the projected map as \(R\). The difference is in the row span of the derived linear reverse equations. The formal reverse map before projection has adjoint equal to the formal forward map, so \(R^\dagger R=I\) follows in degree 3 by the inverse axioms and linear replacement. In this identity the identity acts on the target representation. Thus \(L=R\phi\) is an \(E\)-valued linear polynomial and \(L^*L=h\) follows in degree 3.

Let \(J=\operatorname{rad}E\). It is invariant under \(*\). If \(h\) is a norm modulo \(J\), choose a unit \(g_0\) lifting a quotient solution. Then \((g_0^{-1})^* h g_0^{-1}=1+j\) with \(j\in J\) self-adjoint. The polynomial square root of \(1+j\) exists over \(F\), by solving the successive coefficients in the nilpotent variable \(j\) using division by 2. It is self-adjoint, and multiplying it on the right by \(g_0\) gives an actual norm solution. Consequently some orbit of simple factors of \(E/J\) has a norm obstruction for a noncongruent pair. Exchanged pairs have surjective norm on self-adjoint units: in exchange coordinates with an anti-isomorphism \(\tau\), a self-adjoint pair has form \((c,\tau(c))\) and is the norm of \((c,1)\). A fixed simple factor is a matrix algebra over a finite field and its involution is adjoint to a symmetric, alternating or Hermitian form. For alternating and Hermitian forms, all nondegenerate forms of the given kind and dimension over the same field are isometric. A norm obstruction therefore occurs in a fixed symmetric factor over some finite extension \(k/F\). There is a nonsingular symmetric \(H\) giving the adjoint, and \(H\overline h\) and \(H\) have determinant ratio \(c\) nonsquare in \(k\). [Finite-field involutions and their forms, Ivanyos–Qiao §§3.1, 3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1708.03495#S3.SS5)

For this factor \(M_m(k)\), let \(f=[k:F]\) and \(t=mf\). Each vertex space is an \(E\)-module of dimension \(N\), and their sum is faithful. Lift the chosen factor's identity idempotent (zero in the other factors) across the nilpotent radical. This idempotent acts nontrivially on at least one vertex. If the corresponding simple module were absent from a composition series at that vertex, the idempotent would send each step into the preceding one. Its action would be nilpotent and idempotent, hence zero, a contradiction. The simple module of dimension \(t\) therefore occurs at that vertex, giving \(t\le N\).

The quotient gives the identity \(\overline L^{\mathsf T} H\overline L=H\overline h\) in degree 3. Apply each of the \(f\) coefficient embeddings of \(k\) over \(F\). Determinant expansion and telescoping replacement yield the conjugate equations \(\det(\overline L)^{2}=\sigma(c)\) with the determinant on the left taken in its conjugate coefficients, in degree at most \(\max(3,2m)\). Multiply the conjugate identities by telescoping, so that
\[
D^2=c_0,\qquad D=\mathrm{N}_{k/F}(\det\overline L),\qquad c_0=\mathrm{N}_{k/F}(c)
\]
is derivable in degree at most \(\max(3,2t)\). Norm of a polynomial here means product of coefficient conjugates with the PC variables held fixed. It has coefficients in \(F\) and degree at most \(t\). A generator of \(k^\times\) maps to a generator of \(F^\times\), so \(c_0\) is nonsquare.

From the coordinate field equations, expand \(D^Q-D\) using Frobenius in degree at most \(Qt\). For each monomial this expansion is a telescoping difference of products in which each coordinate's \(Q\)-th power is replaced by that coordinate. Each coefficient is fixed by Frobenius. The equation \(D^2=c_0\) gives \(D^Q-c_0^{(Q-1)/2}D=0\) at the same cap, by successively multiplying by monomials in \(D\) and taking linear combinations. Because \(c_0^{(Q-1)/2}=-1\), the two lines give \(D=0\), then \(1=0\). Every polynomial multiplier in these steps expands into ordinary variable multiplications and linear combinations within the displayed cap. Any uses of \(k\)-coefficients descend to \(F\) by fixed-degree scalar extension. The resulting degree is at most \(QN\).

## Hard nonsingular symmetric examples

Take a nonsquare \(\delta\in F^\times\). For a power \(q=p^j\) of the characteristic set \(a=4q,\ b=3q\), and use
\[
A=\operatorname{diag}(I_a,\delta I_b),\qquad
B=\operatorname{diag}(I_b,\delta I_a).
\]
They are nonsingular, become congruent over an algebraic closure by diagonal scaling, and have determinant ratio \(\delta^{a-b}=\delta^q\), a nonsquare. Let \(Z\) be the \(a\times b\) matrix of Boolean board variables. Send \(X\) to \(\operatorname{diag}(Z^{\mathsf T},Z)\) with the source partition \(a,b\) and target partition \(b,a\), and send \(Y\) to its transpose. The two Gram identities yielded by the substitution follow in degree 2 from matching axioms. The congruence line uses these Gram identities, with the second multiplied by \(\delta\). Every field equation maps to zero or follows from the Boolean equation for one variable within degree \(Q\).

Thus a refutation transfers to the \(4q\times3q\) Boolean matching equations without increasing degree, and with at most polynomial additive sparse-size overhead, since each coordinate maps to a variable, 0 or 1. The existing restriction argument for that board gives size at least \((6/5)^{q-1}\), and Riis's design lower bound gives degree at least \(q\). Pad both matrices by the same identity block and the substitution by the identity to cover every large \(N\), taking the largest \(p\)-power with \(7q\le N\). Then \(q>N/(7p)\). [Sparse-size restriction proof and substitutions](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/TensorSparsePCBounds/Proof.md) · [Riis, Lemma 14.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07280v1#S14)

This establishes sharp worst-case degree and an exponential sparse-size lower bound for the finite-field version of the congruence equations in [GGPS Open Question 7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19320#S7.SS3), in every fixed odd finite field. The unrestricted ordinary-PC system for the hard examples is satisfiable over the algebraic closure.
