# Determinant extraction from every equation of the matrix multiplication orbit closure

Work over the complex numbers. Let $M_n$ denote the matrix multiplication tensor, the trilinear form $(A,B,C)\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(ABC)$ on three matrix spaces of dimension $m=n^2$. Take the Zariski closure of its orbit under independent invertible changes of coordinates in the three modes.

**Theorem.** Let $f$ be any nonzero polynomial of degree $D$ in the $m^3$ ambient tensor entries which vanishes on that orbit closure. There is a constant-depth, division-free $f$-evaluation-oracle circuit of size $\operatorname{poly}(n,D)$ that **exactly computes** the generic determinant of dimension $\Theta(n^{1/3})$. There is a constant-depth border circuit of size $\operatorname{poly}(n)$ for the same asymptotic determinant dimension, with no upper bound on $D$. Both statements are nonuniform. The border circuit outputs a polynomial expression whose coefficientwise limit is the determinant.

In particular, no family of nonzero equations for these orbit closures for all sufficiently large $n$ has polynomial-size constant-depth ordinary arithmetic circuits. An ordinary circuit with those size and depth bounds has polynomial degree when wires and gates are charged. Composing it with the exact reduction would give polynomial-size constant-depth circuits for polynomially growing determinants, contrary to the known lower bounds. The determinant inherits the iterated-matrix-product lower bound via the usual branching-program projection. The orbit closures have nonzero equations for every $n\ge2$, since the ambient dimension $n^6$ exceeds the group dimension $3n^4$. [Forbes, Theorem 10](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.CCC.2024.31)

## The reduction

1. Every tensor supported on outer-mode subspaces of dimension at most $n$, with the entire middle $m$-space allowed, lies in the orbit closure. To see this in trilinear coordinates, such a form is $a(x)^{\mathsf T}B(y)c(z)$, where $a,c$ are linear maps into $\mathbb C^n$ and $B$ is a matrix of linear forms. For the first coordinate vector $e$, substitute $A=e a(x)^{\mathsf T}$, the matrix $B(y)$ and $C=c(z)e^{\mathsf T}$ into $\operatorname{tr}(ABC)$. All three substitutions are linear maps of $m$-dimensional spaces. Singular maps are allowed in the orbit closure because each is a limit of invertible maps. Equivalently the image of any triple of endomorphisms belongs to the closure by density of the invertible triples.

2. Start with **one** generic $(m+m^2)$-by-$m$ matrix $Y$. Its top $m$ rows form $X$; reshape the rest as an arbitrary tensor $T$. Form

   $$\Phi(Y)=(X\otimes I\otimes X)T,\qquad
   \Phi(Y)_{ijk}=\sum_{a,c=1}^{m}X_{ia} T_{ajc} X_{kc}.$$

   The map has cubic coordinates, constant arithmetic depth, and $O(m^5)$ wires. It has an affine right inverse given by prescribing $X=I$ and $T$ freely. Consequently $g=f\circ\Phi$ is nonzero, with degree at most $3D$. If $\operatorname{rank}Y\le n$, then $\operatorname{rank}X\le n$, and both outer supports lie in $\operatorname{im}X$. Step 1 gives $g(Y)=0$.

3. The vanishing ideal of the generic matrices of rank at most $n$ is generated by the $(n+1)$-minors. Thus $g$ satisfies the ordinary rectangular determinantal extraction hypotheses. Dey and Guo's exact theorem gives a depth-three $g$-oracle circuit of size polynomial in both matrix dimensions and $\deg g$, computing a determinant of dimension $\Theta((n+1)^{1/3})$. Andrews and Forbes give the border counterpart of size polynomial in the matrix dimensions, independently of the degree. Replace each $g$ call by $\Phi$ followed by an $f$ call. Coordinates are polynomials in the inputs, so the simulation preserves the exact rational expression in the border parameter before taking its limit. This proves the theorem. [Dey and Guo, Theorem 6 and Corollary 46](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2026.49) · [Andrews and Forbes, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00792)

There is a smaller nonuniform matrix version. Choose a tensor $T_0$ with $f(T_0)\ne0$ and use only $X$, with $T=T_0$ in the display. The resulting quadratic pullback is nonzero at $X=I$ and vanishes at every matrix of rank at most $n$. The cubic version constructs its substitution without choosing any advice about $f$. Dey and Guo's stronger Theorem 45 actually extracts every polynomial computed by an algebraic branching program on at most $n+1$ vertices, with size polynomial also in its number of input variables. This follows by applying that theorem directly in Step 3.

## Two consequences of the same proof

**Equations for low tensor rank.** Fix order $d\ge2$ and let $f\ne0$ vanish on every tensor of rank at most $R$ in $(\mathbb C^m)^{\otimes d}$. Equivalently it vanishes on their Zariski closure, the border-rank variety. Set $k=\lfloor R^{1/(d-1)}\rfloor$. The nonzero-equation hypothesis implies $R<m^{d-1}$, since every tensor is a sum of at most $m^{d-1}$ pure tensors. Stack a generic $m$-square $X$ atop a tensor $T$ reshaped into $m^{d-1}$ more rows. Apply $X$ to the first $d-1$ modes and the identity to the last. Again $X=I$ is a section. On total matrix rank at most $k$, the first $d-1$ supports have dimensions at most $k$. Expanding in one basis in each of these modes gives at most $k^{d-1}$ pure tensors; the remaining vector is simply the coefficient in the last mode. Thus the pullback belongs to the $(k+1)$-minor ideal. For fixed $d$, the map has degree $d$ and polynomial size in the explicit ambient size. The same two published extraction results yield a determinant of dimension $\Theta((k+1)^{1/3})$, with polynomial exact complexity in the ambient size and $D$, or polynomial border complexity in the ambient size. For tensors of order three the exponent in $R$ is $1/6$. This works for every equation, regardless of which generators or flattenings were used to find it.

**Symmetric and ladder determinantal ideals.** Dey and Guo single out symmetric determinantal ideals as an expected extension in §4, item 3. Let $S$ be a generic symmetric $m$-square matrix, and let $f$ be a nonzero element of the ideal generated by its $r$-minors. Pull back along $X\mapsto X+X^{\mathsf T}$. This linear map has section $S\mapsto S/2$. If $\operatorname{rank}X<\lceil r/2\rceil$, its symmetric part has rank at most $2\operatorname{rank}X<r$, so the pullback lies in the ordinary $\lceil r/2\rceil$-minor ideal. Its degree is unchanged. Exact determinant extraction thus retains dimension $\Theta(r^{1/3})$ and polynomial size in $m,D$; border size is polynomial in $m$. In the source depth-three constructions the oracle arguments are affine; composing affine maps preserves that depth. The extracted generic determinant also specializes to the symmetric determinant of the same size. Alternatively $X\mapsto XX^{\mathsf T}$ preserves the full rank threshold, is onto complex symmetric matrices by congruence diagonalization and square roots, and has quadratic entries.

For any ladder, or any coordinate subset of an ordinary generic matrix, a nonzero equation in the ideal generated by supported minors of sizes at least $r$ embeds into the full polynomial ring in all entries. Its image lies in the ordinary $r$-minor ideal by Laplace expansion. The oracle receives only the coordinates it uses. This proves the same extraction with full parameter $r$, also for mixed sizes with $r$ their minimum. Applying linear symmetrization after adjoining missing symmetric entries gives the symmetric-pattern extension. These assertions concern ideals generated by the indicated minors; more general varieties of complexes and quiver varieties require their own maps. [Dey and Guo, open directions](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol362-itcs2026/html/LIPIcs.ITCS.2026.49/LIPIcs.ITCS.2026.49.html#S4)

The factor two in the linear symmetrization bound is optimal as a global bound on relative rank inflation. For a linear map $L$ from a rectangular matrix space into symmetric matrices, suppose $L$ sends every rank-one input to rank at most one. A subspace of symmetric rank-at-most-one matrices has dimension at most one: independent nonzero symmetric matrices of rank one have different image lines, and their sum has rank two. For fixed $v$, the subspace of values $L(uv^{\mathsf T})$ as $u$ varies therefore has dimension at most one. If two such nonzero image lines for $v_1,v_2$ were different, choose $u$ outside the kernels of the two nonzero linear maps. The values at $uv_1^{\mathsf T}$ and $uv_2^{\mathsf T}$ would then be independent. But varying $v$ with this $u$ fixed also yields a subspace of symmetric rank-at-most-one matrices. This contradiction places all the images on one line. Rank-one matrices span the domain, so the entire image has dimension at most one. No linear surjection onto a symmetric matrix space of size at least two can have inflation one.

The variety of all composable matrix pairs $(A,B)$ satisfying $BA=0$ illustrates a limit of the extension. A single coordinate $(BA)_{ij}$ is a nonzero equation for this variety. On square matrices of order $m$ it is a sum of $m$ products and has depth two. Composing with this explicit circuit rules out extracting a determinant of dimension $m^{\Omega(1)}$ in polynomial size and constant depth from *every* nonzero equation for this variety over the complex numbers. Dey and Guo discuss the broader varieties as a direction; they do not state this stronger universal assertion.

## Relationship to earlier work

The determinantal extraction and low-depth lower bounds are published results. The entries of the new substitutions are small circuits and preserve low matrix rank in precisely the sense required by those theorems. Algebraically natural proofs and succinct hitting sets already relate the complexity of defining equations to the classes they describe. Existing work also connects matrix multiplication to PIT and studies conditional proof-complexity hardness of tensor rank principles. Here the object being extracted from is a single arbitrary nonzero equation for a specified tensor orbit closure or secant variety, and the output is an actual generic determinant. Publication priority of these particular transfers has not been established. They concern the equations' arithmetic complexity and give no new bound on the matrix multiplication exponent or on deciding orbit membership.
