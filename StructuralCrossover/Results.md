# Determinant extraction, field extensions, and sharp isomorphism probe bounds

Four informal proofs are collected here. The algebraic complexity result and the quiver result address different roles of the determinant. One extracts it as a polynomial from an equation for a tensor variety. The other computes the exact field extension required to enforce determinant-one basis changes. The other two results determine how much precision homomorphism counts can need on concrete isomorphism problems. Publication priority for these combinations remains unconfirmed.

## Every equation for matrix multiplication yields a determinant

Over the complex numbers, let $f$ be any nonzero polynomial vanishing on the orbit closure of the $n$-by-$n$ matrix multiplication tensor under independent basis changes. A constant-depth circuit of size polynomial in $n$ and $\deg f$, with evaluation gates for $f$, computes the generic determinant of dimension $\Theta(n^{1/3})$ exactly. The proof writes $a^\mathsf{T}Bc$ as a trace, which puts every tensor with two outer supports of dimension at most $n$ in the orbit closure. Compressing both modes with the same matrix converts $f$ into a nonzero equation for ordinary low-rank matrices. Published extraction theorems then apply. The exact version also computes every polynomial given by an algebraic branching program with at most $n+1$ vertices, with circuit size polynomial also in the number of input variables. A separate linear substitution gives the symmetric determinantal-ideal extension explicitly raised by Dey and Guo, over the complex numbers. [Proof](Algebra.md) · [Dey and Guo](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2026.49) · [Andrews and Forbes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00792)

## Exactly when determinant-one quiver isomorphisms appear

For two labeled quiver representations over fixed $\mathbb F_q$, constrain the determinants at any specified vertices. From the raw arrow matrices one can compute in deterministic polynomial time whether a permitted isomorphism ever exists over a finite extension, and, if so, the exact least degree $M$. The admissible degrees are all multiples of $M$. Exactly the positive integers supported on primes dividing $q-1$ occur as $M$. Already over $\mathbb F_5$ an acyclic chain of total dimension $O(b)$, with vertex dimensions at most three, can need degree $2^b$. For fixed $q>2$ and exactly $r$ constrained vertices, the worst least degree under a total dimension cap $s$ is $\Theta_{q,r}(s^r)$. The proof combines radical layers, norm surjectivity and Smith normal form, then builds extremal examples with linked Jordan blocks. This class has labeled arrows; general tensor isomorphism also permits a change of arrow coordinates. [Proof](Transporters.md)

## Reversing a group probe changes its exact precision

Start with Wilson's nonisomorphic groups sharing all proper subgroup and proper quotient types with multiplicities. Adjoin the same elementary abelian direct factor to all of them. On this family the number of base-$p$ digits that a Hom count must return is quadratic in $\log |G|$ for maps *into* the hidden group and linear for maps *out of* it, even with unrestricted probes. For a concrete family of at least twelve groups of order $3^{30}$ the first distinguishing moduli are $3^{232}$ and $3^{64}$. Both exponents are exact on every distinct pair. The extra factor measures abelianization dimension in one direction and central torsion dimension in the other. [Proof](Groups.md) · [Wilson](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v015a019/)

## A prime exactly at the degree bound changes the graph precision regime

Fix a maximum degree $\Delta\ge3$ for connected uncolored targets of common known order $n\le N$. Allow Hom counts modulo any product of powers of primes in a fixed nonempty set $S$. Count the largest answer width, allowing unrestricted probes and queries. If $k$ primes in $S$ are at least $\Delta$, then for $k>0$ the optimum is $(\log_2 N)/k+O_{\Delta,S}(1)$ bits. When $k=0$ it is $\Theta_{\Delta,S}(N)$. Matching lower bounds already occur at bounded pathwidth. Coset trees keep the degree below the required threshold even when protecting against several primes at once. A direct check on five smaller uncolored examples, including the two orders of the coset chain for $r=6$, is included.
