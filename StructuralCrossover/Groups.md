# Reversing a homomorphism query changes its critical precision from quadratic to linear

For a hidden finite group $U$, an incoming probe $X$ reads $|\operatorname{Hom}(X,U)|$ modulo a chosen positive integer. An outgoing probe reads $|\operatorname{Hom}(U,X)|$. Probe groups are unrestricted. The critical $p$-adic precision of a pair is the smallest $k\ge1$ for which some probe distinguishes the pair modulo $p^k$. Thus a difference divisible by $p^v$ but not by $p^{v+1}$ first becomes visible at precision $v+1$. Query number, group order of probes, and evaluation time are uncharged.

**Theorem.** Fix an odd prime $p$ and a sufficiently large prime $e>3$ different from $p$. For every $t\ge0$ there is a family of at least $p^{e-3}/e$ pairwise nonisomorphic class-two exponent-$p$ groups of common order $p^{2e+2+t}$. On **every pair** of distinct members the critical precisions are

| Direction | First distinguishing exponent |
|---|---|
| Incoming | $2et+5e+1$ |
| Outgoing | $2t+5e+1$ |

In both directions there is a distinguishing probe of order $p^{2e+2}$ at the stated precision. Every smaller probe agrees even on the exact integer counts. At $t=2e$, the thresholds are $4e^2+5e+1$ and $9e+1$. Thus the choice of direction costs a quadratic versus linear number of digits in the logarithm of the target order on exactly the same inputs. For example, $p=3,e=7,t=14$ gives at least twelve types of order $3^{30}$, with respective thresholds 232 and 64.

### The integral identity behind both directions

Suppose two nonisomorphic finite groups $G,H$ of equal order have identical multisets of proper subgroup types and identical multisets of proper quotient types, the latter counted once per kernel. Let $E=(C_p)^t$. Write $r_p(X)=\dim_{\mathbb F_p}X/(X'X^p)$, where $X^p$ is the subgroup generated by $p$th powers. Assume $r_p(G)=r_p(H)=r$. Assume also that the subgroups of central elements annihilated by $p$ in $G,H$ both have dimension $z$ over $\mathbb F_p$. Put $a=\gcd(|\operatorname{Aut}G|,|\operatorname{Aut}H|)$. For the pair $G\times E,H\times E$, take gcds of all the count differences, independently in the two directions. Then

$$D_{\rm in}=p^{rt}a,\qquad D_{\rm out}=p^{zt}a.$$

To prove the incoming formula, write $q_X(S)$ for the number of normal subgroups of $X$ whose quotient is isomorphic to $S$. Each map $X\to G$ has an image subgroup. Maps onto a specified subgroup $U$ number $q_X(U)|\operatorname{Aut}U|$. The contributions from all proper subgroups therefore cancel against those for $H$. Taking the direct product with $E$ multiplies each count by $p^{t r_p(X)}$. The full incoming difference equals

$$p^{t r_p(X)}\bigl(q_X(G)|\operatorname{Aut}G|-q_X(H)|\operatorname{Aut}H|\bigr).$$

A nonzero summand requires a quotient $G$ or $H$, which forces $r_p(X)\ge r$. At $X=G$ the difference is exactly $p^{rt}|\operatorname{Aut}G|$; at $X=H$ it is $-p^{rt}|\operatorname{Aut}H|$. This proves the first gcd and proves nonisomorphism of the padded groups.

For outgoing queries a homomorphism $G\times E\to X$ is precisely a map $f:G\to X$ and a map $E\to C_X(f(G))$. Sort by $S=\operatorname{im}f$, ranging over actual subgroups of $X$. The contribution for $S$ is

$$q_G(S)|\operatorname{Aut}S|\ |\operatorname{Hom}(E,C_X(S))|.$$

Every term coming from a proper quotient of $G$ cancels against the corresponding term for $H$, including its centralizer weight, which depends only on that actual subgroup of $X$. The survivors have $S\simeq G$ or $S\simeq H$. The $p$-torsion subgroup of $Z(S)$ is central in $C_X(S)$: every element of the centralizer commutes with every element of $S$. Pointwise multiplication by maps from $E$ to that subgroup gives a free action of a group of order $p^{zt}$ on $\operatorname{Hom}(E,C_X(S))$. Thus every surviving term is divisible by $p^{zt}a$. For $X=G$ the sole full-order image is $G$ itself, with weight $|\operatorname{Hom}(E,Z(G))|=p^{zt}$. The analogous calculation at $H$ gives the other multiple that realizes the gcd. In either direction a probe of order less than $|G|$ has no possible full image or quotient, so its exact count difference is zero.

### The Wilson groups and the exact automorphism valuation

Use the groups in Wilson's Theorem 1.5. They are quotients $G$ of the three-dimensional Heisenberg group over $\mathbb F_q$, $q=p^e$, by a codimension-two $\mathbb F_p$-subspace of its central coordinate. The theorem supplies at least $p^{e-3}/e$ types and equality of both proper multisets. Their abelianization is $\mathbb F_q^2$ and their center equals their commutator subgroup, of order $p^2$. Indeed the underlying commutator on $\mathbb F_q^2$ before central quotient is the determinant. For each nonzero vector this maps the other vector onto all of $\mathbb F_q$. Thus no new vector becomes central after taking the quotient. The groups have exponent $p$ since $p$ is odd. This gives $r=2e$ and $z=2$. [Wilson, Theorem 1.5, Proposition 3.3 and Section 4](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v015a019/v015a019.pdf)

It remains to show $v_p(|\operatorname{Aut}G|)=5e$ throughout the family. Take the action on the abelianization $V=\mathbb F_q^2$. An automorphism in its kernel fixes commutators pointwise, hence fixes the entire center $W$ of order $p^2$. Writing its action as $g\mapsto g f(g)$ with $f(g)\in W$, centrality makes $f$ a homomorphism factoring through $V$. Conversely every linear $f:V\to W$ gives an automorphism of this form, with inverse furnished by $-f$. The kernel has order $p^{4e}$.

Wilson's lifting theorem (Theorem 4.1) lifts every automorphism of this genus-two quotient to the original Heisenberg group. His formula for the action there (Theorem 4.24) places the image on $V$ inside $\Gamma L_2(q)$. It contains $SL_2(q)$: determinant-one matrices act on the original group fixing the center pointwise and descend through every chosen central subspace (Section 5, proof using Lemma 5.4). The $p$-parts of $|SL_2(q)|$ and $|\Gamma L_2(q)|$ are both $q$, since the field automorphism group has order $e$ prime to $p$. The image thus has valuation $e$. Adding the kernel gives $5e$. The two critical precisions now follow from the integral identity and the two explicit probes. [Wilson, Theorems 4.1 and 4.24](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v015a019/v015a019.pdf)

### Worst case for all finite groups

For equal-order groups of order at most $N$, the optimal worst-case incoming width with powers of a fixed odd $p$ is $\Theta_p((\log N)^2)$ bits. This lower bound already holds in class two and exponent $p$, with an elementary abelian direct factor. For every sufficiently large $N$, Bertrand's postulate supplies a prime $e$ of order $\log_p N$ with $4e+2\le\log_p N$ and $e\ne p$, so the explicit subsequence with $t=2e$ gives the claimed order-cap lower bound.

For the upper bound, fix nonisomorphic $G,H$ of equal order. A group of order $n$ has a generating set of size at most $\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ by successively adjoining a generator outside the current subgroup. Its automorphism group has at most $n^{\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor}$ elements. The count of injections from $G$ to $G$ is $|\operatorname{Aut}G|$, and that from $G$ to $H$ is zero. Injection counts from $G$ are integral linear combinations of Hom counts from quotients of $G$: subtract smaller quotient contributions inductively in the identity that sorts maps by their kernels. Therefore some probe of order at most $n$ separates the groups modulo $p^{1+v_p(|\operatorname{Aut}G|)}$. A universal modulus of width $O((\log N)^2)$ bits suffices; all probes up to order $N$ form a finite procedure. This upper bound holds for $p=2$ as well. The Wilson construction here uses odd characteristic.

If arbitrary moduli may be tried, the optimal worst-case incoming width over all equal-order finite groups is $\Theta(\log\log N)$ bits. For the upper bound, the product of primes through $C(\log N)^2$ exceeds the automorphism bound above, for a sufficiently large absolute $C$, by Chebyshev's estimates. At least one of these primes therefore distinguishes each pair by the injection argument. Enumerate all such primes and probes up to order $N$; the prime may differ between pairs of the same order.

For the lower bound take $L=\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ldots,s)$, so $\log L=O(s)$. Linnik's theorem gives a prime $\ell\equiv1\bmod L$ with $\ell\le C L^A$ for absolute $A,C$. The abelian groups $C_{\ell^2}$ and $C_\ell\times C_\ell$ are nonisomorphic and equal in order. Every Hom count into either target is an $\ell$-power, since each Hom set is itself an abelian $\ell$-group. Thus every answer modulo every integer from 2 to $s$ is 1 for both targets, even with adaptive queries. For a sufficiently small constant $c$, taking $s=\lfloor c\log N\rfloor$ keeps $\ell^2\le N$ and forces width at least $\log_2\log N-O(1)$. [Xylouris on Linnik's theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/0906.2749)

### Scope and provenance

Wilson proved the original indistinguishable families and developed efficient isomorphism tests for the unpadded cores. The claims here concern divisibility of arbitrary probe counts, exact directional thresholds after a common elementary abelian factor, and worst-case measurement precision. They impose no computational lower bound on algorithms that read a Cayley table. Quotient–kernel correspondence, the abelianization universal property and centralizers provide the library connections. The arguments are informal. Priority for the precision results has not been established.
