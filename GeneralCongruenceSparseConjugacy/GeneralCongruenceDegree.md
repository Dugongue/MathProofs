# Minimum Polynomial Calculus degree for general matrix congruence in characteristic different from two

Let $A,B$ be square matrices of the same positive side over a field $F$ of characteristic different from two. Use the literal equations $XAX^T=B$, $XY=YX=I$, with independent entries of $X,Y$ and no field axioms. Degree means the largest total degree of an actual proof line in ordinary Polynomial Calculus (PC). The theorem computes the minimum degree for each individual geometrically negative pair, including singular and nonsymmetric matrices. [Galesi, Grochow, Pitassi and She, Open Question 7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19320#S7.SS3)

Compute the congruence canonical forms over an algebraic closure $K$. For each distinct canonical congruence block $C$ with unequal multiplicities let $r_C<s_C$ be those multiplicities in increasing order. Count copies of the whole congruence block, even when its associated matrix pencil has two copies of one indecomposable. Blocks can be taken to be $J_m(0)$, a cosquare root of $J_m((-1)^{m+1})$, and $[J_m(\lambda)\backslash I_m]$ for $\lambda\ne0,(-1)^{m+1}$ up to inversion. The last notation denotes a matrix with zero diagonal blocks and the given two off-diagonal blocks. Distinct canonical lists mean geometric noncongruence. [Horn and Sergeichuk, Theorem 2.1(a)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0709.2408)

Set $\delta(0,s)=0$. At $0<r<s$ set $\delta(r,s)=2$ in characteristic zero and
$$
 \delta(r,s)=\min\{r+2,\ 2p^{v_p(s-r)}\}
$$
in characteristic $p>2$. The minimum degree is as follows.

1. Equal canonical lists give a solution over $K$, hence no refutation over $F$.
2. A nonzero constant entry among the original axioms gives degree zero. These cases are $A=0$, $B\ne0$, and alternating $A$ with some $B_{ii}\ne0$.
3. If $A$ is nonzero symmetric and $B$ is nonsymmetric, or if $A$ is nonzero alternating and $B$ is nonalternating, the degree is two after the degree-zero cases above.
4. Every other geometrically negative pair has degree $\max(3,\min_C\delta(r_C,s_C))$.

In particular all characteristic-zero instances with refutations have degree at most three. The degree-zero test is made in the input coordinates. For example a target with vanishing diagonal can exhibit a nonzero diagonal after a basis change. This can change degree zero to degree two when transporting a proof back to the original equations.

## Linear preparation in the original variables

Field extension preserves and reflects the existence of an ordinary degree-bounded PC proof. In the vector space of polynomials of degree at most $d$, iterate linear span, intersection with degree at most $d-1$, and multiplication by one variable on the latter part. These vector-space operations commute with scalar extension. Thus work over $K$. An invertible constant congruence change on each input effects invertible linear substitutions on $X,Y$ and replaces each axiom by a linear combination of the corresponding axioms. This preserves existence in every degree bound $d\ge2$. For the small exception, transforming a degree-zero axiom back can require combining degree-two lines.

Represent a matrix by the two-arrow quiver representation $R_A=(A,A^T)$ from $K^n$ to $K^n$. Duality swaps its two vertices and arrows and transposes all maps. In these bases $R_A$ is strictly self-dual. The node maps $f=(Y^T,X)$ should form a morphism $R_A\to R_B$. Both required linear equations follow in degree three. For the first use
$$
 XA-BY^T=(XAX^T-B)Y^T-XA(YX-I)^T ,
$$
and transpose the constant forms for the other arrow. The formal adjoint $f^*=(X^T,Y)$ has the opposite direction. The entries of both node products $ff^*-1$, $f^*f-1$ already belong to the inverse equations and their transposes.

Choose a constant linear projection from all pairs of node matrices onto the space of actual morphisms $\operatorname{Hom}(R_A,R_B)$. Set $\tilde f$ equal to the projection of $f$. Its entries are homogeneous linear forms. The projection errors are scalar combinations of the two-arrow intertwiner equations, hence available in degree three. The entries of $\tilde f\tilde f^*-1$ and $\tilde f^*\tilde f-1$ follow in degree three as well. Telescope the two products using the linear projection errors and their adjoints multiplied by linear forms. This argument works in characteristic two without any division.

To pass to multiplicities take the finite-dimensional algebra $E=\operatorname{End}(R_A\oplus R_B)$. Its radical quotient is a product of full matrix algebras, one for each indecomposable quiver type $U$, of sizes equal to the total multiplicities on the two sides. Indeed Krull–Schmidt gives local endomorphism algebras on the indecomposables. Their residue division algebras are $K$ over the algebraically closed field. Off-type Hom maps and same-type radical entries are the radical in the block description of $E$. The corner idempotents selecting $R_A,R_B$ select the matching source and target subspaces in each factor. The adjoint on $E$ preserves its radical, swaps dual factors, and fixes a factor whenever $U\simeq U^*$. On a fixed factor it induces the adjoint of the two multiplicity forms. In more detail, choose an isomorphism of $U$ with its dual and use it as reference at all copies. A duality identification on their sum induces a matrix $H$ on the residue scalar coefficients, and dualizing a corner matrix $P$ gives $H_A^{-1}P^T H_B$. This follows by applying transpose to the individual scalar entries in the identification; the block diagonal identification of $R_A\oplus R_B$ makes $H$ the sum of the two forms. Thus both quadratic equations on $P$ and its residue adjoint follow by taking constant linear combinations of the projected product equations. The quotient coordinates make sense on endomorphisms; extend each coordinate linearly to all node pairs to take these combinations. In particular no inverse basis change or division depending on variables is involved. The derivation remains valid in characteristic two whenever the residue forms have been identified there.

For clarity, the relation to the canonical congruence blocks in odd or zero characteristic is the following standard quiver interpretation of the Horn–Sergeichuk forms. An indecomposable $U$ not isomorphic to its dual contributes a hyperbolic block with underlying pair $U\oplus U^*$, each type occurring once. A genuinely selfadjoint single copy of $U$ contributes a block whose multiplicity form is symmetric. If $U$ is isodual and admits no selfadjoint single copy, it has a skew-adjoint identification with its dual. Indeed for an arbitrary isomorphism $T:U\to U^*$ at least one of $T+T^*$ and $T-T^*$ is invertible since their sum is $2T$ and the nonunits in the local endomorphism algebra form an ideal after identifying $U^*$ with $U$. A symmetric invertible identification would give a selfadjoint copy (one vertex basis can be chosen dual to the other). A skew-adjoint reference makes the multiplicity form alternating. One congruence block then gives a hyperbolic plane on two underlying copies. Equivalently duality exchanges the summands $U,U^*$ of a hyperbolic block, with opposite signs when their scalar coordinates are identified via this skew reference. The multiplicity forms for $a$ and $b$ copies are consequently nonsingular alternating forms on dimensions $2a,2b$. Over $K$ symmetric forms are equivalent to identity matrices and alternating forms to standard symplectic matrices. Krull–Schmidt and selfadjoint decomposition identify these cases also for the singular summands. In particular the argument does not require an invertible input or a cosquare for that input. [Horn and Sergeichuk, Section 3, especially (42)–(45), Theorem 3.1 and the proof of Theorem 2.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0709.2408) · [Sergeichuk's quiver classification](https://imath.kyiv.ua/~sergeich/sergeichuk_files/1988_izvest.pdf)

## The symplectic rectangular upper bound

Suppose that $0<a<b$. A matrix $P$ of size $2b$ by $2a$ and its symplectic adjoint $Q$ satisfy both rectangular inverse equations. They also satisfy $P J_{2a} P^T=J_{2b}$ and the dual Gram identity, with standard alternating matrices $J$ in symplectic pairs. The direction may be exchanged using the adjoint if the larger multiplicity occurs on the other side.

The two inverse equations give degree $2p^{v_p(b-a)}$ in odd characteristic by comparing elementary coefficients for $PQ$ and $QP$ at $q=p^{v_p(b-a)}$. Their identity specializations have coefficients of $(1+z)^{2b}$ and $(1+z)^{2a}$ differing at $q$, since $p$ is odd. Cauchy–Binet makes the coefficients equal before specialization. Telescoping the elementary minors gives the comparison in degree $2q$. In characteristic zero the trace difference already gives degree two.

For degree $a+2$, take the $b$ rows of $P$ corresponding to the first vector of each symplectic pair. The matrix $T$ of these rows has a right inverse $L$ by the corresponding columns of $Q$. Its row vectors are pairwise isotropic by the Gram equation. All minors of size $a+1$ of $T$ are consequences of these zero pairings in degree $a+1$ in every characteristic. Here is an integral proof, avoiding division in the exterior algebra.

Fix $a+1$ rows and let $W$ denote their exterior product in the standard $2a$ column coordinates. Its entries are the minors in question. For $j\ge1$ contract $W$ by the sum of the coordinate $2j$-covectors choosing $j$ complete standard symplectic pairs. This is the divided exterior power of the alternating form, with coefficient one on each such coordinate set in the standard interleaved order. On any $2j$ of the rows its value is the Pfaffian of their pairwise Gram matrix. This follows by expanding exterior products or by the integral Pfaffian minor summation identity, with no factorial denominator. The contraction of $W$ is the alternating sum over choices of those $2j$ rows of that Pfaffian times the exterior product of the other rows. Every coefficient is therefore derivably zero in degree $a+1$. Expand each Pfaffian to obtain multiples of zero Gram entries.

Fix a coordinate set $S$ of size $a+1$. Retain its singly occupied symplectic slots. If $S$ uses $d$ complete slots, there are exactly $d-1$ missing slots. Let $M$ denote their set. For every $T_0\subseteq M$ take the coefficient on the retained singletons and complete slots $T_0$ in the contraction for $j=d-|T_0|\ge1$. It sums the coefficients on all original coordinate sets having those same singletons and a set of $d$ complete slots containing $T_0$. Removing adjacent pairs incurs no varying sign. Alternating over $T_0\subseteq M$ kills every coefficient using any missing slot, leaving precisely $W_S$. This proves the claimed minor consequences in all characteristics.

Descend to the empty minor using $TL=I_b$. Given vanishing $(k+1)$-minors for $k\le a$, choose $k+1$ rows and $k$ columns and form their signed row of $k$-cofactors $v$, supported on those rows. Then $vT=0$ by expansion of $(k+1)$-minors, including repetitions. From $(vT)L=0$ and $v(TL-I)=0$ obtain $v=0$ in degree $k+2$. Since $b>k$, every $k$-minor occurs. At $k=0$ this gives one. Thus the two displayed upper bounds have minimum $\delta(a,b)$. If either multiplicity vanishes, the corresponding opposite identity is already a nonzero constant after projection.

For a pair $U,U^*$ of different types, the quotient equations restricted to one member already give an ordinary rectangular inverse pair of dimensions $a,b$. For a symmetric multiplicity form they give such a pair with the second matrix the transpose. Both have a proof of degree $\delta(a,b)$ by the elementary-coefficient and cofactor proofs for the ordinary rectangle. All replay maps are linear, and the preparation used degree three. This yields $\max(3,\min_C\delta(r_C,s_C))$ as an upper bound. [Rectangular inverse degree and matching-design lower bound](ConjugacyDegree.md)

## Lower bound and low degrees

In canonical coordinates independently assign a rectangular matrix $P_C$ of size $b$ by $a$ to each mismatched congruence type. Substitute $P_C\otimes I_{\dim C}$ into $X$, and $P_C^T\otimes I_{\dim C}$ into $Y$, on these summands, using identities on matched types and zero cross terms. The congruence entries specialize to scalar multiples of entries of $(P_C P_C^T-I_b)\otimes C$, and the inverse pairs specialize to both signed rectangle equations. The previous signed-rectangle result gives the exact degree $\delta(a,b)$ for this system. Here the word signed refers to the single matrix of variables with transpose as inverse. Its lower bound uses signed partial-matching weights, as opposed to any assumption about a Boolean or finite-field domain. The minimum degree of a disjoint union equals the least of the local degrees by the filtered tensor-product proof in the rectangle reference. Consequently a global proof in canonical coordinates needs degree at least $\min_C\delta(r_C,s_C)$. To obtain this lower bound on the original system above degree two, transport any purported smaller degree proof to the canonical one at cost at most degree two. [Signed rectangle and disjoint-system references](SymmetricCongruenceDegree.md)

In original coordinates an entry of $XAX^T$ is identically zero on distinct row variables precisely when $A=0$. For a diagonal entry it is identically zero precisely when $A$ is alternating. This proves the degree-zero test. With no such constant input, no degree-one proof can start. Every input has pure quadratic and constant parts, so in degree two there can be no new multiplication step unless a constant was already derived. It suffices to decide whether the span of all inputs contains a nonzero constant. The inverse entries have bidegree $(1,1)$ in $X,Y$ besides constants. If a combination cancels their quadratic parts, its constant is zero by evaluation at $X=Y=I$. The other quadratic parts have bidegree $(2,0)$. Thus a degree-two refutation exists exactly when some linear functional vanishes identically on $XAX^T$ but does not vanish on $B$.

Over the infinite field $K$, the linear span of these values is the symmetric matrices for nonzero symmetric $A$, the alternating matrices for nonzero alternating $A$, and the entire matrix space for nonsymmetric nonalternating $A$. If $A$ is nonalternating, take a vector $u$ with $u^TAu\ne0$ and $X=vu^T$ to get every rank-one symmetric direction. They span the symmetric matrices, including the off-diagonal symmetric matrix units by polarization. If $A$ is nonsymmetric, two rows of $X$ may be chosen so their ordered pairings differ. Using just those rows and subtracting a symmetric contribution gives an off-diagonal direction modulo the symmetric matrices for every pair of indices. For alternating $A$, independently choose two rows with nonzero pairing, obtaining the elementary alternating matrices directly. This establishes the low-degree cases and completes the proof.

## Sharp numerical consequences

For every odd $p$ and threshold $D\ge4$ the least side supporting a congruence instance of degree at least $D$ is exactly
$$
 2(D-2)+p^e,\qquad e=\min\{i\ge0:2p^i\ge D\}.
$$
All mismatched types must have smaller multiplicity at least $D-2$ and gap divisible by $p^e$. Equal total sides force at least two mismatches of opposite signs; on the side with the positive gap their total contribution is at least the asserted number. The two symmetric diagonal matrices with complementary numbers of ones and zeros, with multiplicities $D-2$ and $D-2+p^e$, attain it. Larger sides follow by adding common copies of the rank-one type.

A nonsymmetric nonsingular example uses the two blocks
$$
 U=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Use four and seven copies of $U,V$ in the first matrix and seven and four in the second. In every characteristic different from two these are distinct canonical block types. The cosquare of $U$ has a single Jordan block of size two and eigenvalue $-1$, and $V$ is one alternating plane. Both inputs are nonsymmetric and nonalternating with full rank 22. The exact refutation degree is six in characteristic three and three in every other characteristic different from two.

The matching-design and canonical-form results cited above are inputs. The general degree formula and its assembly are written proofs; independent formal verification and priority review remain open. The argument uses no hypothesis on the distribution of primes.
