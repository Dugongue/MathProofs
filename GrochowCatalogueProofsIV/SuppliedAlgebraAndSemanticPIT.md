# Identity testing in a supplied algebra and two semantic circuit tests

## GP-0659 · Supplied multiplication

For a finite algebra over any fixed finite field, identity as a function on the supplied algebra is coNP-complete. This already holds for one-dimensional associative algebras and polynomial formal degree. For a finite-dimensional algebra over an infinite field, formal identity testing in the supplied algebra is polynomial-time equivalent to ordinary commutative circuit PIT over that field. Over a finite base field, the same equivalence holds for the different semantics allowing all scalar extensions.

For the finite-domain assertion, nonidentity has a certificate consisting of one input tuple, and structure constants give polynomial-time evaluation. Conversely let $A=\mathbb F_q$. Convert a Boolean 3-CNF to its satisfaction indicator using polynomial operations for NOT, OR and AND. At each input use $b(x)=1-(x-1)^{q-1}$, which maps the entire field onto $\{0,1\}$. At assignments to the field the resulting function is zero everywhere exactly when the 3-CNF is unsatisfiable. Its formal degree is at most $3(q-1)$ times the number of clauses. If genuinely nonassociative inputs are required, use a fixed direct product $A=\mathbb F_q\times B$ where $B$ is any fixed nonassociative algebra, multiply each input by the idempotent $(1,0)$ first, and use $(1,0)$ as the Boolean scalar. The output lies in the first component and the same reduction applies.

For the formal assertion, write each input as $x_i=\sum_j t_{ij}e_j$ in the supplied basis, with independent commuting $t_{ij}$. Replace each gate by its coordinate vector. Each multiplication uses only the bilinear formula from the structure constants, so the resulting list of commutative output circuits has polynomial size. They are all identically zero exactly when the supplied-algebra expression is identically zero on all tuples over an infinite field, or over the algebraic closure of a finite field. Each coordinate can be checked separately, or packed into one polynomial by multiplying each by a different power of a fresh variable. Ordinary PIT embeds as the case of a one-dimensional algebra with field multiplication. This is a classification of the supplied evaluation algebra. The free nonassociative algebra model of the catalogue source has different equations. [Mukhopadhyay–Ramya–Shastri](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.APPROX/RANDOM.2025.56)

## GP-0682 · Degree compared with a binary threshold

For division-free rational circuits promised to have semantic output degree at most $D$, with $D$ a polynomial parameter, deciding whether the semantic degree is at most a binary-supplied $b$ is deterministically many-one equivalent to commutative PIT with the same semantic low-degree model. The reduction does not assume any bound on intermediate gate degrees.

For $b\ge D$ the answer is immediate. Otherwise interpolate the univariate polynomial $C(tx)$ of degree at most $D$ in $t$ from $D+1$ distinct small integer values of $t$. Sum the coefficient-extraction functionals for the powers $t^{b+1}$ through $t^D$. The resulting linear combination of $D+1$ circuits $C(t_i x)$ is exactly the sum of the high-degree homogeneous components. It has polynomial circuit size and coefficient encoding overhead, since the Lagrange formula at integer points of size at most $D$ has polynomial-bit rational coefficients. Vanishing of this one circuit is equivalent to the degree test. For the reverse direction, map $C$ to $1+yC(x)$ and compare with $b=0$. This remains a polynomial-degree promise and vanishes in positive degree exactly if $C$ is zero. [Fine-grained degree and PIT context](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2026/313/)

## GP-0692 · Dimension of the full derivative space

There is a bounded-error polynomial-time algorithm in the rational bit model, and a deterministic polynomial-time many-one equivalence with bounded-degree commutative PIT. The dimension of the span of all partial derivatives equals the coefficient matrix rank of $f(X+Y)$ across $X\mid Y$. Indeed, the coefficients of the $Y$ monomials are the Hasse derivatives, which over the rationals differ from ordinary derivatives only by nonzero scalars.

Take $r+1$ independent tuples $X_i$ and $r+1$ independent tuples $Y_j$. Form the matrix $M_{ij}=f(X_i+Y_j)$. Its determinant is identically zero if and only if the derivative dimension is at most $r$. One direction follows by factoring the coefficient matrix into rank-many products. For the other, the evaluation vectors of all monomials of the bounded degrees span their full coefficient dual space over the rationals. Otherwise a nonzero polynomial would vanish at every rational tuple. Thus among the specialized row vectors one can select $r+1$ independent ones when the coefficient rank exceeds $r$. Next choose $r+1$ evaluations for the other block keeping those rows independent, using the same spanning argument. The selected determinant is nonzero. The determinant is computed by a polynomial-size division-free circuit and has degree at most $(r+1)\deg f$. This reduces the test to one PIT instance. The reverse reduction fixes $r=0$, because the zero-order derivative is $f$ itself.

For completeness, randomized PIT here can be done in bit-polynomial time even with repeated squaring of rational constants. In a size-$S$ circuit whose constants have at most $B$ bits, repeated addition and multiplication give a common denominator and a coefficient numerator of bit length at most $(S+B+2)2^{O(S)}$. This follows by propagating products of denominators at multiplication and cross products at addition, using polynomial coefficient $\ell_1$ norms in the numerator. Consequently any fixed nonzero numerator coefficient, together with all input denominators, rules out at most $(S+B+2)2^{O(S)}$ bad primes. Sample a prime of bit length a sufficiently large polynomial in $S+B$ by randomized sampling and deterministic primality testing, then evaluate over that prime field at independent uniform points. The proportion of bad primes tends to zero by the prime count, and Schwartz–Zippel bounds the remaining failure probability. A zero circuit always gives zero on admissible primes. This yields the asserted bit-model algorithm.
