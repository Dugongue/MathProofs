# Arbitrary-precision factorization on recognized Newton classes

## GP-0607

Let $F$ be monic over $\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z$ with $p$ prime. There is a Las Vegas polynomial-bit algorithm to construct a proper factor or certify irreducibility on the following recognizable class, for arbitrary $k$. First separate distinct irreducible factors of the reduction modulo $p$. If the reduction is a pure power $\phi^m$, pass to the unramified ring for $\phi$ and use the branch of degree $m$ at one residue root. Translate that root to zero. The algorithm applies if an integral Newton scaling of this branch at usable precision has reduction either irreducible or divisible by two distinct irreducibles. It also recognizes one-edge branches with slope denominator equal to their degree, certifying their irreducibility. The scaling and the one-edge tests are specified below. These conditions allow reduction modulo $p$ with arbitrarily high multiplicity and impose no upper bound on $k$. ### Reducing a repeated nonlinear residue to one linear branch

Work first over $R=\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z$. Any factorization of monic $F$ as two nonunits is equivalent to a factorization as two positive-degree monic polynomials. A nonunit factor must reduce to a nonconstant polynomial, since any polynomial reducing to a nonzero constant is a unit, and reduction of a product equal to $\overline F$ precludes zero reduction. Each such factor can be written as a unit times a monic polynomial of its residual degree. To see this, start with its normalized reduction and lift a decomposition $U G$ one precision digit at a time. For the error modulo the next digit, division by monic $\overline G$ gives the correction to $U$, and the lower-degree remainder divided by the nonzero constant $\overline U$ gives the correction to $G$. The product of two monic factors has the degree of $F$; hence its unit multiplier must be constant, by comparing their leading terms, and is one.

If $\overline F$ contains two distinct irreducibles, partition its primary powers into two coprime parts and use coprime Hensel lifting. If $\overline F=\phi^m$ with $s=\deg\phi$, take $S=R[T]/(\Phi)$ for any monic lift $\Phi$. It is the unramified ring with residue field $\mathbb F_{p^s}$. The residue root $\alpha$ given by $T$, and its Frobenius conjugates, are distinct. Their lifts as roots of $\Phi$ are unique by simple-root Hensel lifting. Sending $T$ to the lift of $\alpha^p$ gives the Frobenius automorphism $\tau$ of $S$, of order $s$, because the same uniqueness lifts its inverse and its $s$th power. Its fixed ring is $R$. To see this last fact, an invariant element reduces to the prime field; subtract a lifted scalar and divide by $p$, reducing the required precision by one. Induction lifts all of its digits in $R$.

Coprime lifting gives a unique monic degree-$m$ branch $P$ of $F$ reducing to $(x-\alpha)^m$, and $F=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\tau^j(P)$. If $F=AB$ with monic positive degrees, the two reductions are $\phi^u,\phi^{m-u}$ with $0<u<m$. Comparing their coprime branches at $\alpha$ gives a proper monic factor of $P$. Conversely, for a proper monic factor $G$ of $P$, the product of its $\tau$-conjugates belongs to $R[x]$ and is a proper factor of $F$. Thus either branch output descends correctly. For $m=1$ there is no proper factor.

### Integral scaling

Choose any lift $c$ of $\alpha$, and write $Q(y)=P(c+y)=y^m+\sum_{i<m}q_i y^i$. For an integer $h\ge0$ with $hm<k$, the scaling test requires $\nu_p(q_i)\ge h(m-i)$ for every $i$, with valuations truncated at $k$. Its normalized reduction has coefficients
$$
 \overline{\widetilde Q}(z)=z^m+\sum_{i<m}
       \overline{p^{-h(m-i)}q_i}\,z^i .
$$
Each quotient modulo $p$ is well defined by $hm<k$. Try every such $h$ satisfying the inequalities. If a normalized reduction has two distinct irreducibles, choose integral coefficient representatives of $Q$ with the required divisibilities, divide out the indicated powers, and lift a coprime factorization of the resulting $\widetilde Q$ to precision $k$. Undo the scaling on a monic degree-$u$ lifted factor by $p^{hu}G(y/p^h)$, and similarly on the complement. These polynomials are integral and their product equals $Q$ modulo $p^k$, since undoing the scaling multiplies the coefficient of $y^i$ by $p^{h(m-i)}$. The coefficient representatives can equally be handled in the unramified ring at precision $k+hm$ defined by the same lift $\Phi$.

If $\overline{\widetilde Q}$ is irreducible, any proper factorization of $Q$ is impossible. For an integral monic factor of degree $u$ its weighted valuation $\min_i(\nu_p(g_i)+hi)$ is at most $hu$. Weighted valuation is multiplicative, by substituting $y\mapsto p^h y$, dividing off the minimum power, and using that the residue polynomial ring is a domain. Products equal to $Q$ modulo $p^k$ have weighted valuation $hm$, since the error has weighted valuation at least $k>hm$. Both factors must therefore attain their degree upper bounds, so every coefficient of each satisfies its corresponding scaling divisibility. Undoing the scale in the equality gives a proper monic factorization of $\overline{\widetilde Q}$, a contradiction.

### One-edge irreducibility at finite precision

Suppose instead that $\nu_p(q_0)=A<k$, $A>0$, $\gcd(A,m)=1$, and $m\nu_p(q_i)+Ai\ge Am$ for all $i$. This is the single-edge test. Use weighted valuation $\min_i(m\nu_p(g_i)+Ai)$, which is multiplicative just as above, or by adjoining a formal $m$th root of the uniformizer. Each degree-$u$ factor has weighted valuation at most $Au$. Again the error modulo $p^k$ has weighted valuation larger than $Am$, so both factors attain their degree upper bounds. The constant coefficients obey $m\nu_p(g_0)\ge Au$ and $m\nu_p(h_0)\ge A(m-u)$. Their valuations sum to $A$ because $A<k$. Both inequalities are therefore equalities. Coprimality gives $m\mid u$, excluding any proper factor.

There are at most $k$ scalings to test. Hensel lifting, arithmetic in $S$, valuation tests and the conjugate products have polynomial bit cost in $\deg F,k,\log p$. Finite-field polynomial factorization supplies the Las Vegas step. Each returned factor is checkable by multiplication, and irreducibility uses a deterministic finite-field irreducibility check or the displayed valuation certificate. This theorem does not decide repeated-residue inputs for which neither recognized test succeeds.
