# A sharp query hierarchy for simultaneous similarity from homomorphism counts

Fix a field of order $q$. A tuple of $m$ square matrices specifies a finite-dimensional module for the free algebra on $m$ generators. A hidden target can be inspected by naming any finite-dimensional probe module $X$ and receiving the exact integer $|\operatorname{Hom}(X,M)|$. For comparison of two hidden targets a query returns both counts for the same $X$. Charge $\dim X$ once per query and add costs along the execution path. The dimension and candidate promise are public. Computation and preprocessing of the probe dictionary are free. This information model distinguishes the cost of a universal invariant from the cost of examining input matrices.

**Theorem.** Let $S_1,\ldots,S_N$ be known pairwise nonisomorphic absolutely simple targets of the same dimension $n$, where $N\ge2$. For each fixed $k\ge1$ the optimal deterministic worst-case total probe dimension to identify a hidden target, or to test two-target isomorphism, is

| Queries | Optimal cost |
|---|---|
| $k$ fixed in advance, including all probe choices | $\Theta_k(nN^{1+1/k})$ |
| Up to $k$ adaptively chosen probes | $\Theta_k(nN^{2^k/(2^k-1)})$ |
| Unrestricted adaptive probes | $n(N-1)$ |

One randomized query suffices for two-target isomorphism with one-sided error at most $1/3$ and cost at most $2nN$. Every randomized algorithm with error at most $1/3$, even with unlimited adaptive queries, has worst-case cost $\Omega(nN)$. The one-query random upper bound is for equality; identification has a larger output requirement. At one deterministic query the exact cost is $nN(N-1)/2$.

### A probe is exactly a nonnegative integer code

Write $w_i=\dim_{\mathbb F_q}\operatorname{Hom}(X,S_i)$, obtainable from the exact answer $q^{w_i}$. Taking a basis of maps to each $S_i$ gives a surjection $X\twoheadrightarrow\bigoplus_i S_i^{w_i}$. Indeed, if its image is proper, the cokernel, a quotient of a semisimple module, maps onto some $S_i$. The resulting nonzero map from the direct sum vanishes on the image. Schur's lemma and $\operatorname{End}(S_i)=\mathbb F_q$ identify this with a nontrivial scalar dependence among basis maps. Therefore $\dim X\ge n\sum_i w_i$. Conversely the displayed direct sum itself realizes these weights at exactly that cost. The equivalence applies to arbitrary probes, including indecomposable or nonsplit probes.

Distinct targets must receive distinct $k$-vectors of nonnegative weights for fixed queries. Minimize the sum of their coordinate sums by using $N$ lattice points of smallest total degree. There are $\binom{s+k}{k}$ points of degree at most $s$, so the minimum total degree is $\Theta_k(N^{1+1/k})$. This also gives an exact finite optimizer with arbitrary tie-breaking in the outer shell. Taking $k=1$ gives weights $0,1,\ldots,N-1$.

For adaptive queries let $C_k(N)$ be the optimal worst-case dimension divided by $n$. The first weights partition candidates into buckets according to their answers. Give the largest buckets the smallest distinct nonnegative weights, and let $B$ be the largest bucket size. For $B\le N/2$, the first cost is $\Omega(N^2/B)$: at most $jB$ targets have weight below $j$, and summing the remaining target counts for the first $\lfloor N/(2B)\rfloor$ thresholds proves the bound. Continuing in the largest bucket costs at least $C_{k-1}(B)$; arbitrary outside candidates cannot help since the first cost inequality applies to every subset. Conversely partition into buckets of size at most $B$ and label them consecutively. The worst cost is $O(N^2/B)+C_{k-1}(B)$ using the same strategy recursively. If $C_{k-1}(B)=\Theta_k(B^\alpha)$ for growing $B$, balancing gives $B\asymp N^{2/(1+\alpha)}$ and exponent $2\alpha/(1+\alpha)$. The case $B>N/2$ already pays the inductive lower bound on that bucket, and bounded $B$ pays quadratic first cost. The base exponent is 2, yielding $2^k/(2^k-1)$. For unrestricted queries test the first $N-1$ simple types individually. Along the all-weight-zero path, a query of cost $nd$ can eliminate at most $d$ candidates. Until total cost reaches $n(N-1)$ at least two candidates can give all zero weights, proving the matching lower bound.

To transfer identification lower bounds to deterministic equality, run an equality tester on the diagonal pair $(S_i,S_i)$. Two distinct candidates following the same complete diagonal transcript would also give this transcript on their mixed pair, and equality would be answered incorrectly. Thus diagonal simulation identifies the target. In the opposite direction apply an identification tree simultaneously to both inputs and reject at the first disagreement; the probes follow the common transcript while the answers coincide.

For randomized equality, choose independent uniform weights from $\{0,1,2\}$. A distinct pair collides with probability $1/3$. For the lower bound couple a uniform identical pair $(U,U)$ with $(U,V)$ where $V$ is a uniformly random different candidate. For fixed algorithm coins trace the branch on zero weights, meaning Hom-count one. Its probes hit at most $D/n$ types if the worst cost is $D$; when $D<n(N-1)$ the branch remains feasible. If neither $U$ nor $V$ is hit, the two transcripts coincide. Their output probabilities differ by at most $2D/(nN)$ after averaging coins. Bounded error requires a difference of at least $1/3$.

### Size of the generic family, and an upper bound on all targets

For fixed $m\ge2,q$, let $K_d$ be the number of isomorphism classes of all $d$-dimensional tuples and $N_n$ the number of absolutely simple classes of dimension $n$. Then, as $n$ grows,

$$N_n\sim K_n\sim H_n:=\frac{(q-1)q^{mn^2}}{|\mathrm{GL}_n(q)|}.$$

The probability that two random matrices generate the full matrix algebra tends to one, which implies the same for $m\ge2$. Such tuples have orbit size $|\mathrm{GL}_n(q)|/(q-1)$ and are absolutely simple. This supplies the lower bound $(1-o(1))H_n$. Reineke studies these counts as polynomials across changing fields, while the asymptotic here fixes the field. [Sercombe and Shalev](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01115) · [Reineke](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06959)

For the upper bound, Burnside's lemma gives $K_n=\sum_{[g]}q^{m c(g)}/|C(g)^\times|$, where $g$ runs over similarity classes of invertible matrices, $C(g)$ is its commuting algebra and $c(g)=\dim C(g)$. The scalar terms sum to $H_n$. Every nonscalar has $c(g)\le(n-1)^2+1$; over the algebraic closure the Jordan formula sums squares of conjugate partition parts over eigenvalues, with positive parts totaling $n$ and none equal to $n$. The number of classes is $O_q(q^n)$: including singular matrices gives generating series $\prod_{j\ge1}(1-qu^j)^{-1}$ by rational canonical form and the Euler product for monic polynomials. The product over $j\ge2$ converges at $u=q^{-1}$.

Uniformly in $g$, the proportion of units of $C(g)$ is at least $q^{-o(n)}$. Indeed the semisimple quotient of the commuting algebra of the rational canonical blocks is a product of matrix algebras over $\mathbb F_{q^{\deg f}}$, one per occurring pair of irreducible polynomial $f$ and block length. This follows by quotienting the endomorphism ring of the direct sum of cyclic modules by its radical; the residue field of a block with annihilator $f^j$ is $\mathbb F_q[x]/(f)$. The log inverse unit proportion of each factor is $O_q(q^{-\deg f})$ by the general linear group order formula, and units lift modulo the radical. For degrees at most $(\log_q n)/4$ there are $O_q(n^{1/4})$ possible polynomials, each with $O(\sqrt n)$ distinct block lengths. The larger degrees together have at most $n$ factors and contribute $O_q(n^{3/4})$ to the log inverse density. Thus nonscalar terms total at most $q^{n+o(n)+(m-1)((n-1)^2+1)}=o(H_n)$.

All isomorphism classes up to dimension $n$ give a separating dictionary of probes. If two dimension-$n$ targets have identical answers on these probes, their injective Hom counts from every $X$ of dimension at most $n$ agree. Induct on dimension, subtracting from $|\operatorname{Hom}(X,M)|$ the injective counts for $X/L$ over each nonzero submodule $L$, since maps have unique kernels. Taking $X$ to be the first target yields an injection to the second, hence an isomorphism. This is the classical finite Hom-count argument. The dictionary costs $\sum_{d\le n}dK_d\sim nH_n$ by the growth rate above. With unrestricted queries the absolute-simple lower bound yields the sharp worst-case asymptotic $(1+o(1))nH_n$ on all targets.

For a randomized one-query test on all targets, take the direct sum of independent uniform $0,1,2$ copies of each dictionary probe. For a nonisomorphic pair at least one difference of Hom dimensions is nonzero. Conditioning on the other weights, at most one of the three coefficients can make the total difference vanish. The cost is at most $2\sum_{d\le n}dK_d$, with the preceding randomized lower bound on the simple stratum applying. Thus the optimal randomized worst-case cost is $\Theta(nH_n)$ even with all targets admitted and only one query allowed. In contrast, on raw matrix inputs the intertwining system can be formed directly. Exponentially large oracle probes are an information-model cost; they give no lower bound for algorithms reading the matrices.

**Scope and earlier work.** Chen, Flum, Liu and Xun derive a query-count hierarchy for hidden graphs with arbitrary probe sizes. Derksen, Klep, Makam and Volčič separate matrix-tuple orbits with ranks of linear pencils. Those queries may encode finitely presented modules of infinite vector-space dimension. Our bounds charge the dimension of each finite-dimensional Hom probe. The sharp adaptive exponents concern exact deterministic identification or equality in that model. The generic orbit counts, Schur's lemma, Krull–Schmidt theory and finite Hom reconstruction are ingredients from earlier work. Publication priority for the query-dimension tradeoffs remains unverified.
