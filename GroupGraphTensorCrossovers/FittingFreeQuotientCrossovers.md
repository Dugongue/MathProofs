# Fitting-free quotients, punctured codes, and graph counting

Three results are proved below. First, epimorphism testing for permutation groups is NP-complete already for a family of Fitting-free groups with elementary abelian abelianization. Exact epimorphism counting on the family is #P-complete under polynomial-time metric reductions. Second, on this same family a target of fixed abelianization rank admits polynomial-time decision and counting. NP-completeness persists with target code of codimension one, even on a subfamily with polynomial-time group isomorphism. Third, the kernels of all Fitting-free quotients of an arbitrary finite group form a distributive lattice, with a canonical poset description by monolithic quotients. The first two results use permutation generators as the succinct group input.

## 1. Subgroups of products of symmetric groups

For a binary linear code $C\leq\mathbb F_2^n$, define $G_C=\operatorname{sgn}^{-1}(C)\leq S_5^n$, with the sign on each coordinate interpreted as a bit. Use its faithful permutation action on $n$ disjoint blocks of five points. From a generator matrix of $C$ one can construct permutation generators in polynomial time. Include generators of $A_5$ on each block individually, and for each generating row put a fixed transposition in its nonzero coordinates and the identity elsewhere. These elements generate the inverse image because they generate the kernel of the sign and map onto the code. This is the family used by Grochow, Johnson, and Levet for a reduction from code equivalence to group isomorphism. [Grochow–Johnson–Levet, §8](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19777v1#S8)

The derived group $G_C'$ is $A_5^n$. It is contained there because the quotient is abelian, and contains it because $A_5^n$ is perfect. It is also the socle. Every coordinate $A_5$ is normal and simple, and its product has trivial centralizer in $S_5^n$. A minimal normal subgroup outside this product would centralize it, which is impossible. It follows similarly that $G_C$ is Fitting-free. Indeed, any abelian normal subgroup intersects $A_5^n$ trivially and would centralize it. Here the standard equality $\operatorname{Aut}(A_5)=S_5$ identifies the latter with its action by conjugation, so the centralizer is trivial. One quick verification is to use the five Sylow $2$-subgroups of $A_5$, one for each fixed point in its natural action. This action of $A_5$ is faithful, so an automorphism fixing these five subgroups commutes with the action and fixes every element of $A_5$. Conjugation by $S_5$ realizes all permutations of the five Sylow $2$-subgroups.

For $J\subseteq[n]$, let $C|_J$ denote the projection onto the ordered coordinates in $J$, called puncturing. The coordinate projection sends $G_C$ onto $G_{C|_J}$. Every epimorphism $G_C\to G_D$, with $D\leq\mathbb F_2^m$, is a coordinate projection onto some $m$-element subset $J$, followed by a coordinate bijection and independent conjugations by elements of $S_5$ on the retained coordinates. In particular $G_C$ surjects onto $G_D$ exactly when some $C|_J$ is permutation-equivalent to $D$.

For proof, the epimorphism maps the derived group onto the derived group. Each coordinate simple direct factor in $A_5^n$ maps either trivially or onto one coordinate simple direct factor of $A_5^m$. Its image is normal in the derived group of the target by surjectivity on derived groups. A nontrivial simple normal subgroup of $A_5^m$ is a coordinate factor. This follows from the normal subgroups of a direct product of centerless nonabelian simple groups being products of coordinates, as one can check by commuting elements with individual coordinate factors. Since the source coordinate factors commute pairwise, exactly one source coordinate maps nontrivially to each target coordinate. These coordinates give $J$, and the chosen identifications of $A_5$ factors extend to conjugations by $S_5$. On all of $G_C$, the epimorphism is determined by this restriction, because it must respect conjugation on $A_5^m$ and its centralizer in $S_5^m$ is trivial. This gives the asserted form. Local conjugations preserve sign, so the coordinate bijection identifies the two codes. Conversely each such coordinate bijection and local conjugations give the required epimorphism.

Thus there is exactly one possible kernel for each matching subset $J$, and different subsets give distinct kernels on the source derived group. For any particular coordinate injection $[m]\to[n]$ that matches the codes, the local conjugations give exactly $120^m$ distinct epimorphisms. In particular
$$
 \big|\operatorname{Aut}(G_D)\big|
   =120^m\big|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{perm}}(D)\big|.
$$
This follows also by taking $m=n$ in the epimorphism description. The related decision problem of finding permutation-equivalent punctured codes has appeared in coding-based cryptography. Here the reduction below uses only graphic cut codes, identifies the kernels themselves, and computes the exact counting factor. [Equivalent punctured codes, Definition 5](https://eprint.iacr.org/2012/585.pdf)

## 2. Hardness from clique and cycle detection

For a simple graph $X$, let $C(X)\leq\mathbb F_2^{E(X)}$ be the row space of its binary vertex–edge incidence matrix. Deleting edge coordinates gives exactly the cut code of the resulting spanning subgraph. Its dimension is $\sum_j(v_j-1)$, where $v_j$ are the numbers of vertices in its connected components. Indeed, the kernel of the map from vertex labels to edge differences consists of the functions constant on each component. Isolated vertices contribute zero to the rank.

Fix $k\geq3$, take $G_{C(X)}$ as the source, and take $G_{C(K_k)}$ as the target. Write $m=\binom{k}{2}$. A matching puncturing uses $m$ edges and has rank $k-1$. For each nontrivial connected component of the selected subgraph put $r_j=v_j-1>0$. Its number of edges is at most $\binom{r_j+1}{2}$, and
$$
 \sum_j\binom{r_j+1}{2}\leq\binom{1+\sum_jr_j}{2}=\binom{k}{2}.
$$
The inequality is strict if two $r_j$'s are positive, since the difference is $\sum_{j<\ell}r_jr_\ell$. Equality of total edge counts therefore forces one nontrivial component on $k$ vertices with every possible edge. Conversely the edge set of every $k$-clique gives the target code. Thus the epimorphism exists exactly when a $k$-clique exists, and the kernel count is exactly the number of $k$-cliques. Inputs with $k>|V(X)|$ may be answered directly before reduction.

The minimum-weight nonzero words of $C(K_k)$ are its $k$ star cuts. The weight of a cut with $a$ vertices on one side is $a(k-a)$, which is minimized at a singleton side. This also covers $k=3$. Any coordinate automorphism permutes those stars. It is determined by that permutation because each edge is the unique common coordinate of two stars. Conversely every vertex permutation gives a code automorphism. Consequently the exact number of epimorphisms in the reduction is
$$
 \#\operatorname{Epi}(G_{C(X)},G_{C(K_k)})
 =120^{\binom{k}{2}}k!\,\#\{k\text{-cliques in }X\}.
$$

For completeness, counting $k$-cliques with $k$ supplied is #P-hard parsimoniously. For each clause of a 3-CNF formula make one vertex per satisfying assignment of the variables of that clause. Join vertices of different clauses exactly when their local assignments agree on overlapping variables. A clique of size equal to the number of clauses chooses one local assignment per clause, and such choices are in bijection with global satisfying assignments when every variable occurs. Tautological clauses may ensure occurrence of each variable and at least three clauses. Counting 3-CNF assignments is #P-complete by the computation witness encoding with uniquely determined gate values. The displayed multiplier has polynomial bit length and can be divided out exactly.

The unrestricted decision and counting problems for finite groups given by permutation generators are in NP and #P, respectively. Guess one target permutation $h_i$ for each listed source generator $g_i$, testing $h_i\in H$. Form the subgroup $L$ generated by $(g_i,h_i)$ on the union of the two permutation domains. Its projection to $G$ is surjective. Therefore the chosen tuple defines a homomorphism exactly when $|L|=|G|$, which says this projection is bijective. The homomorphism is an epimorphism exactly when $|\langle h_i\rangle|=|H|$. Permutation group membership and order are computable in deterministic polynomial time by the standard stabilizer-chain algorithms. A fixed binary encoding for the tuple gives one accepting witness per epimorphism. This proves the stated completeness results. [Seress, *Permutation Group Algorithms*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511546549)

NP-hardness already holds when the target code has codimension one. Given a simple graph $X$ with $v\geq3$ vertices, use $G_{C(X)}$ and take the target $G_D$, where $D\leq\mathbb F_2^v$ is the even-weight code. A matching puncturing selects $v$ edges. The orthogonal complement of the selected cut code is the binary cycle space of those edges, by the incidence-matrix kernel description. It equals the span of the all-ones vector exactly when the selected edges form a single cycle, together with isolated vertices. To see this, any simple cycle supplies a nonzero cycle-space vector, so when the cycle space consists of zero and the full-support vector, that cycle uses every chosen edge. A nonzero cycle-space vector exists only when some cycle exists, since a nonempty even-degree edge set contains a cycle. With $v$ edges a simple cycle in $X$ is Hamiltonian. Conversely a Hamiltonian cycle gives precisely this code. The target code automorphism group is all of $S_v$, and hence the epimorphism count is $120^v v!$ times the number of undirected Hamiltonian cycles counted as edge sets.

In fact this hardness holds on a family whose group isomorphism problem is in polynomial time. Take the union of the sign-code groups of cut codes of simple cycles and of 3-connected cubic planar graphs. Both sides of the Hamiltonian reduction belong to this family when $X$ is taken from the latter class, for which Hamiltonian Cycle is NP-complete. For the isomorphism test, recover the codes from permutation generators by taking signs on the five-point orbits. Code equivalence is equivalent to isomorphism of the binary column matroids. Indeed, the circuit dependencies over $\mathbb F_2$ are the indicator vectors of the circuits and span the kernel, since any nonzero dependency contains a circuit and removing it decreases support. The kernel determines the row space. Cycle codes are recognized by being the even-weight subspace on their coordinates, and two such codes of the same length are equivalent. In the other case, use polynomial-time graphic matroid realization to obtain a graph on the labeled edges, using Gaussian elimination for independence queries. Whitney's theorem identifies a graphic realization of the column matroid of a 3-connected graph with that graph up to graph isomorphism and isolated vertices. Thus for two inputs of the latter type, code equivalence reduces to isomorphism between the reconstructed cubic graphs, which is polynomial by Luks's bounded-degree algorithm. Section 1 with all coordinates retained gives the corresponding test for the original groups. [Garey–Johnson–Tarjan](https://doi.org/10.1137/0205049) · [Seymour](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579179) · [Whitney](https://doi.org/10.2307/2371127) · [Luks](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0000(82)90009-5)

## 3. Bounded target abelianization rank

Let $d=\dim D$, the rank of the abelianization of $G_D$. Given permutation generators in the stated representation, decision and exact counting take time $n^d L^{O(1)}$, where $n$ is the number of source blocks and $L$ is the input length. The case with no source blocks is decided directly. The 5-point blocks are the permutation orbits, so one can recover them from the generators. Taking each generator's signs yields generator matrices of $C,D$. In the natural permutation representation one can also verify the promise in polynomial time. Check that all the orbits have size five and that the group contains each localized alternating group, using membership tests on generators of those localized groups. Any subgroup of $S_5^n$ containing the product of the localized alternating groups is the full preimage of its sign code.

Take full-row-rank generator matrices for $C,D$ by row reduction. Fix $d$ ordered basis-column positions of the target, and change the target row basis so that those columns form the identity matrix in that order. An injection of target coordinates into source coordinates gives the required puncturing equivalence if and only if the row spaces of the target and the ordered restriction of the source matrix agree. For any such injection the images of those $d$ basis columns are independent source columns. Enumerate those ordered source positions in at most $n^d$ ways. For an independent choice let $B$ be the matrix of the selected source columns. A target column with vector $u\in\mathbb F_2^d$ must then map to a source column $Bu$. In fact equal row spaces imply that the restricted source matrix is a left linear image of the target matrix, and the identity positions fix the linear image to be $B$. Conversely, choosing columns by this rule gives the restricted matrix as $B$ times the target matrix. Full column rank of $B$ implies equality of the two row spaces.

For each pattern $u$ occurring outside the fixed target basis positions, let $a_u$ count the remaining source positions with column $Bu$, and let $b_u$ count the unassigned target positions with that pattern. Since $B$ is injective, distinct $u$ use disjoint sets of source positions. A compatible injection exists exactly when $a_u\geq b_u$ for all patterns. The number of compatible injections for the fixed basis assignment is $\prod_u (a_u)_{b_u}$, where $(a)_b=a(a-1)\cdots(a-b+1)$ and is zero for $a<b$. Sum over the choices and multiply by $120^m$ to count epimorphisms by Section 1. Each injection has exactly one ordered choice of the images of the fixed basis positions, so this counts it once. The all-zero rank case uses an empty basis and the same product rule. The total count is at most $n^m120^m$, with polynomial bit length. This proves the time bound, including the arithmetic of the counts. The clique reduction in Section 2 has $d=k-1$, so the problem on this family is W[1]-hard parameterized by $d$. [Downey–Fellows, *Fundamentals of Parameterized Complexity*, clique problem](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4471-5559-1)

## 4. The lattice of radical-free quotients of an arbitrary finite group

For any finite group $G$, let $\mathcal Q(G)=\{K\trianglelefteq G:G/K \text{ is Fitting-free}\}$, ordered by inclusion. It is a finite distributive lattice. More precisely, let $P(G)$ be the normal subgroups $C\trianglelefteq G$ such that $G/C$ is a nontrivial Fitting-free group with a unique minimal nontrivial normal subgroup, ordered by inclusion. Then $\mathcal Q(G)$ is anti-isomorphic to the lattice of upper sets of $P(G)$. From a Cayley table one can compute $P(G)$ and its order in polynomial time.

Fix a chief series $1=G_0<\cdots<G_t=G$. For each nonabelian chief factor $N_i=G_i/G_{i-1}$, let $C_i$ be the kernel of the conjugation action on $N_i$. The quotient $G/C_i$ contains $M_i=\operatorname{Inn}(N_i)$ in $\operatorname{Aut}(N_i)$. This is a minimal normal subgroup of $G/C_i$, since $G$ acts transitively on the simple factors of its nonabelian chief factor. It has trivial centralizer in $\operatorname{Aut}(N_i)$, since an automorphism commuting with all inner automorphisms fixes all elements of the centerless group $N_i$. If a nontrivial normal subgroup of $G/C_i$ intersected $M_i$ trivially, it would centralize $M_i$, an impossibility. Every such normal subgroup therefore contains $M_i$. In particular $G/C_i$ is Fitting-free and has the required unique minimal normal subgroup.

Every $K\in\mathcal Q(G)$ is an intersection of $C_i$'s. Take the nonabelian chief factors of $G$ that survive in $G/K$. The images of the chief series terms give a chief series of $G/K$ on removing repetitions, and the action on each surviving section is the action on its original factor through $G/K$. Intersect the centralizers of all these surviving nonabelian factors. The intersection contains $K$. Its image in $G/K$ has a restricted normal series with only abelian nontrivial sections. Indeed, a nontrivial section at a nonabelian chief factor would surject to that factor by minimality, and since the intersection centralizes that factor the factor would then commute with itself. Thus the image is solvable and is trivial because $G/K$ is Fitting-free. Conversely every such intersection gives a Fitting-free quotient, since its quotient embeds into the product of the selected $G/C_i$'s, surjectively on each coordinate. A solvable normal subgroup must have trivial image in each coordinate. This uses the chief-factor centralizer characterization of the solvable radical. [Hajiaghayi–JafariRaviz, §A.6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.01399v1)

It also follows that $P(G)$ is exactly the set of distinct $C_i$'s. If $G/K$ is nontrivial Fitting-free with a unique minimal nontrivial normal subgroup, the $C_i/K$'s used in an intersection representing $K$ have trivial intersection. One of them must be trivial, since all nontrivial normal subgroups of $G/K$ contain the same minimal normal subgroup. This gives $K=C_i$. The reverse inclusion was proved above.

Each $C\in P(G)$ is meet-prime among all normal subgroups of $G$. Suppose $X,Y\trianglelefteq G$ and $X\cap Y\leq C$. Their images commute in $G/C$, because $[X,Y]\subseteq X\cap Y$. If both images were nontrivial they would both contain the same nonabelian minimal normal subgroup, which cannot commute with itself. Thus $X\leq C$ or $Y\leq C$. Applying this repeatedly gives
$$
 \bigcap_{C'\in U} C'\ \leq C
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 C'\leq C \text{ for some } C'\in U
$$
for $U\subseteq P(G)$, with both sides false for $U=\varnothing$. For $K\in\mathcal Q(G)$, the set $\{C\in P(G):K\leq C\}$ is an upper set and its intersection recovers $K$. Conversely an upper set $U$ is exactly the set recovered from $\bigcap U$, by the equivalence just proved. These two maps are inverse and reverse inclusions. It follows that the lattice is distributive. Its meet is intersection. Its join is the inverse image of the solvable radical of $G/(K_1K_2)$, by the fact that every epimorphism to a Fitting-free group kills the solvable radical and the quotient by that radical is Fitting-free.

The order on $P(G)$ can be recovered from the table by computing a chief series, computing its nonabelian chief-factor centralizers, deduplicating and testing inclusions. A chief series can be constructed by repeatedly choosing a smallest nontrivial normal closure in the current quotient. Each step and each centralizer computation uses polynomially many table operations, and there are at most $\log_2|G|$ steps.

The join in $\mathcal Q(G)$ can differ from the product of the two kernels. In the subgroup $\{(x,y)\in S_5^2:\operatorname{sgn}(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(y)\}$, the two coordinate $A_5$'s have individual quotients isomorphic to $S_5$, whereas their product gives quotient $C_2$. The join in $\mathcal Q(G)$ is then $G$. For $G_C$ above, the chief series can first adjoin its coordinate $A_5$'s, after which all chief factors are abelian. Their centralizers are exactly the various coordinate-projection kernels, an antichain. Thus $\mathcal Q(G_C)$ is a Boolean lattice independently of $C$, even though deciding which of its quotients has a prescribed isomorphism type is NP-complete in the permutation-generator model.

## 5. Relation to the classical lattice realization theorem

Every finite distributive lattice occurs, as is already implicit in Silcock's normal subgroup lattice theorem using generalized wreath products of nonabelian simple groups. There is also a short construction in terms of the canonical poset just described. Let $P$ be a finite poset, and work in an order in which all elements above a new element $x$ have already been inserted. Suppose $G$ realizes the old poset by its chief-factor centralizers $C_y$. Let $K=\bigcap_{y>x}C_y$, let $Q=G/K$, and replace $G$ by the semidirect product $A_5^{Q}\rtimes G$, where $G$ acts on the coordinates by the regular action of $Q$. The base group is a minimal normal subgroup by transitivity. Its centralizer consists exactly of the embedded $K$, since an element centralizing the base cannot permute its coordinates and a base element centralizing it must be in its trivial center. The previous chief series lifts after this base, with previous centralizers replaced by their preimages from $G$. Hence the new centralizer is contained in a previous one exactly when $K\leq C_y$, equivalently $y>x$ by meet-primality and upper closure. No previous centralizer is contained in the new one because each previous one contains the base. Induction realizes $P$. Every composition factor in the resulting group is $A_5$, so each normal quotient is Fitting-free and $\mathcal Q(G)$ is its ordinary normal subgroup lattice. Birkhoff's representation by lower sets and the upper-set description above give every finite distributive lattice. [Silcock, *Generalized wreath products and the lattice of normal subgroups of a group*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02485445)

The closest references distinguish the input models. Grochow, Johnson, and Levet analyze isomorphism of the groups $G_C$, which corresponds to selecting all coordinates. Grochow, Srivastava, and Thakkar formulate the epimorphism problem with Cayley tables. The graph reductions above produce permutation generators of polynomial length, and the groups can have order exponential in the number of coordinates. They therefore give hardness for the succinct model. [Grochow–Johnson–Levet](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19777v1#S8) · [Grochow–Srivastava–Thakkar](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07429v1)
