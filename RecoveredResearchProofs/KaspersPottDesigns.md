# Kaspers–Pott's Galois-ring and finite-field designs

**Theorem.** Let $p$ be an odd prime, let $r\ge 1$, and set $q=p^r$. The designs $\operatorname{dev}(E^H)$ and $\operatorname{dev}(C^H)$ of Kaspers and Pott are nonisomorphic for every $q\ge 7$. At $q=5$ both are complete $2$-$(25,2,1)$ designs and hence isomorphic. The constructions have block size one at $q=3$.

This determines the entire design range of the conjecture in §5. The original Theorem 4.12 assumes $q-1\equiv0\pmod{24}$ and $2^{p-1}\not\equiv1\pmod{p^2}$. The proof is informal and uses a reproducible exact enumeration for the isolated case $q=9$. [Kaspers–Pott, Theorems 3.2, 3.4, 4.12 and §5](https://arxiv.org/html/1907.05848v2)

## 1. The blocks and the field-side symmetry

Write $R=\operatorname{GR}(p^2,r)$, $I=pR$, and let $\tau\colon\mathbb F_q\to R$ be the Teichmüller lift. Every element has unique form $\tau(a)+p\tau(b)$, and $b\mapsto p\tau(b)$ identifies the additive groups of $\mathbb F_q$ and $I$. These facts follow because $I$ is generated by $p$, is its own annihilator, and is the kernel of reduction to the field. The lift $\tau$ is multiplicative.

Let $S$ be the subgroup of squares of the nonzero Teichmüller representatives. The ring-side blocks are all translates of $uS$ for $u\in R\setminus\{0\}$. For units $u$ call these unit blocks. Reduction is injective on any unit block because it is injective on $S$. The other blocks, called ideal blocks, are contained in single cosets of $I$. Inside each coset they are identified with translates of the nonzero squares or nonsquares of $\mathbb F_q$. Both designs have block size $(q-1)/2$ and pair multiplicity $\lambda=(q-3)/2$ for $q\ge5$ by the source constructions.

The field-side subgroup consists of the $2(q+1)$-th powers in $\mathbb F_{q^2}^{\times}$. It has order $(q-1)/2$, so by cyclicity it is exactly the squares in $\mathbb F_q^{\times}$. The field-side blocks are translates of $u(\mathbb F_q^\times)^2$ for nonzero $u$ in the two-dimensional $\mathbb F_q$-space $\mathbb F_{q^2}$. Every $\mathbb F_q$-linear bijection preserves this block family. Together with translations these maps act transitively on ordered pairs of distinct points. It suffices to show that the ring-side automorphism group fails to be 2-transitive.

## 2. Unit-block intersections

For $a,b\in\mathbb F_q$ define the carry $\kappa(a,b)$ by
$$
 \tau(a)-\tau(b)=\tau(a-b)+p\tau(\kappa(a,b)).
$$
Put $A=a^{1/p}$ and $B=b^{1/p}$ using inverse Frobenius. Then
$$
 \kappa(a,b)=C(A,B),\qquad C(X,Y)=-\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\frac{X^iY^{p-i}}{i}.
$$
Indeed, put $x=\tau(A)$, $y=\tau(B)$. The mixed terms in $(x-y)^p$ have coefficients $p\binom{p-1}{i-1}/i$ multiplying $x^i(-y)^{p-i}$. After dividing the coefficients by $p$ and reducing modulo $p$, the sign in $\binom{p-1}{i-1}\equiv(-1)^{i-1}$ cancels the sign in $(-y)^{p-i}$. Subtracting the mixed terms gives the claimed negative sum. Also $(x-y)^p=\tau(A-B)^p=\tau(a-b)$ because $x-y$ differs from $\tau(A-B)$ by an ideal element and its $p$-th power eliminates the difference modulo $p^2$.

The intersection of two unit blocks counts solutions of $s-ut=h$ with $s,t\in S$, $u\in R^\times$ and $h\in R$. Write $u=\zeta(1+p\tau(\alpha))$ with $\zeta$ a nonzero Teichmüller lift, and write $h=\tau(h_0)+p\tau(h_1)$. Put $a=s\bmod I$ and $b=\zeta t\bmod I$. The equations are $a-b=h_0$ and $\kappa(a,b)-\alpha b=h_1$. Given $a,b$, there is at most one pair $s,t$.

If $h_0\ne0$, put $H=h_0^{1/p}$. The equation for $X=a^{1/p}$ becomes
$$
 C(X,X-H)-\alpha(X-H)^p-h_1=0.
$$
It has degree at most $p$ and coefficient $H$ in degree $p-1$. Indeed the $X^p$ coefficient of $C(X,X-H)$ is $-\sum_i i^{-1}=0$, and its $X^{p-1}$ coefficient is $H\sum_i(p-i)/i=H$ in $\mathbb F_p$. The $p$-th power contributes nothing in degree $p-1$. The nonzero polynomial has at most $p$ roots. If $h_0=0$, then $C(X,X)=0$ and the remaining equation is $-\alpha a=h_1$. It has at most one solution when $\alpha\ne0$, and none when $\alpha=0$, $h_1\ne0$. When both vanish, the translated blocks have the same shift and their Teichmüller cosets either coincide or are disjoint. Thus two distinct unit blocks intersect in at most $p$ points. A unit block and an ideal block meet in at most one point, by injectivity of reduction on the former.

## 3. Extension degree at least two

For a block $D$ let $m(D)$ be its largest intersection with a distinct block. Thus $m(D)\le p$ for unit blocks. For an ideal block, the sum of its intersection sizes with its $q-1$ nonzero ideal translates is $|D|(|D|-1)$, by counting ordered pairs of distinct elements. Each nonzero translate is distinct from $D$ since $|D|$ is prime to $p$. Hence $m(D)\ge \lceil(q-3)/4\rceil$. When $q>4p+3$, these bounds distinguish the two classes. Every block through $\{0,1\}$ is a unit block, while every block through $\{0,p\}$ is an ideal block. The two multisets of $m(D)$ values differ, so no automorphism transports one pair to the other.

For $r\ge2$ the inequality holds except at $p=3,r=2$. At $q=9$ use the number of blocks disjoint from every block through a given point pair. This is again invariant. In $R=(\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z)[t]/(t^2+1)$, the counts for $\{0,1\}$ and $\{0,3\}$ are $1047$ and $1026$, respectively. The companion [finite check](check_kaspers_pott.py) constructs the 1,620 blocks by finding the nonzero roots of $x^9=x$, squaring them, multiplying the resulting set by each nonzero ring element, and translating each resulting block by every ring element. It verifies the counts directly.

## 4. Extension degree one

The translation group on $R=\mathbb Z/p^2\mathbb Z$ is regular cyclic of degree $p^2$. Suppose $p\ge7$ and the ring-side automorphism group is 2-transitive. By Jones's classification of primitive groups with a regular cyclic subgroup, only the alternating/symmetric and projective cases can have this degree. The affine case has prime degree, and the remaining exceptions have degrees 11 or 23. The alternating/symmetric case would make every $(p-1)/2$-element subset a block. The construction has at most $2p^2(p+1)$ blocks, while even $\binom{p^2}{3}>2p^2(p+1)$ for $p\ge7$. [Jones, Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1209.5169#S1)

In the projective case $p^2=(s^d-1)/(s-1)$ for a prime power $s$, $d\ge2$, and the permutation group contains $\operatorname{PGL}(d,s)$ in its projective action. If $d=2$, then $s=(p-1)(p+1)$, so both even factors must be powers of two. The only positive pair of powers of two differing by two is $2,4$, giving $p=3$. If $d>2$, Ljunggren's square-repunit theorem gives only $(s,d,p)=(3,5,11)$ or $(7,4,20)$. The latter has composite $p$. [Ljunggren's square case, as recorded by Bugeaud and Mihăilescu](https://doi.org/10.7146/math.scand.a-15038)

The sole remaining candidate contains $\operatorname{PGL}(5,3)$ acting on the 121 points of $\operatorname{PG}(4,3)$. The block size is five and the total number of blocks is at most 2,904. Every orbit of a five-point subset exceeds this size. Its vector-span dimension $j$ is between 3 and 5 because a projective line has four points. For $j=3$ or $j=4$, the projective group acts transitively on the $j$-dimensional subspaces and their stabilizers induce the full projective groups on those subspaces. Each within-span orbit covers every point pair in the span, by 2-transitivity, so has size at least $\binom{(3^j-1)/2}{2}/10$. There are 1,210 possible spans for $j=3$ and 121 for $j=4$, by Gaussian binomial coefficients. Thus the orbit size is at least $1210\cdot 78/10>2904$ or $121\cdot 780/10>2904$, respectively. If $j=5$, the five points are independent lines, and the group is transitive on all unordered independent five-sets. There are $(3^5-1)(3^5-3)(3^5-3^2)(3^5-3^3)(3^5-3^4)/(2^5\,5!)>2904$ such sets by counting ordered independent vectors. The block set cannot contain any full orbit. This rules out the remaining possibility. The finite check also independently distinguishes the pairs $\{0,1\}$ and $\{0,11\}$ using $m(D)$.

Finally at $q=5$ each design has block size two and pair multiplicity one, so every two-element subset is a block. At $q=3$ the developments consist of singletons and cover no pairs.
