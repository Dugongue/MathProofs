# Graph isomorphism for the three-orbit symmetry-characterized branch

**Theorem 1.** Let \(X\) be a symmetry-characterized graph whose full automorphism group has exactly three orbits on unordered pairs of distinct vertices. Given the raw adjacency matrices of \(X\) and any graph \(Y\), one can decide \(X\cong Y\) in deterministic polynomial time. In fact, outside an absolute finite set of orders, every graph in the promised class is a cubic Paley graph or its complement over a finite field of characteristic \(1\bmod 3\). The promised class can also be recognized in polynomial time from its adjacency matrix. This concerns the three-orbit branch of Grochow–Urisman's Open Question 1.9. [Original question](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26244v2#S1.SS3)

**Theorem 2.** Given two raw adjacency matrices, if one graph is an affine ovoid graph or its complement, one can decide isomorphism to the other graph in deterministic polynomial time. In particular this covers both affine rank-three graph families at the Suzuki–Tits versus elliptic polar parameter collision. Consequently isomorphism is in deterministic polynomial time when both graphs are promised to admit affine rank-three actions.

The sentence preceding Open Question 1.9 suggests that all symmetry-characterized graphs other than stars, their complements, and trivial graphs have permutation rank three. The seven-cycle shows why the three-orbit branch must be included. Its automorphism group has three orbits on distinct unordered pairs, corresponding to cycle distances \(1,2,3\). Each distance graph is another seven-cycle, and each two-orbit union is a complement of a seven-cycle. The automorphism group has permutation rank four on ordered pairs, counting the diagonal. Theorem 1 gives the general algorithmic result for this branch. [Grochow–Urisman, §§1.1 and 1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26244v2)

## 1. The three-orbit branch is a cyclic homogeneous factorization

A graph is symmetry-characterized if every graph on the same vertex set fixed by \(\Gamma=\operatorname{Aut}(X)\) is isomorphic to the empty graph, the complete graph, \(X\), or \(\overline X\). Every such graph is a union of the \(\Gamma\)-orbits on unordered pairs. Suppose there are exactly three nonempty pair orbits \(E_0,E_1,E_2\), and put \(s_i=|E_i|\), \(N=\binom n2\), \(a=|E(X)|\). Every single orbit and every two-orbit union has size \(a\) or \(N-a\). Write \(b=\min(a,N-a)\), \(c=\max(a,N-a)\). Each \(s_i\) is \(b\) or \(c\), with no size-\(c\) orbit possible because \(c+2b>b+c=N\). Thus \(s_i=N/3\) for all \(i\), and the three single-orbit graphs are isomorphic, all to the same one of \(X,\overline X\).

The full automorphism group of each single-orbit graph is \(\Gamma\). It contains \(\Gamma\), and its order equals \(|\Gamma|\) since it is isomorphic to \(X\) or its complement. An isomorphism between two factors therefore normalizes \(\Gamma\). Consequently \(\Sigma=N_{S_n}(\Gamma)\) is transitive on factors. Its stabilizer of each factor is exactly \(\Gamma\), so it induces the **regular cyclic group of order three** on the factors. This last condition uses full automorphism groups, which matters when applying the classification.

Also \(\Gamma\) is vertex-transitive. If it has \(t\ge3\) vertex orbits, the \(\binom t2\) cross-orbit classes already account for all three pair orbits when \(t=3\), so no vertex orbit can have size greater than one. This gives \(n=3\), where the single-edge factors cannot have trivial full automorphism group. If \(t=2\) and both vertex orbits have size \(r,s\ge2\), the two within-orbit classes and the cross class must have equal sizes. The first equality \(\binom r2=\binom s2\) gives \(r=s\), and then \(rs>\binom r2\). If one orbit is a singleton, the cross class contains \(n-1\) pairs and equality with \(N/3\) forces \(n=6\). The factor through the singleton is a star, while a factor omitting those five pairs leaves that vertex isolated, so those factors cannot be isomorphic.

Thus \((\Sigma,\Gamma)\) acts on an edge-transitive homogeneous factorization of the complete graph into three factors. The classification of Li–Lim–Praeger, Theorem 1.1, provides acting subgroups \(G\le S_n\), \(M\unlhd G\) for this same factorization, with \(G\) 2-transitive and \(M\) transitive on the vertices and edges of each factor. Outside a fixed finite set of degrees the groups are one-dimensional affine semilinear over \(\mathbb F_q\), \(q=n\), with all translations in \(M\). To apply the subgroup statement, every permutation preserving the factor partition normalizes \(\Gamma\), since it conjugates any factor's full automorphism group to the image factor's full automorphism group. The \(G\)-action on factors therefore has image exactly \(C_3\). Replace \(M\) by the kernel of that action if necessary. [Li–Lim–Praeger, Theorem 1.1 and §4](https://emis.de/ft/43761)

## 2. The semilinear classification at cyclic index three

At the point \(0\in\mathbb F_q\) let \(L=G_0\) be the point stabilizer, and label nonzero vectors by \(x=\omega^j\), \(j\in\mathbb Z/(q-1)\), for a primitive field element \(\omega\). Each unordered factor is a Cayley graph, since its automorphisms contain the translations. Thus its neighborhood of \(0\) has exactly the differences of that factor. The three neighborhoods have equal sizes by vertex transitivity and the equality of the edge counts, so \(3\mid q-1\). The neighborhood colors \(c(j)\) form the \(L\)-equivariant map from the exponents to \(\mathbb Z/3\). The color action \(\chi:L\to\mathbb Z/3\) is surjective because translations act trivially on colors and \(G\) acts transitively on them. Accordingly \(c(gj)=c(j)+\chi(g)\).

The scalars in \(L\) are the translations of the exponent by multiples of a divisor \(D\mid(q-1)\). If \(L\) contains only scalars then transitivity forces \(D=1\) and the conclusion follows. Otherwise one can take \(L=\langle T,F\rangle\), where \(T(j)=j+D\) and \(F(j)=t j+b\) for \(t=p^s\), \(p=\operatorname{char}\mathbb F_q\). Indeed the quotient of the semilinear group by the scalars is cyclic. On \(\mathbb Z/D\) the permutation \(F\) is a single cycle by transitivity of \(L\) on exponents. Consequently for every prime \(\ell\mid D\), \(t\equiv1\pmod\ell\) and \(b\not\equiv0\pmod\ell\). Otherwise the induced affine permutation on \(\mathbb F_\ell\) would have a fixed point and could not be transitive.

If \(\chi(T)=0\), the color depends only on the exponent modulo \(D\). Then \(\chi(F)\ne0\) and the \(D\)-th power of \(F\) fixes the exponent modulo \(D\), so \(3\mid D\). Modulo three, \(F(j)\equiv j+b\) with \(b\ne0\). Along the single orbit of \(0\) modulo \(D\), this shows \(c(j)=c(0)+\chi(F)b^{-1}j\) modulo three.

If \(\chi(T)\ne0\), the relation \(FTF^{-1}=T^t\) gives \(t\equiv1\bmod3\). If \(3\mid D\), then \(F^D(0)=b(1+t+\cdots+t^{D-1})\) is a multiple of \(D\), and its quotient by \(D\) is nonzero modulo three. In fact \(b\not\equiv0\bmod3\) and \(v_3(1+t+\cdots+t^{D-1})=v_3(D)\) by the lifting-the-exponent identity. The identity also holds for \(t=1\) with the sum interpreted directly. The color shift of \(F^D(0)\) would then be nonzero by the shift action of \(T\), while \(\chi(F^D)=0\). Hence \(3\nmid D\). Set \(u=\chi(T)D^{-1}\) modulo three. Comparing the colors of \(0\) and \(F^D(0)\), first by applying \(F^D\) and then by shifting with \(T\), now yields
$$
D\chi(F)=uF^D(0)=ubD\pmod3,
$$
so \(\chi(F)=ub\). Both \(F\) and \(T\) therefore change \(c(j)\) by \(u\) times their change of the exponent modulo three. Transitivity gives \(c(j)=c(0)+uj\) for all exponents.

In both cases the factor differences are the three cubic cosets. These factors are isomorphic by scalars, giving the cubic Paley graph \(P_3(q)\) and its complement as the only possible models for \(X\) outside finitely many orders. If \(p\equiv2\bmod3\), Frobenius fixes the cube coset and exchanges the other two. It is then an automorphism of the cubic Paley graph that merges two factors into one pair orbit, contrary to the three-orbit hypothesis. Also \(p=3\) is impossible because \(3\mid q-1\). Thus \(p\equiv1\bmod3\).

Conversely, for \(p\equiv1\bmod3\) and all sufficiently large \(q\), the full graph automorphism group is contained in \(\mathrm{A\Gamma L}(1,q)\) by Ponomarenko's Theorem 1.1. The translation and cube multiplication subgroups are present. Every semilinear automorphism preserving the adjacency differences maps each cubic coset to itself, because its scalar is a cube and Frobenius acts trivially on the three cosets. Thus there are exactly three pair orbits. A primitive scalar rotates the three factors. Every invariant union is then one of the four required isomorphism types, establishing symmetry-characterization. [Ponomarenko, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18304v1)

## 3. The raw-adjacency algorithm for Theorem 1

For \(n\) in the finite exceptional range of the classification or Ponomarenko's theorem, exhaustive enumeration of permutations is a fixed constant bound in an asymptotic polynomial-time algorithm. This works for isomorphism and recognition, by directly computing the full automorphism group and testing its three pair orbits and their unions. Otherwise construct the cubic Paley template at \(q=n\) if \(n\) is a prime power of characteristic \(1\bmod3\). Finite-field construction and finding a primitive element take time polynomial in \(n\) by exhaustive coefficient and element search. Ponomarenko's Theorem 1.2 gives Weisfeiler–Leman dimension at most five for the fixed cyclotomic index \(3\) at all sufficiently large \(q\). This identifies the template against any graph of its order, including an otherwise unrestricted graph supplied by adjacency matrix. Compare each input to the template, complementing graphs of complementary degree first. Their degrees \((q-1)/3\) and \(2(q-1)/3\) distinguish these possibilities. The resulting test both recognizes the promised class and decides isomorphism of a promised graph with an arbitrary second input.

Ponomarenko explicitly defines identification against *all* nonisomorphic graphs in the paragraph before Theorem 1.2. Remark 5.2 gives explicit bounds for the estimates used in Sections 6.1–6.3, and Theorem 1.1 of Li–Lim–Praeger lists the possible non-semilinear degrees in its finite tables. Thus the finite exceptional branch can use a uniform fixed threshold. Brute force on that branch gives an asymptotic bound. [Cyclotomic identification theorem and proof](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18304v1)

## 4. Recognizing affine ovoid graphs against arbitrary inputs

Let \(O\) be an ovoid in \(\mathrm{PG}(3,q)\). There are \(q^2+1\) points in \(O\), at most two on a projective line, and a projective plane meeting \(O\) in at least two points meets it in exactly \(q+1\). The affine ovoid graph has vertex set \(V=\mathbb F_q^4\) and adjacency when the direction \(\langle y-x\rangle\) lies in \(O\). The Suzuki–Tits ovoid and the elliptic quadric give the two affine rank-three families mentioned above. For \(q<5\) exhaustive isomorphism testing has an absolute fixed bound. [Ovoids in projective three-space](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04126) · [The affine rank-three families and the parameter collision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14696)

**Recover lines and parallelism.** Every graph edge \(xy\) identifies the line \(L_{xy}=\{x,y\}\cup(N(x)\cap N(y))\). Every other point of the affine line through \(x,y\) is in the common neighborhood. A common neighbor off the line would produce three different directions in \(O\) on the same projective line, from the sides of a noncollinear affine triangle. This contradicts the ovoid condition. Deduplicating these sets enumerates all affine lines of the \(O\)-directions.

For disjoint lines \(x+\mathbb F_q a\) and \(y+\mathbb F_q b\) of different directions, \(w=y-x\) is outside the span of \(a,b\). The map

$$
(s,t)\longmapsto \langle w+t b-s a\rangle
$$

is a bijection between their \(q^2\) cross pairs and the projective plane \(\mathrm{PG}(\langle a,b,w\rangle)\) minus its line \(\mathrm{PG}(\langle a,b\rangle)\). The plane has \(q+1\) ovoid points, and the removed line contains exactly two of them. Thus exactly \(q-1\) graph edges run between the two lines. For two distinct parallel lines, take \(b=a\). The difference is \(w+(t-s)a\) and its direction ranges over the projective line \(\mathrm{PG}(\langle a,w\rangle)\) minus \(\langle a\rangle\). This set contains zero or one ovoid point. Each difference occurs for \(q\) pairs, so their number of cross edges is zero or \(q\). Intersecting distinct lines cannot be parallel. This recovers the parallel classes using only the adjacency matrix.

**Recover a field from a fixed-size frame.** Choose an origin \(o\) and four independent directions \(D_1,\ldots,D_4\) from \(O\). They exist because no projective plane contains \(O\). A fifth direction \(D_5\) can be chosen outside all four coordinate hyperplanes: they contain at most \(4(q+1)<q^2+1\) ovoid points when \(q\ge5\). Enumerate these choices from the recovered parallel classes, which takes polynomial time since the tuple size is fixed. Write \(L_i\) for the line of direction \(D_i\) through \(o\). The relation generated by lines of directions \(D_j\), \(j\ne i\), has classes that meet \(L_i\) once, giving a computable \(i\)-th coordinate for every vertex. Projection of \(L_5\) onto every \(L_i\) is bijective. Use these four bijections to identify the four coordinate alphabets with \(A=L_1\), so that \(L_5\) has coordinates \((t,t,t,t)\).

In these coordinates, the line of class \(D_5\) through \((a,0,0,0)\) has a unique point with second coordinate \(b\), whose first coordinate defines \(a+b\). This recovers field addition on \(A\) after the frame normalization. For every recovered direction whose line through \(o\) projects bijectively onto the first axis, read the second coordinate on that line as a map from the first coordinate. On a true ovoid graph these maps are scalar multiplications by the slopes \(m=x_2/x_1\). At least \(q^2-q\) ovoid directions have \(x_1\ne0\). Each slope occurs at most \(q+1\) times, by the projective plane bound applied to \(x_2=m x_1\). Thus at least \(q-1\) distinct slopes occur. A proper additive subgroup of \(\mathbb F_q\) has size at most \(q/2\), so the additive span of the maps under pointwise addition is the full scalar field. Compute the span by closure under addition, stopping if it grows past \(q\). Choose a nonzero element \(u\) on the first axis. Evaluation at \(u\) is a bijection from the scalar maps onto \(A\), and composition recovers multiplication on \(A\), with \(u\) representing the unit.

**Decision algorithm and correctness.** Given graphs of a common order \(n=q^4\), perform the construction on both adjacency matrices, testing the graph pair and the pair of complements. Validate each proposed frame by the partition, projection, and bijection conditions above and verify the recovered addition and multiplication are field operations. For each pair of successful frames and their choices of \(u\), enumerate the isomorphisms of the recovered fields in polynomial time in \(q\). For example, find a generator of the first field's multiplicative group by testing each element, try its image among the second field's elements, and verify the resulting power map preserves addition and multiplication. Apply each field map componentwise to the coordinate tuples, and accept only on direct verification of an adjacency-preserving bijection. Construction uses only polynomially many sets, relations, frames, and maps. If one graph is an affine ovoid graph and an isomorphism exists, the isomorphism carries its recovered lines and parallelism to those of the other graph. A good frame and its image are both enumerated. Every recovery step is preserved when transferring the frame and \(u\), so the true isomorphism appears among the tested bijections. Soundness on any input follows from the final direct adjacency check.

## 5. The pair promise for affine rank three

Suppose both graphs admit affine rank-three actions, and compare orders and valencies. Except when the graph is trivial, an imprimitive rank-three action has its within-block pairs as one of the two off-diagonal orbitals. Thus the graph is a union of equal cliques or its complement, decided directly. In the primitive case apply Skresanov's Proposition 6. Outside finitely many orders, the subdegrees distinguish affine rank-three graph types up to complement except for Paley versus Peisert and Suzuki–Tits versus elliptic polar. For unequal subdegrees the marked adjacency valency fixes whether to use the graph or its complement. At equal subdegrees, the one-dimensional families in the proposition are self-complementary, and any remaining bounded exceptions can be tested exhaustively. In the Paley versus Peisert case, compare each input with the explicit Paley model using Ponomarenko's identification theorem at fixed index \(2\). If both tests fail, the promise identifies both with the one remaining Peisert type. In the Suzuki–Tits versus elliptic polar case, use the affine ovoid isomorphism algorithm above. Thus both promised inputs can be compared in polynomial time. [Skresanov, Proposition 6 and Lemma 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.14696#page=10) · [Ponomarenko, Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18304v1)

The full two-unordered-orbit branch of Open Question 1.9 remains open. The results here cover every three-orbit graph, isomorphism against arbitrary inputs for the affine ovoid families, and the two-sided promise for affine rank-three actions.
