# Recovering projective incidence from classical point and Grassmann graphs

There is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm, uniform in all parameters, that recognizes Grassmann graphs on the \(k\)-subspaces of \(\mathbb F_q^d\) for \(2\le k\le d-2\), and alternating symplectic orthogonality graphs on all projective points of \(\mathbb F_q^{2r}\), together with their complements. It recovers a labeled standard model. This also gives isomorphism search between a promised member and any graph. The method combines raw coordinate recovery with projective incidence recognized inside the graph. It extends cases of Grochow–Urisman Question 1.9.

## Grassmann graphs

Two vertices \(U,V\) are adjacent if their intersection has vector dimension \(k-1\). For an edge put \(P=U\cap V\), \(Q=U+V\). The common closed neighbors are the union of the star of \(P\), consisting of \(k\)-spaces containing it, and the top of \(Q\), consisting of \(k\)-spaces contained in it. Indeed if a common neighbor does not contain \(P\), its intersections with \(U,V\) span it inside \(Q\). A star-exclusive neighbor meets \(Q\) exactly in \(P\); its intersection with a top-exclusive neighbor has dimension \(k-2\). Thus the complementary graph on the common closed neighbors consists of one complete bipartite component on the exclusive sides, and isolated vertices in the overlap. Both exclusive sides are nonempty in the stated range. This recovers the star and top from the edge. Every maximal clique is a star or top, since a clique containing an edge must lie within one of those two sets. Thus scanning edges and deduplicating gives all maximal cliques.

Join maximal cliques when their overlap has at least two vertices. Two stars or two tops meet in at most one. A star and top meet in \(q+1\) vertices exactly when their defining subspaces are incident. This gives a connected bipartite graph. Indeed any two \((k-1)\)-spaces can be joined by basis replacements, and consecutive spaces of this sequence are in some common \((k+1)\)-space. Every top is incident with a star. Choose the side with the larger clique cardinality, swapping to the dual model if necessary. These are the stars after taking \(k\le d/2\); a tie permits either orientation. Intersecting stars gives the adjacency of the \((k-1)\)-subspace graph. Retain its incidence with the current vertices and recurse. The number of \(j\)-spaces is nondecreasing up to \(d/2\), by the Gaussian binomial formula. Thus no level exceeds the original number of vertices. The complete graph at the last level consists of projective points, with the previous level supplying projective lines and their incidents. The retained incidence maps recover the point sets of every original vertex.

## Coordinatizing projective incidence

In a verified model these operations use just the recovered point-line incidence. Compute spans by closing under lines, and choose a projective basis by repeatedly adding a point outside the current span. Choose a hyperplane, a point \(O\) outside, and independent directions on the hyperplane. Pick unit points on the corresponding affine axes. In the plane of the first two axes, use parallels to the line joining their unit points to identify the coordinates \((a,0)\) and \((0,a)\). The grid intersection \((a,b)\) projects back along the same direction to \((a+b,0)\). The line through \(O\) and \((1,a)\), intersected with the vertical at \(b\), gives \((b,ab)\), and horizontal projection extracts \(ab\). This recovers field addition and multiplication on the labels of an axis. Coordinates of other affine points are obtained by projecting along the coordinate hyperplanes parallel to the span of all but one axis. Points at infinity take the direction of a line through \(O\). These constructions are correct by the projective coordinatization theorem or directly by applying an invertible linear change of basis in the original model. Each step is a polynomial number of incidence scans. Rank, field size, and recovered point sets are checked by final bijection and literal adjacency against the field model.

## Symplectic point graphs

Extend orthogonality by adding each point to its own closed neighborhood. For distinct points \(x,y\), the common closed neighborhood consists of the projective points of \(\langle x,y\rangle^\perp\). Taking a second common closed neighborhood gives all points of the projective line \(\langle x,y\rangle\), by nondegeneracy. Recover the point-line structure and coordinates as above. Solve homogeneous linear equations for an alternating form requiring every prescribed orthogonality to vanish. On promised input this solution space is one-dimensional. For if \(B_0\) is the true form, each covector \(B(x,-)\) of a candidate vanishes on the hyperplane \(B_0(x,-)=0\), and is proportional to \(B_0(x,-)\). Nondegeneracy of \(B_0\) turns this into a linear map taking each line into itself, hence a scalar by comparing independent vectors and their sum. This also handles zero candidate covectors. Take any nonzero solution and verify nondegeneracy and all adjacencies. An alternating form can be normalized by choosing successive symplectic pairs. In vector dimension two the graph is empty on \(q+1\) vertices, which can be treated directly.

On malformed inputs impose the vertex-count caps at every descent, test incidence operations for uniqueness, and reject when intermediate choices or the final model checks fail. At each descent also require the vertex count to decrease strictly, as it does on the valid models. Enumerate subspaces for the Grassmann standard model by extending row-reduced spans one vector at a time and deduplicating at each rank, with the same caps. The recovered field can be put in a fixed standard presentation by finding a generator and trying its possible images in that presentation. Polynomial-time formation and the complete final checks ensure that an accepted input is genuinely in the family. Applying the procedures to the complement adds the complementary families. For the one-promised-input isomorphism problem, retain one verified standard model from the promised input, and require the other input to reach that model. Coordinate bijections then give the desired isomorphism. This proof is informal. [Grochow–Urisman, Question 1.9](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26244v2#S1.SS3)
