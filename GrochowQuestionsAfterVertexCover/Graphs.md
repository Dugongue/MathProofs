# Recognizing affine rank-three graphs from raw adjacency matrices

**Informal theorem.** There are deterministic polynomial-time recognition algorithms, from an arbitrary raw adjacency matrix, for the following individually specified graph models and their complements.

- All bilinear-forms graphs $H_q(2,e)$, where $q$ is any prime power and $e\ge 2$.
- The affine expansions $VJ_q(5,2)$ and $VD_{5,5}(q)$ of the line Grassmann and half-spin geometries, over every prime power $q$.
- The affine orthogonal graphs $VO^+_{2s}(q)$ for $s\ge 3$ and $VO^-_{2s}(q)$ for $s\ge 4$, over every prime power $q$. In the binary case the same algorithmic conclusion holds for both signs for every $s\ge 2$.

Given a graph promised to be isomorphic to some model in this list and a completely arbitrary second graph, these tests decide whether the two graphs are isomorphic in polynomial time. Neither input carries coordinates, automorphism generators, a group action, or a family designation. This extends the one-input promise portion of Grochow–Urisman's Open Question 1.9 to the listed series. Their full question and unrestricted recognition remain open. [Grochow–Urisman, §1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26244v2#S1.SS3).

## The local-to-global step

For a graph $C$, its $b$-clique extension replaces every vertex by a $b$-clique, with complete joins along its edges. Two vertices are true twins if their closed neighborhoods coincide. When $C$ has no true twins, the true-twin classes in its $b$-clique extension are exactly the copies of its vertices. Thus a raw graph is such an extension of a given twin-free model if and only if every true-twin class has size $b$, its quotient has that model, and adjacencies agree with the clique-extension rule. These conditions can be checked directly in polynomial time.

For $q>2$, Mitrović–Schillewaert–Van Maldeghem's Theorem A applies to $H_q(2,e)$ with $e\ge 2$, $VJ_q(5,2)$, $VD_{5,5}(q)$, and $VO^\pm_{2s}(q)$ with $s\ge 3$. They identify the graph by the induced graph on the neighbors of every vertex, together with constancy of the number of common neighbors of nonadjacent vertices. We also check connectivity, the vertex count, and the exact values of all strongly regular graph parameters by direct enumeration. For the four types of graphs in this theorem, the neighborhood is the $(q-1)$-clique extension, respectively, of the rectangular rook graph, the line Grassmann point graph, the half-spin point graph, or the projective orthogonal polar point graph of the same sign. The neighborhood identifications below turn these structural hypotheses into raw-input algorithms. [Mitrović–Schillewaert–Van Maldeghem, Table 1 and Theorem A](https://cage.ugent.be/~hvm/artikels/280.pdf).

Use the individual conclusions of Theorems 5.2 and 7.2 for these inputs. Theorem 5.2 treats the projective polar neighborhoods, including the $e=2$ rectangular case because the $2\times2$ matrix determinant is a split quadratic form on four coordinates. Theorem 7.2 treats the other rectangular cases, the affine Grassmann graphs, and the affine half-spin graphs. Checking the same required local model at every vertex makes each theorem applicable directly. [Mitrović–Schillewaert–Van Maldeghem, Theorems 5.2 and 7.2](https://cage.ugent.be/~hvm/artikels/280.pdf).

## Bilinear forms graphs

At the zero matrix in $H_q(2,e)$, the neighbors are the nonzero rank-one matrices $uv^\top$, with $u\in\mathbb F_q^2$, $v\in\mathbb F_q^e$. The difference of two nonzero rank-one matrices has rank at most one exactly when their left factors are proportional or their right factors are proportional. If both pairs of factors are independent, applying independent changes of basis gives a rank-two difference. For each pair of projective points $([u],[v])$ there are $q-1$ nonzero matrices. Therefore the local graph is the $(q-1)$-clique extension of the rook graph with sides $q+1$ and $(q^e-1)/(q-1)$.

Rook graph recognition is explicit. Choose a vertex $a$. Its neighbors must induce two disjoint nonempty cliques of the required sizes, up to switching the two sides. These cliques together with $a$ supply the two distinguished axes. Each vertex outside both axes, that is, outside $N[a]$, must have precisely one neighbor in each clique. Use those two neighbors as its row and column labels. Check that each row-column pair occurs once and that the entire adjacency relation is exactly equality of a row or a column. Both sides are at least three, so the rook graph is twin-free. Translation by matrices shows that the same local graph occurs at every vertex. For $q>2$, the local-to-global theorem now proves recognition sound and complete. [Mitrović–Schillewaert–Van Maldeghem, Theorems A, 5.2 and 7.2](https://cage.ugent.be/~hvm/artikels/280.pdf).

For $q=2$, the local graph is the rook graph itself. In addition to all local checks, require diameter two, exactly $2^{2e}$ vertices, and the model's intersection numbers $b_0=3(2^e-1)$, $b_1=2(2^e-2)$, $b_2=0$, and $c_2=6$. Theorem 1.2 in the cited paper gives a covering by $H_2(2,e)$. A surjective covering between graphs of the same finite order is an isomorphism. This includes $e=2$. [The binary bilinear-forms characterization, Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.09435#page=4).

## Line Grassmann and half-spin graphs

We first recognize the line-intersection graph $J_q(d+1,2)$ of $\mathrm{PG}(d,q)$ for every $d\ge4$. The affine Grassmann and half-spin algorithms below use $d=4$. Write $N[x]$ for a closed neighborhood. In the input graph enumerate each triangle $X,Y,Z$ of distinct vertices and form $N[X]\cap N[Y]\cap N[Z]$. Retain the distinct intersections of size $1+q+\cdots+q^{d-1}$.

In the true Grassmann graph this procedure obtains precisely the point-stars, meaning the sets of all projective lines through one projective point. Indeed two intersecting lines $X,Y$ have common point $P$ and span a projective plane $U$. A line meeting both either contains $P$ or lies in $U$. If $Z$ contains $P$ and lies outside $U$, the common closed neighborhood is exactly the star of $P$. If $Z$ lies in $U$ and avoids $P$, the common closed neighborhood is the set of all lines of $U$. If $Z$ contains $P$ and lies inside $U$, the common closed neighborhood is the union of those two sets. A plane has $q^2+q+1$ lines and its intersection with the point-star has $q+1$ lines, so only the first case has the retained size. For every point, three incident lines spanning more than a plane exist, so every star occurs.

On an arbitrary candidate, make the retained sets into a point set and make each original vertex a line through the retained sets containing that vertex. Require all lines to be distinct, every line to have exactly $q+1$ points, every pair of points to lie on exactly one line, and the input adjacency to agree with line intersection. Check also that for every four distinct points $a,b,c,f$ with the line $ab$ meeting $cf$, the line $ac$ meets $bf$. Require exactly $1+q+\cdots+q^d$ points. This is the Veblen–Young point-line recognition axiom, with dimension at least three since the number of points exceeds the order-$q$ plane count. Its finite division ring is a field by Wedderburn's theorem. The number of points forces dimension $d$. Hence every accepting quotient is the line-intersection graph of $\mathrm{PG}(d,q)$, and a copy of the model passes all checks. This recognition is polynomial even when $q$ and $d$ vary. The graph has no true twins because for distinct intersecting lines $X,Y$, one can choose a line through a point of $X\setminus Y$ and outside the plane spanned by $X,Y$. It meets $X$ and misses $Y$. [Veblen and Young, *Projective Geometry*](https://archive.org/details/projectivegeomet01vebluoft).

Next recognize the point graph of $D_{5,5}(q)$, the half-spin geometry of the ten-dimensional hyperbolic quadratic vector space. Its neighborhood at each point is the $q$-clique extension of $J_q(5,2)$, by the point-residue description. Test this at every vertex by the preceding algorithm and require connectivity. Ihringer and coauthors' Main Result 1.1 states that this local graph characterizes the connected point graph of the half-spin geometry in question. In particular the family has no true twins. If two adjacent vertices were true twins, one would be universal in the neighborhood of the other, whereas the neighborhood is a clique extension of a graph without any universal vertex. [Ihringer et al., Main Result 1.1, §2.4 and §3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.09649).

Finally apply these two recognizers to the neighborhood quotients for $VJ_q(5,2)$ and $VD_{5,5}(q)$. For $q>2$, the local-to-global theorem gives the claimed recognition. For $q=2$, these are just two graphs of fixed orders $2^{10}$ and $2^{16}$. Exhaustive comparison with their fixed finite models gives an absolute constant in the asymptotic time bound. This finite exception step does not apply to any variable-size family.

## Affine orthogonal graphs

For $q>2$, the quotient of a neighborhood is the point graph of a nondegenerate quadratic polar space. Its maximal totally singular vector subspace dimension, called the polar rank, is $s$ for the hyperbolic type and $s-1$ for the elliptic type. We use the following raw graph recognition test when that rank is at least three.

For a point set $X$ in a candidate graph, let $X^\perp$ be the set of vertices equal or adjacent to every vertex of $X$. For each edge $xy$, take the line $L_{xy}=\{x,y\}^{\perp\perp}$. On the target graph these are exactly its projective singular lines. In fact, for the underlying two-dimensional totally singular subspace $U$, the totally singular vectors in its polar orthogonal complement $U^\perp$ span that complement. Its quotient by $U$ still contains a hyperbolic pair, and any vector in the anisotropic part is the difference of a suitable singular vector and a linear combination of the hyperbolic pair. The polar form in even ambient dimension is nondegenerate. Consequently taking the polar complement twice gives the projectivization of $U$.

Test that these lines each have $q+1$ points, form a partial linear space with the original graph as the collinearity graph, and satisfy the one-or-all axiom. For every point outside a line, the set of its neighbors on that line must contain either exactly one point or the whole line. Also require every point to lie on at least two lines and no point to be universal. The Buekenhout–Shult axioms give a finite nondegenerate polar space. Compute its rank by greedily adding a point adjacent to the current singular subspace but outside it, and closing under all lines between its points after every addition. This maintains a totally singular subspace and ends at a maximal one. All such subspaces are projective spaces of the same dimension. Their size gives the polar rank $r$.

Tits's finite classification of polar spaces of rank at least three gives a classical polar space. At fixed line size $q+1$ and rank $r$, its point count has the form
$$
\frac{(q^r-1)(q^{r+\eta-1}+1)}{q-1},
$$
with $\eta=0$ for hyperbolic, $\eta=2$ for elliptic, $\eta=1$ for parabolic or symplectic, and $\eta\in\{1/2,3/2\}$ for the two Hermitian types when the line field has square order. Thus the target sign and rank can be tested from exact integer counts. The graph is then precisely the target point graph. This point graph is twin-free. Two adjacent distinct points span a line, and a point in $x^\perp$ outside $y^\perp$ exists since the singular points span $x^\perp$ and the two hyperplanes are distinct. Quantifying over all point-line pairs and closing point sets under lines are polynomial-time operations. The affine local-to-global theorem now applies. [Buekenhout–Shult, *On the Foundations of Polar Geometry*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00183207), [Tits, *Buildings of Spherical Type and Finite BN-Pairs*](https://books.google.com/books?id=UUs-to6uwBUC), [Finite classical point counts, Table 1.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11131), [Affine polar local-to-global theorem](https://cage.ugent.be/~hvm/artikels/280.pdf).

In characteristic two with $q=2$, one can instead recover the whole quadratic space directly. Fix a vertex $o$. For the input define $F(x,y)\in\mathbb F_2$ as zero if the vertices coincide or are adjacent and one otherwise. Set $Q(x)=F(x,o)$ and
$$
B(x,y)=F(x,y)+Q(x)+Q(y).
$$
On the model, after moving $o$ to the vector-space zero, these are the quadratic form and its polar form. Let $D$ be the set of distinct rows of $B$, considered as binary vectors indexed by vertices. Require that every row is distinct, the row of $o$ is zero, and $D$ is a linear subspace of cardinality $2^{2s}$. Transport $Q$ to $D$. Verify on all pairs of row vectors that $Q(u+v)+Q(u)+Q(v)$ equals $B$ on those pairs. The resulting function is bilinear because each row records a linear functional of its first argument and $B$ is symmetric. It is alternating and nondegenerate because $B$ has zero diagonal and no nonzero row vanishes identically. Thus $Q$ is a nondegenerate quadratic form on the recovered space, and the graph joins precisely the pairs on which the quadratic form of their difference vanishes. The number of zeros distinguishes the two isomorphism types. Conversely a target graph passes every check. All vector-space and quadratic-form tests are polynomial in the input order.

## Uniform algorithm and scope

Write $N$ for the number of vertices of an input graph. Enumerate the prime powers $q\le N$ and integer exponents with $q$ raised to the specified dimension equal to $N$. These are polynomially many possibilities. Test the graph and its complement for the listed models. For the one-input isomorphism promise, run these tests on both graphs and accept when both match a common model, with the same complement convention. Soundness follows from model recognition, and completeness follows since membership in a model is invariant under isomorphism. All procedures have fixed polynomial exponents. In particular there is no enumeration of variable-size coordinate frames.

The earlier affine rank-three algorithm used a promise on both inputs. The exact local tests here also distinguish the named models from arbitrary strongly regular graphs with the same parameters. The one-dimensional Peisert branch, the unbounded-index Van Lint–Schrijver branch, the nonbinary rank-two elliptic local case, and other almost-simple families remain outside this theorem's scope.
