# When can field extensions hide tensor nonisomorphism?

Fix a finite field $F$ and a nonempty finite list of positive extension degrees $K$. Consider ordinary three-factor tensor isomorphism of multiplication tensors of finite étale $F$-algebras. Each test asks for isomorphism after the indicated scalar extension. The changes of basis may depend on the extension.

**Theorem.** If any degree equals 1, simultaneous agreement implies isomorphism over the base. Otherwise let $H$ be the minimum-cost set of primes containing at least one divisor of each tested degree, with cost $C(H)=\prod_{p\in H}2p$. The least common algebra dimension in which nonisomorphic multiplication tensors can pass all tests is $C(H)/2$. Both the optimal set and the unordered pair of tensor orbits in that dimension are unique. Below this threshold the tests determine the tensor orbit within this family. In every greater dimension one obtains a collision by adding matching copies of the ground field.

With $m=\prod_{p\in H}p$, partition the divisors of $m$ according to parity of the number of prime factors. On each side put $m/d$ copies of the degree-$d$ field for each divisor $d$ of that parity. This unique extremal pair becomes isomorphic over the degree-$k$ extension exactly when $\gcd(k,m)>1$. For pairwise coprime degrees $k_1,\ldots,k_r>1$, the answer specializes to $2^{r-1}\prod_i p_i$, where $p_i$ is the least prime divisor of $k_i$. In particular the quadratic and cubic tests first fail together at dimension 12. The factors on the two sides then have degrees $1,1,1,1,1,1,6$ and $2,2,2,3,3$.

## Proof

Write $X,Y,Z:A\to B$ for linear bijections with $Z(xy)=X(x)Y(y)$. Commutativity and the unit give $Z(1)Z(xy)=Z(x)Z(y)$. Choose $t$ with $Z(t)=1$. Then $Z(1)Z(t^2)=1$, so $x\mapsto Z(1)^{-1}Z(x)$ is a unital algebra isomorphism. Conversely a unital algebra isomorphism works in all three tensor factors. This argument also works over any commutative scalar semiring for unital commutative semiring algebras.

The field factors of a finite étale algebra correspond to the cycles of Frobenius on its geometric points. Scalar extension of degree $k$ replaces Frobenius by its $k$-th power. A length-$d$ cycle breaks into $\gcd(d,k)$ cycles of length $d/\gcd(d,k)$. This description also follows from the equivalence with finite Galois sets. [Stacks Project, §58.2](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/04JI)

For a pair of cycle types let $z_d$ be their difference in multiplicities and set $w_d=d z_d$. Extension acts on these signed masses by pushing forward under $E_k(d)=d/\gcd(d,k)$. The two tensors agree after every test if and only if all these pushforwards vanish. In any such nonzero vector the positive and negative masses are equal, as each pushforward preserves total mass. The corresponding reduced pair has dimension $\|w\|_1/2$ per side. Common factors raise both dimensions.

We first prove that every nonzero, finitely supported integral vector $w$ with $d\mid w_d$ killed by all the tests satisfies $\|w\|_1\ge\min_H C(H)$. For an empty test list take the empty hitting set and use the bound 1. A test of degree 1 annihilates only the zero vector. In the remaining cases induct on the number of distinct test primes. Pick such a prime $p$. Group the coordinates by writing $d=e p^a$ with $p\nmid e$, and let $v_e=\sum_{a\ge0}w_{ep^a}$. This contracts the norm and preserves divisibility by the index. Erasing the $p$-coordinate commutes with each test after its $p$-part has been removed, because powers by distinct primes act on distinct exponent coordinates.

If $v\ne0$, induction on the list with $p$ removed from all prime supports bounds $\|w\|_1$ by the minimum hitting cost excluding $p$, from below. If $v=0$, let $a$ be the largest occupied exponent. It is at least 1 since a nonzero line sums to zero. The nonzero slice $s_e=w_{ep^a}/p^a$ satisfies $e\mid s_e$ and is killed by each test whose degree is prime to $p$, since such tests preserve the slice. The complement must compensate every entry of this slice, giving $\|w\|_1\ge2p^a\|s\|_1$ by the triangle inequality on each line. Induction bounds the last quantity below by the minimum cost of a hitting set including $p$. The smaller of the two branch costs is the unrestricted optimum.

For any hitting set, the two displayed parity constructions have signed masses $\pm m$ at the divisors of $m$. If $p\mid\gcd(k,m)$, pair each $d\mid m$ avoiding $p$ with $pd$. Their images under $E_k$ coincide, so they cancel. If $\gcd(k,m)=1$, the map fixes all occupied lengths. This proves attainability, the claimed extension spectrum, and the upper bound.

**Rigidity at equality.** Distinct prime sets have distinct costs. Odd prime factors recover the odd members, and inclusion of 2 multiplies the cost of the resulting set by 4. Thus the optimum $H$ is unique. For each $p\in H$, deleting $p$ from all tests gives a lower bound strictly greater than the attained norm (or leaves a degree-1 test). Therefore $\sum_a w_{ep^a}=0$ for every $p\in H$ and every $e$ prime to $p$.

Separate the support by its prime-to-$m$ part $u$. In each such part all the indicated lines remain balanced. For a nonzero part with axis primes $H$ its norm is at least $u\prod_{p\in H}2p$. Indeed, with no axes a nonzero entry is a nonzero multiple of $u$. On any chosen axis choose the largest occupied $p^a$. There are at least two occupied exponents and hence $a\ge1$. Its slice still satisfies all remaining balances, and its entries have the extra divisor $p^a$. Applying the lower-dimensional assertion and doubling for the compensating complement proves the claim, with the stronger factor $2p^a$ on the chosen axis. Equality forces a single nonzero part with $u=1$. Applying this bound with each axis chosen first forces every largest exponent to be 1. All lines now have just two possible entries, and along any edge these entries are negatives. One nonzero entry propagates throughout the entire Boolean cube. At its far corner the absolute mass is divisible by $m$, and the total norm forces that mass to equal $m$. Equality of the original dimensions to the minima leaves no common factors. This proves uniqueness.

## The complexity of designing a test schedule

**Corollary.** Given extension degrees greater than 1 in binary and a dimension budget $N$ in binary, deciding whether there exists a colliding pair in dimensions at most $N$ is NP-complete. Hardness holds over any fixed finite field when every test degree is a product of two distinct primes. This concerns a succinct schedule and an existential dimension bound. It does not establish hardness for comparing two supplied multiplication tables.

A certificate gives the chosen primes, each dividing a tested degree. There need be no more primes than there are tests, since one may choose one witness for each test and discard the rest. Primality, coverage and the binary product bound are polynomial-time checks.

For hardness start from Vertex Cover on an $n$-vertex graph with target $1\le s\le n$ and at least one edge, where $n\ge2$. Deterministically find $n$ distinct primes in an interval $[a,b]$ with $b/a\le1+1/n$. Sieve a dyadic interval $[X,2X)$ and distribute its primes among $n$ equal-width intervals of length $X/n$ (use rational endpoints). Double $X$ until some bin has $n$ primes. The prime number theorem gives $\pi(2X)-\pi(X)\sim X/\log X$, so $X=O(n^4)$ suffices, with the constant absorbing small $n$. Use the smallest and largest actual selected primes as $a,b$. Assign a prime $p_v$ to each vertex and insert the degree $p_up_v$ for every edge $uv$. The prime hitting sets correspond exactly to vertex covers after discarding irrelevant primes. Every cover of size at most $s$ costs at most $(2b)^s$, while any cover of size at least $s+1$ costs at least $(2a)^{s+1}>(2b)^s$, since $(b/a)^s\le(1+1/n)^n<3<2a$. Set $N=(2b)^s/2$. All outputs have polynomial bit length. The decision equivalence follows from the dimension theorem. [Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9) and [Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena, *PRIMES is in P*](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2004.160.781)

For a unary dimension bound there is a direct polynomial-time algorithm. Enumerate squarefree integers $m$ from 2 through $2N$ by sieve. Accept if some $m$ has $2^{\omega(m)}m\le 2N$ and $\gcd(m,k)>1$ for every tested degree $k$. The theorem proves completeness and soundness. Thus the hardness relies on succinctly specifying the dimension. When a full multiplication tensor is supplied, its dimensions are explicit in its representation.
