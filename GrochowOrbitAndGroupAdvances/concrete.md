# Sharp generic concrete recovery in dimension \(d\)

Let the ambient space be a known real or complex Hilbert space of dimension \(d\ge1\) over its field. The input consists of actual unlabeled point sets for \(k\) generic orbits, in the common ambient coordinates. Competing groups are also required to have generic observations. The exact worst-case number of orbits for concrete recovery among Borel recovery rules is \(\lceil d/2\rceil\). For a fixed group there is an exact residual-representation criterion below whenever \(k\ge2\) over \(\mathbb R\) or \(k\ge1\) over \(\mathbb C\). This answers the minimum concrete orbit count question in the jointly generic model. The proof is informal. [Mixon–Vose, §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S4)

## Exact criterion after the permutation action is known

Write \(X\) for the union of observed point sets and \(W=\operatorname{span}X\). Under the indicated lower bound on \(k\), the generic multi-orbit theorem recovers the permutation subgroup on \(X\). Its action on \(W\) is then recovered by linear extension and is faithful because a generic orbit is free. Thus every competing group with generic observations has the same abstract group \(G\) identified by its action on \(W\). [Mixon–Vose, Theorem 3.2 and Lemma 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S3.SS1)

For each irreducible \(\pi\) of this recovered group over the ambient field, let \(d_\pi\) be its dimension over that field, and let \(c_\pi\) be the number of copies occurring in the regular representation over that field. In particular \(c_\pi=\dim_{D_\pi}U_\pi\), where \(U_\pi\) is the irreducible space and \(D_\pi=\operatorname{End}_G(U_\pi)\) is its division endomorphism algebra. In complex dimension \(c_\pi=d_\pi\). In real dimension \(c_\pi=d_\pi/\dim_\mathbb R D_\pi\).

For ambient multiplicity \(m_\pi\), the generic observed multiplicity is
\[
 r_\pi=\min\{m_\pi,kc_\pi\}.
\]
Indeed, the span of \(k\) orbits is the image of the module map from \(k\) copies of the regular representation sending each distinguished identity-basis vector to its seed. All module maps occur this way. By semisimplicity the maximum multiplicity of the image is the displayed minimum in each isotype, and all maxima are obtained simultaneously. Maximal rank in every isotype is a dense open condition given by nonvanishing minors. [Mixon–Vose, Lemma 3.3 and Theorem 3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S3.SS2)

Put \(D=d-\dim W\). The possible orthogonal or unitary actions of the group on \(W^\perp\) are exactly the representations of dimension \(D\) supported on the saturated types \(\pi\) with \(r_\pi=kc_\pi\). The necessity follows from the minimum formula for a competing representation. Conversely any extension on \(W^\perp\) of this form leaves every observed orbit unchanged and preserves the generic ranks in all isotypes. It also preserves free orbit size and the observable uniqueness of the permutation action on \(X\). This gives the criterion using those explicit generic tests.

When \(D>0\), the concrete group is unique under these tests exactly when there is a single possible residual representation type and that type consists of \(D\) copies of one one-dimensional character. If two different residual representations fit, their extensions differ. If a residual representation is not scalar, conjugation on \(W^\perp\) by an orthogonal or unitary map outside its commutant gives a distinct extension of the same type. These extensions are distinct as subgroups: their faithful restriction to \(W\) matches their elements individually. A scalar residual extension has no conjugacy freedom.

## The universal upper bound

For \(k=\lceil d/2\rceil\), suppose \(D>0\) and first assume the generic multi-orbit theorem applies. Two saturated one-dimensional types would already contribute \(2k\ge d\) to \(\dim W\), a contradiction. A saturated type of dimension at least \(2\) would also contribute at least \(kc_\pi d_\pi\ge2k\). Thus at most one type can be saturated, and it is one-dimensional. The true representation provides a residual extension, so this type exists and its scalar extension is unique. The case \(D=0\) is immediate.

The real exceptions to the applicability of the multi-orbit theorem here are \(d=1,2\). In one dimension, a generic orbit has either one or two points and distinguishes the only two subgroups. In two dimensions, every group is cyclic or dihedral. A cyclic rotation group of order at least three gives a regular polygon, and a generic dihedral orbit of order at least four gives a rectangle or a polygon with two alternating angular gaps of different sizes. The orbit determines the concrete group: in the regular case the rotation subgroup is determined, and in the nonregular case the entire orthogonal symmetry group of the orbit is the dihedral group. A two-point orbit with midpoint zero distinguishes the central involution from a reflection observed generically, while a two-point orbit with nonzero midpoint determines the unique reflecting line by its midpoint. Singleton generic orbits give the trivial group. [Mixon–Vose, Examples 2.1, 2.8 and 3.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434)

The upper bound admits a Borel rule. For each finite cardinality one can test the finitely many point permutations for Gram preservation, extend them linearly on the observed span, and enumerate real or complex finite-dimensional irreducible representation multiplicity options by finite-group algebra. For the uniform upper bound with nonzero complement, it suffices to test the real or complex one-dimensional characters of this finite group for multiplicity \(k\) in the observed span. The matrices, their multiplication table, and the span projections can be selected measurably by ordering finite candidates and using rank tests. Under the generic hypotheses the resulting extension is the unique concrete group.

## A lower bound for any Borel rule and arbitrary dense open success loci

Suppose \(1\le k<\lceil d/2\rceil\), so \(d\ge2k+1\). Consider order-two groups generated by the orthogonal or unitary involution with positive eigenspace \(E\) of dimension \(s\in\{k,k+1\}\), and negative eigenspace \(E^\perp\) of dimension \(d-s\). For a batch of seeds decompose each as \(x_j=p_j+m_j\) with \(p_j\in E\), \(m_j\in E^\perp\). Its observed orbit is the unordered pair \(\{p_j+m_j,p_j-m_j\}\). Generically the tuples \(P=(p_j)\) and \(M=(m_j)\) each have rank \(k\). We may even reveal the ordered vectors \(P,M\) to the rule.

For either value of \(s\), the output data space \(Y\) of such ordered pairs of rank-\(k\) tuples with orthogonal spans is the same. The parameter space of \((E,x_1,\dots,x_k)\) with these full ranks is an open subset of the product of the Grassmannian of \(s\)-planes with the seed space. The observation map to \(Y\) is continuous, surjective, and open. In fact, over \((P,M)\), the possible \(E\) are exactly the span of \(P\) plus an \((s-k)\)-plane in \((\operatorname{span}(P,M))^\perp\), and the seeds are fixed by \(x_j=p_j+m_j\). These choices form a locally trivial Grassmannian bundle over \(Y\), using local orthonormal frames on the indicated complement.

If a Borel rule succeeds on a dense open set of seeds for every fixed \(E\), its success set in the full parameter space is comeager by the Kuratowski–Ulam theorem. This theorem applies to the open parameter space inside the stated product of second-countable Baire spaces. Let \(Y_s\) be the Borel set on which the output is an order-two group with positive eigenspace dimension \(s\). Its inverse image contains the comeager success set. Therefore \(Y_s\) is comeager in \(Y\). Indeed, a nonmeager Borel complement would be comeager in a nonempty open subset of \(Y\), and its inverse image under a continuous open surjection would be comeager in the corresponding nonempty open subset of the parameter space. This contradicts the comeagerness already obtained. But \(Y_k\) and \(Y_{k+1}\) are disjoint, so they cannot both be comeager in the nonempty Baire space \(Y\). Thus no such rule exists with \(k<\lceil d/2\rceil\). With zero observations, the scalar order-two group and the trivial group already force impossibility.


## If competitors may be nongeneric

A different observation model allows a competing group to realize the same sets with arbitrary stabilizers. In that model the exact worst-case number is \(d\), over both fields. Once the true group acts as the full permutation symmetry group on the observed union, any competitor has restriction on \(W\) inside \(G|_W\). Its image is transitive on a free true orbit, and a subgroup of a regular permutation action is transitive only if it is the whole group. If \(W\) is the entire ambient space, this identifies the competitor with \(G\). If \(W\) is proper, \(G|_W\times\langle -I_{W^\perp}\rangle\) gives a competitor of double order with exactly the same observed orbits.

For \(k=d\) the seeds span the ambient space generically, and the true permutation symmetry assertion applies, except when \(d=1\) over the reals where the answer follows directly. Conversely the scalar sign group has orbit span of dimension at most \(k\). Its observations admit the above product competitor whenever \(k<d\), regardless of the seeds. Thus \(d\) is sharp for unrestricted competitors. The generic module-rank formula also gives an exact criterion for an individual group once the applicable multi-orbit theorem holds: \(k\ge\max_\pi\lceil m_\pi/c_\pi\rceil\).
