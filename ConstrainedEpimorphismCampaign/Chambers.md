# Counting shortest representatives of a prescribed surface homology class

**Theorem.** In an unweighted, closed, connected, orientable triangulated surface of unbounded genus, counting the distinct binary 1-cycles of minimum support in a prescribed homology class is #P-hard even when both the minimum and a particular minimizer are supplied. More precisely, there is a polynomial-time, count-preserving reduction from counting independent sets. The total function counting representatives of a specified support size is #P-complete under polynomial-time counting reductions, and its hardness holds on these promised instances.

**Context.** Grochow and Tucker-Foltz's Questions 20 and 21 concern approximation of the optimum. The hardness in Borradaile, Maxwell and Nayyeri already answers those questions. Here we ask how many distinct cycles attain the optimum. Our proof strengthens the earlier MathProofs translation-constraint construction by making every optimal lift rigid. A related paper counts satisfying vertex spin assignments on triangulations. Here the witnesses are edge chains in one prescribed homology class and an optimum is given with the instance. Priority for this counting formulation has not been established. [Questions 20 and 21](https://arxiv.org/html/1803.06800#S6) · [Borradaile–Maxwell–Nayyeri](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.02801) · [Counting satisfying spin assignments on triangulations](https://arxiv.org/abs/1107.3767)

## A lift that preserves every optimum

Start with a connected multigraph of translation equations over a finite abelian group A. An edge oriented from u to v has the equation x(u) minus x(v) equals its specified offset. Assume there is an edge and every vertex has positive degree. Duplicate every equation so every incidence count is even. This scales the objective by two and preserves the entire set of minimizers. The following construction uses polynomial size directly, without replacing high-degree variables. For binary coefficients one can omit duplication.

For a variable of degree d in the resulting instance construct an orientable chamber with d triangular boundary circles. The three faces incident to each boundary circle are its terminals, one per boundary edge. Write N = 3d. We need the following routing property inside its dual graph. For any matching of the terminals, every matched pair has two connecting paths, with all the paths edge-disjoint. Here is the construction underlying the earlier surface reduction.

* Start each incidence with a triangle divided into three sectors by an interior vertex. Subdivide each sector into three triangles around a second interior vertex. One is its terminal. In each of its two neighbors place a triangular hole strictly inside the face and triangulate the annulus. Thus each terminal has a private route to each of its two holes, starting across its two different internal edges.
* For every terminal i make two separate triangulated spheres, indexed by j = 1, 2, each with N disjoint triangular holes. Use one hole to attach the sphere to the j-th route of i. Index its other holes by the other terminals. For every unordered pair i,i' and each j, glue the named holes of the corresponding spheres to each other. Boundaries are glued with opposite orientations. Such a sphere costs O(N) triangles: use a bipyramid on a cycle of length at least max(3,N), puncturing N faces using a fresh triangular annulus in each.
* A matched pair travels from i to its j-th sphere, over its dedicated connector, through the other sphere and out to i'. Interior dual paths between boundary faces exist in each sphere because its topological interior is connected. Different matched pairs have separate spheres and routes. The two paths for a single pair use different spheres and start and finish across distinct edges.

Glue the two incidence boundaries of every equation. The outer boundaries of all incidence pieces are disjoint before gluing. All internal punctures use fresh vertices and each boundary is paired exactly once. At the level of the original incidence pieces and individual spheres, each pair shares at most one boundary, even for repeated equations between the same variables. No piece contains a filled simplex on a triangular boundary. Hence the pairwise intersections consist exactly of the identified boundary circles, vertex links close up to circles, and this is a simplicial, closed, connected, orientable surface. The construction uses O(sum of d squared) triangles. Special inputs with no edges are dispensable for the hardness reduction.

Orient faces coherently. On the triangular seam of an oriented equation put its offset times the boundary of the hole, oriented from its first chamber. The sum z is a cycle. Writing a candidate representative as z minus the boundary of a face labeling, its support counts internal edges whose labels disagree and seam edges where the difference of the two labels misses the offset. A constant label on each chamber gives exactly three support edges per violated equation in the duplicated instance.

**Strict rounding.** In one chamber let M be the greatest multiplicity of a terminal label and let T = N minus M. Pair differently labeled terminals into min(T,N/2) disjoint pairs. If M is at least N/2, pair each minority terminal to a different majority terminal. Otherwise list equal labels consecutively and match each terminal in the first half to its opposite in the second half. Each of the two routed paths for a pair must have a disagreement, so there are at least min(2T,N) internal disagreements. If T is positive this exceeds T, since T is at most N minus one. Changing every face to the most frequent terminal label creates at most T new seam disagreements. If T is zero and some interior face still has a different label, connectedness of the interior dual gives at least one disagreement, and rounding affects no seam. Thus every nonconstant chamber strictly decreases total cost when rounded. For binary labels one always has M at least half of N, and the same strict inequality uses 2T without requiring even N.

It follows that *every* minimizer is constant on every chamber. Two face labelings give the same edge chain exactly when their difference is constant across each adjacent pair of faces, hence globally constant on this connected surface. We have proved a bijection between minimum representatives and optimal assignments modulo simultaneous translation. With duplicated equations the minimum support is six times the original minimum violation count. Without duplication it is three times the count in the binary case. An optimum-preserving reduction alone would not establish this bijection.

## Counting independent sets with a shortest cycle in hand

Given a simple graph with n positive vertices and m edges, form binary variables for its vertices and a reference r. Use relative bits b(v) = x(v) minus x(r). Add two equations per vertex, b(v)=0 and b(v)=1. Exactly one fails. These connect even isolated vertices to r. For each edge uv add three equations, b(u)=0, b(v)=0 and x(u) minus x(v)=1. Their violation count is

$$
b(u)+b(v)+1-(b(u)\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}b(v))=1+2b(u)b(v).
$$

Thus the overall minimum is m+n. The optimal relative assignments are precisely the indicator vectors of independent sets, including the empty one. Global translation accounts for exactly the choice of x(r). Use the unduplicated binary construction. The minimum support is exactly 3(m+n). The zero face labeling gives the explicit representative z of that size, and the bijection proves that the number of shortest representatives equals the independent-set count, with no unknown factor. The empty input graph can be handled separately by a triangulated sphere and the zero cycle. This establishes the hardness claim by the established hardness of counting independent sets, equivalently complements of vertex covers. [Provan–Ball](https://doi.org/10.1137/0212053)

For the total exact-size problem a nondeterministic machine guesses the edge bit vector once, tests its weight, and solves the linear boundary equation over the binary field to decide membership in the given class. There is exactly one accepting computation per counted chain. Supplying a minimizer in the hardness construction means that optimization, or knowing a first witness, cannot account for the difficulty of the resulting counting problem. This counts chains, which can have several connected components.

## A supplied shortest cycle and the complexity of finding a second one

There is a stronger transfer. Given a Boolean circuit F on n input bits, we construct an instance with a specified shortest representative y0 such that *the other shortest representatives are in bijection with satisfying inputs of F*. Both directions of the bijection take polynomial time. In particular, deciding whether a second representative of the same size exists is NP-complete even under the promise that the supplied one is shortest. On those instances, counting the additional representatives is parsimoniously #P-hard from #SAT.

Evaluate constant gates by substitution and simplify every Boolean square to its bit, including at gates with repeated inputs. Use an AND/NOT circuit, introducing a Boolean variable for each gate output. Put a switch t beside the original inputs x. Impose all gate equations regardless of the switch. For an AND gate with inputs a,b and output h, use the nonnegative integer penalty
$$
ab-2ah-2bh+3h .
$$
At h=0 this equals ab. At h=1 its values on 00,01,10,11 are 3,1,1,0. For h=NOT a use the penalty $(h+a-1)^2$, reducing Boolean squares to the bit itself. Add the penalties $(1-t)x_i$ for each input and $t(1-o)$ for the output bit o. The sum P is a quadratic polynomial of polynomial coefficient mass. Its zero assignments are exactly one default assignment with t=0, all input bits zero and all gate values obtained by evaluating F, together with one assignment with t=1 for each satisfying input. The default is computable without solving F.

Convert $2P$ into binary translation constraints. Replace every $2ab$ by $a+b-(a\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}b)$ and view each unary bit as a difference from the reference r. An integer term $d b$ on a bit or pairwise difference is represented by d copies of the equality-to-zero constraint when d is positive. For negative d, use $|d|$ copies of the equality-to-one constraint. Their violation contribution is $d b+|d|$. Constants only change the known offset. Add the opposite pair of unary equations at every variable to connect it to r while adding the same cost for every labeling. This polynomial-size translation instance has exactly the zero assignments of P as its optimizers, modulo the global flip. Its optimum B is known by evaluating the default.

Apply the unduplicated binary chamber construction. The specified shortest representative is z minus the boundary of the chamber labeling for the default, and its length is 3B. For any other representative of length 3B, integration across the dual graph with the reference label fixed to zero recovers the unique corresponding relative assignment. Strict rounding forces it to be uniform in every chamber. It therefore has t=1 and yields a satisfying input. Conversely a satisfying input uniquely fixes its circuit wire values and one other representative. Distinct inputs yield distinct edge chains.

For the unpromised decision language, ask whether a supplied cycle has a distinct homologous representative of exactly the same support size. Verifying a second chain and the linear boundary equation is polynomial. The reduction establishes NP-hardness with the additional shortest-input guarantee. Counting those distinct equal-size chains is in #P, and the same reduction is parsimonious. A consequence is coNP-hardness of certifying uniqueness among shortest representatives even if one optimum and its exact length are given. This reasoning permits arbitrary support, including disconnected representatives, and unbounded surface genus.

The earlier [MathProofs surface construction](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/SurfaceHomologyLocalization/PROOF.md) supplied the two disjoint routes. The new use is strict rounding on each optimal fiber, followed by a Boolean circuit with one explicitly known default solution. The classical circuit hardness and the standard quadratic gate penalty are inputs to the proof.
