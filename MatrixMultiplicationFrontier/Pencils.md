# Which two-slice tensors are limits of matrix multiplication?

Work over the complex numbers. Write $P(x,y)=xA+yB$ for a homogeneous matrix pencil of size $u$ by $v$. Fix positive integers $a,b$ and put $m=ab$. Assume $u,v\le m$. We consider the triangular tensor network with equal bonds $a$ incident to its two-dimensional physical mode and third bond $b$. Equivalently, we ask whether $P$ is a border restriction of rectangular matrix multiplication with these bond dimensions. For $a=b=n$ the source is ordinary $n$-by-$n$ matrix multiplication. Border restriction means membership in the Zariski closure of all linear restrictions over the complex numbers.

**Common divisor criterion.** The answer is yes exactly when there is one nonzero homogeneous binary form $Q(x,y)$ of degree $a$ such that, for every $s=1,\dots,b$,
$$
 Q(x,y)^s \text{ divides every minor of } P(x,y) \text{ of size } m-b+s.
$$
A nonexistent minor, or one that vanishes identically, imposes no condition. The same $Q$ must work at every size and for every minor. This criterion includes pencils with no invertible member, eigenvalues at infinity and repeated eigenvalues. It gives both explicit, possibly large, coefficient equations and a separate deterministic polynomial-arithmetic membership algorithm.

Bernardi and Gesmundo already identified the dense pencil family, proved the corresponding generic dimensions, and obtained equations using coincident roots. They explain after Theorem 4.1 why those equations can miss the Jordan structure. The common divisor across *all* specified sizes supplies the additional data. The proof below deduces this criterion from their description and published bundle-closure and strict-equivalence orbit-closure theorems. [Bernardi and Gesmundo, §§3–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15114v1#S4) · [De Terán, Dopico, Koval and Pagacz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16702)

## From the dense family to a pointwise test

Pad the pencil by zero rows and columns to size $m$ by $m$. Inclusion into these larger physical spaces and projection back are linear restrictions, so padding preserves border membership in both directions. The dense family at size $m$ is the strict-equivalence bundle with $a$ distinct eigenvalues, each carrying $b$ Jordan blocks of length one. Indeed, restricting the physical mode with two slices first produces a pencil of two $a$-by-$a$ matrices tensored with $I_b$. A generic pair can be put into the form $(I,D)$ with distinct eigenvalues of $D$. The two other maps are generically invertible and supply arbitrary left and right changes. This is also the saturated-pencil case in Bernardi and Gesmundo.

The closure of this bundle consists of the union of the strict-equivalence orbit closures obtained by coalescing its eigenvalues. In coalescing, partial multiplicities are added componentwise. Assigning $t_\lambda$ of the original eigenvalues to $\lambda\in\mathbb P^1$ therefore produces $b$ blocks of length $t_\lambda$ there, with $\sum_\lambda t_\lambda=a$. Use this published coalescence theorem over $\mathbb C$, including the point at infinity. The Euclidean and Zariski closures agree here because the sets are constructible. [Bundle coalescence and orbit inequalities, Theorems 4.1 and 4.6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16702)

Let $\rho$ be the normal rank of the padded target and $h=m-\rho$. At each projective eigenvalue write $d_1\ge\cdots\ge d_\rho\ge0$ for the local Smith exponents, padded by zero. The right and left minimal-index conditions of the strict-equivalence orbit-closure theorem are automatic. The source pencil in the coalesced orbit is regular; the target has $h$ minimal indices of each kind, so each entry in either target's conjugate minimal-index sequence is at most $h$. For the Weyr sequence at $\lambda$, the remaining prefix inequalities are
$$
 b\min(j,t_\lambda)\ \le\ hj+\sum_{i=1}^\rho\min(j,d_i)\quad(j\ge1).
$$
These are exactly the inequalities of Theorem 4.1(c) at that eigenvalue: a block of length $d$ contributes $\min(j,d)$ to the prefix of length $j$. If $t=t_\lambda>0$, it suffices to test $j=t$. For $j\le t$ divide by $j$ and observe that $\min(j,d)/j$ decreases as $j$ increases. For $j\ge t$ the right side is nondecreasing. For $t=0$ there is no condition. If $h\ge b$, all inequalities hold for every assignment of eigenvalues; every minor in the stated criterion vanishes as well.

Suppose $k=b-h>0$. At each $\lambda$ we now require $kt\le\sum_i\min(t,d_i)$. For decreasing $d_i$ the sum is the minimum over $0\le j\le\rho$ of $jt+\sum_{i>j}d_i$. Thus the condition is equivalent to
$$
 (k-j)t\le\sum_{i>j}d_i \qquad(0\le j<k).
$$
The right side is the local valuation of the gcd of all $(\rho-j)$-minors, by Smith form over the local discrete valuation ring. Put $s=k-j$; then $\rho-j=m-b+s$. Values $s>k$ correspond to minors exceeding the normal rank, hence zero. Multiplying one linear form vanishing at each $\lambda$ to exponent $t_\lambda$ gives precisely the common degree-$a$ form $Q$. Conversely any such $Q$ specifies the coalescence counts and satisfies the complete orbit-closure test. This proves both directions.

## A deterministic membership algorithm without a Jordan basis

When all the specified minors vanish, accept. Otherwise choose a rational projective direction where the rank equals the normal rank and use it as infinity. Testing $m+1$ distinct directions and maximizing rank suffices: some nonzero maximal minor has degree at most $m$. Even if $\rho=0$, the initial acceptance handles this case. Compute the polynomial Smith invariant factors of the dehomogenized pencil. For each required size $j\le\rho$, the product of its first $j$ invariant factors gives the monic determinantal divisor $\delta_j(z)$. No relevant divisor has a root at the chosen infinity because all ranks through $\rho$ occur there.

For $t=1,\ldots,a$, find the number $N_t$ of distinct complex roots at which every nonzero $\delta_{m-b+s}$ has order at least $st$. Compute the gcd of each divisor and its derivatives through order $st-1$, take a common gcd across all applicable $s$, then take the degree of its squarefree part. Including a nonzero constant derivative gives the correct answer zero when the threshold exceeds the degree. A root counted at level $t$ can accommodate $t$ coalescing eigenvalues. Hence $\sum_{t=1}^a N_t$ is the total capacity truncated per root at $a$. Accept precisely when it is at least $a$: integer allocations from zero up to the individual capacities realize every smaller total. No polynomial needs to split over the input field. Standard polynomial-matrix Smith algorithms and univariate gcd and differentiation use polynomially many field operations at these matrix dimensions and degrees. This is an exact arithmetic bound with rational inputs allowed. The geometric decision is over the algebraic closure. [Polynomial matrix Smith algorithms, Storjohann](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~astorjoh/diss2up.pdf)

For a nonzero pencil first compress its matrix rows and columns to their combined image and coimage, of dimensions $u,v$. Let $n_0=\lceil\sqrt{\max(u,v)}\rceil$. The least square matrix multiplication order admitting a border restriction is either $n_0$ or $n_0+1$: the lower bound is the flattening bound; for $n=n_0+1$ all required minors have size at least $n^2-n+1>\max(u,v)$. The criterion makes the choice for each raw pencil. Both answers occur. A diagonal pencil of size $n_0^2$ with $n_0^2$ distinct roots needs $n_0+1$ when $n_0\ge2$, while a generic saturated repeated-eigenvalue pencil needs $n_0$. The ambient-filling range itself is already present in Bernardi–Gesmundo.

## An explicit finite system in the input coefficients alone

Here is an algebraic elimination if actual equations are desired. Let $q_0,\ldots,q_a$ be indeterminates for the coefficients of $Q$. For each $s$ and each existing minor $F$ of degree $d=m-b+s$, take the binary-form multiplication matrix by $Q^s$ from degree $d-as=(a-1)(b-s)$ to degree $d$. Append the coefficient column of $F$. At $Q\ne0$ the multiplication matrix has full column rank, so all minors of size $d-as+2$ of the augmented matrix vanish exactly when $Q^s\mid F$. The equations involving $F$ are homogeneous of positive degree $s(d-as+1)$ in $q$. Identically zero equations may be retained.

To eliminate $q$, raise the equations to common degree $D=\max_{1\le s\le b}s(d_s-as+1)$ by multiplying each by *all monomials* of the required difference in degree, where $d_s=m-b+s$. This preserves their projective zero set. Put $E=(a+1)(D-1)+1$. Form the matrix whose columns are coefficient vectors of these degree-$D$ equations multiplied by all monomials of degree $E-D$ in $q$. A common projective zero forces its row rank to be deficient, by evaluation at that zero. Conversely, in the absence of a base point, $a+1$ generic linear combinations of the degree-$D$ equations have no common projective zero: for a fixed point the vanishing imposes $a+1$ independent scalar conditions on the choices, exceeding the dimension $a$ of the point space. The combinations form a homogeneous system of parameters in a polynomial ring and hence a regular sequence. The quotient has Hilbert series $(1+z+\cdots+z^{D-1})^{a+1}$ and its degree-$E$ part is zero. Thus the columns span that degree and the matrix has full row rank. Its maximal row minors, taken in the entries of the original pencil alone, are set-theoretic equations. If there are fewer columns than rows, the rank condition holds identically. No polynomial bound on the size of this system is claimed.

For instance, with $a=b=2$ and a pencil of size $4$, the single-eigenvalue regular pencils $(I,J_4(0))$ and $(I,J_2(0)\oplus J_2(0))$ have the same homogeneous determinant. The second is an exact restriction. The first fails because some $3$-minor has valuation zero at the eigenvalue, so the required quadratic form cannot divide it. The test detects what a determinant-root-multiplicity test misses.

These statements concern two-slice tensor targets, including singular targets. The individual-pencil common-divisor formula, its elimination and its use to compute the minimum equal bond are the deductions proposed here. The source bundle and the pencil degeneration order both precede this work. Independent publication priority for these deductions remains unverified.
