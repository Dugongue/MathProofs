# Computing exact matrix proof degree without polynomial factorization

**Theorem.** Over any explicitly presented finite field, the exact ordinary Polynomial Calculus refutation degree of the two matrix systems in Question 7.3 is computable in deterministic polynomial bit time. The algorithm distinguishes finite degree from geometric satisfiability. It includes nonsymmetric and singular matrices in characteristic two. The same bound holds for arbitrary directed quivers and pure bipartite form graphs in every characteristic, and for arbitrary mixed graphs of maps and bilinear forms in odd characteristic. The procedure uses the deterministic radical algorithm and linear algebra. It needs neither polynomial factorization nor explicit absolute indecomposable normal forms. [Original matrix questions](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19320#S7.SS3) · [Mixed-graph formula](MixedGraphDegree.md) · [Directed quivers](QuiverProofDegree.md)

The rank-profile computation for directed quivers uses the endomorphism algebra of the direct sum of both inputs. The new step for forms detects the kind of the involution and the alternating flags at each hidden factor without splitting its center. This is especially relevant in characteristic two, where the underlying ordinary Kronecker multiplicities alone can miss a congruence distinction.

## The semisimple endomorphism algebra

Use the ordinary quiver for directed diagrams and the undoubled one for bipartite forms. For arbitrary mixed graphs or a single bilinear form, use the doubled quiver with its transpose adjoint. Form the endomorphism algebra \(E\) of the direct sum by solving the linear arrow equations and multiplying basis matrices to obtain structure constants. Compute its Jacobson radical by Rónyai's deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for finite algebras. Construct \(S=E/\operatorname{rad}E\), its center \(Z\), and the image \(e\) of the input-\(A\) projector. The center is computed by commutation equations. The input-\(B\) projector is \(1-e\). In the doubled construction both projectors are self-adjoint and the radical is adjoint-stable. [Rónyai, *Computing the structure of finite algebras*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0747-7171(08)80017-X) · [Imran, discussion of the classical radical step](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.30340v2)

Abstractly \(S=\prod_i M_{n_i}(K_i)\) for finite extensions \(K_i\) of the input field, and \(Z=\prod_i K_i\). Over an algebraic closure the radical is still the scalar extension of the radical: it remains nilpotent, and the quotient just described becomes a product of full matrix algebras. Each center embedding gives an absolute simple factor with the same corner sizes. On each factor the \(K_i\)-dimensions of \(S_i\) and \(eS_i\) are \(n_i^2\) and \(a_i n_i\), where \(a_i\) is the rank of \(e_i\). Hence the component ranks of the two \(Z\)-modules \(S,eS\) give the two ordinary multiplicities \(a_i,n_i-a_i\). The center fields and their embeddings need never be listed.

## Recover ranks over the product of fields

For a finite module \(M\) over a product of fields \(Z\) given by structure constants, one can partition \(Z\) into ideals of constant component rank for \(M\) without factoring. Pick \(0\ne v\in M\). Compute \(I=\operatorname{Ann}_Z(v)\) by solving \(zv=0\), and \(J=\operatorname{Ann}_Z(I)\) by requiring zero products with a basis of \(I\). Here \(Z=I\oplus J\). Their identities are the two summands of \(1\). On \(I\), recurse on \(IM\). On \(J\), recurse on \(JM/Jv\) and add one to the eventual ranks, since \(Jv\) is a free rank-one \(J\)-module. Report rank zero on a sector where the module vanishes. At any stage the relevant multiplication actions on subspaces and quotients are available by linear algebra. There are at most \(\dim_F Z\) nonzero terminal sectors, and at a step without a sector split the module dimension strictly decreases. Thus polynomially many linear operations suffice. Apply the procedure successively to a constant list of modules to refine the center into joint rank sectors. [Directed-quiver application and finite controls](QuiverProofDegree.md)

## Detect the involution and both flags

For the doubled quiver, \(*\) induces an involution on \(S\) and on \(Z\). Compute the ideal generated by all \(z-z^*\) for \(z\) in a basis of \(Z\). Its annihilator is exactly the part of the center on which \(*\) fixes every geometric component. Indeed if two center factors are swapped, their idempotents have a difference supported on them both. If a center field is fixed but its automorphism is nontrivial, a nonzero difference generates that whole field ideal. After scalar extension, the nontrivial field automorphism permutes the embeddings without fixed points. On the complementary sector all geometric factors are dual pairs. Each pair uses ordinary copy counts \(a_i,n_i-a_i\). Computing the same degree candidate on both members of a pair has no effect on a minimum.

Restrict now to the first-kind sector, where the involution fixes the center pointwise. Over a geometric component it is \(x^*=H^{-1}x^T H\) for a nonsingular \(H\), since composing with transpose is an inner automorphism. The relation \(e^*=e\) decomposes \(H\) orthogonally into its restrictions to the two corner spaces.

**Odd characteristic.** Apply the rank routine also to the \(Z\)-module \(\{x:x^*=-x\}\) on this sector. If \(H\) is symmetric its component dimension is \(n_i(n_i-1)/2\), since multiplication by \(H\) identifies it with skew-symmetric matrices. If \(H\) is alternating the dimension is \(n_i(n_i+1)/2\), since multiplication by \(H\) identifies it with symmetric matrices. Thus these ranks distinguish the two kinds. Use \(a_i,n_i-a_i\) for symmetric factors and halve both counts for alternating factors. This supplies the exact normalized counts for the mixed-graph formula.

**Characteristic two for single forms.** The first-kind \(H\) is symmetric. Put \(U=\operatorname{im}(x\mapsto x+x^*)\) on the first-kind sector. It is a \(Z\)-submodule. For a self-adjoint projector \(e\), multiplication by \(H\) identifies the test \(e\in U\) with \(He\) belonging to the space of alternating matrices. Indeed \(H(x+x^*)=Hx+(Hx)^T\), whose image is precisely that space in characteristic two. In a basis adapted to \(e,1-e\), \(He\) is the restriction of \(H\) to the first corner, with zeros on the other corner. Thus on every nonzero \(A\) corner, membership is exactly its alternating flag. The \(B\) flag is the corresponding test for \(1-e\). To obtain both flags on unsplit center sectors, compute the annihilators of the classes of \(e\) and \(1-e\) in \(S/U\). Such an annihilator is exactly the center ideal of components where the class vanishes. Their support idempotents refine the existing rank sectors. Count a missing factor as a discrepancy of weight zero. When both sides are present, opposite flags give weight zero, and like alternating flags halve both multiplicities. The prior characteristic-two matrix theorem identifies these residual flags with the geometric congruence data, including at the eigenvalue-one blocks. [Characteristic-two classification and proof](BinaryCongruence.md)

Finally run the degree-zero and degree-two tests in the original coordinates. If neither applies, compute the candidate for each mismatched normalized count pair and take its minimum, with the degree-three floor. When all sectors match, the geometric degree is infinite. Computing the \(p\)-adic valuation of an integer count gap uses repeated integer division. The construction uses explicit polynomial-size vector spaces, module action matrices and the deterministic radical algorithm. It works uniformly as the characteristic and the field size vary. The algorithm computes the numerical degree; its steps need no search for an optimal derivation.
