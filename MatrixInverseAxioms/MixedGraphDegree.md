# Exact Polynomial Calculus degree for systems of maps and bilinear forms

Fix a finite mixed graph, allowing directed arrows, bilinear-form edges, loops and parallel edges. Two inputs assign a vector space at each vertex and matrices $A_e,B_e$ to each edge. Write $X_v$ for the forward map from the source space of the comparison to the target space at $v$, and $Y_v$ for the reverse map. The original equations are both inverse equations at each vertex, the linear equations $X_tA_e=B_eX_s$ for directed arrows $e:s\to t$, and the quadratic equations $X_tA_eX_s^T=B_e$ for bilinear edges from endpoint $s$ to endpoint $t$. An ordering of the two endpoints gives the written matrix of the form. Work with ordinary Polynomial Calculus (PC) and no field equations. A negative instance means one with no solution over an algebraic closure. [Matrix prototype and degree convention](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19320#S7.SS3) · [Sergeichuk's mixed-graph classification](https://arxiv.org/abs/0801.0823)

**Theorem.** In characteristic different from two, the procedure below gives the exact minimum PC refutation degree of every such instance. It handles any number of edges and arbitrary input dimensions. With only bilinear edges on a bipartite graph the same result holds in every characteristic, using ordinary quiver indecomposable multiplicities with one undoubled copy of each vertex. This includes matrix equivalence and simultaneous left–right equivalence of labelled matrix tuples. A separate degree-two test below works on every mixed graph in every characteristic.

For $0\le r<s$ define $\delta(0,s)=0$. For $r>0$ define $\delta(r,s)=2$ in characteristic zero and
$$
\delta(r,s)=\min\{r+2,\,2p^{v_p(s-r)}\}
$$
in characteristic $p>0$. Take an algebraic closure $K$ of the coefficient field. Decompose the two inputs into normalized indecomposable mixed-graph blocks over $K$ as described below. If their block counts agree, the degree is infinite. Otherwise use the following test in the displayed coordinates:

1. A literal nonzero constant axiom gives degree zero.
2. Otherwise apply either of the two linear tests below. A positive test gives degree two.
3. Otherwise the degree is $\max(3,\min_C\delta(r_C,s_C))$, where $C$ ranges over discrepant normalized types and $r_C<s_C$ are their counts in increasing order. For an alternating multiplicity space, count whole hyperbolic pairs. For a paired type of nonisomorphic duals, count one copy of the pair.

### The degree-two tests

Let $H$ be the vector space of forward tuples $X=(X_v)$ satisfying every directed-arrow equation. Work over the input field and interpret an identity on $H$ as a polynomial identity after parametrizing $H$ by a linear basis. Let $L$ be the space of all scalar linear functionals on the product of the target bilinear edge matrix spaces. The first test asks whether some $\lambda\in L$ satisfies
$$
 \lambda((X_tA_eX_s^T)_e)=0\ \text{as a polynomial on }H,
 \qquad
 \lambda((B_e)_e)\ne0 .
$$
The second asks whether at some vertex $v$ there are $C\in\operatorname{End}(A_v)$ and $D\in\operatorname{End}(B_v)$ with
$$
 X_vC=DX_v\quad(X\in H),\qquad
 \operatorname{tr}(C)-\operatorname{tr}(D)\ne0 .
$$
Here $A_v,B_v$ denote the vertex spaces. The tests are Gaussian elimination, including over finite fields, where polynomial coefficients are compared directly. When there are only bipartite form edges and all vertex spaces on both inputs have positive dimension, the inverse test just detects a trace-visible dimension gap. The first test asks whether for some ordered endpoint pair a scalar dependency among the source parallel-edge matrices fails for the corresponding target matrices. Order all edges coherently by transposing matrices as needed.

To prove the tests, first discard literal zero axioms. Until a nonzero constant appears, the only degree-one consequences within degree two are linear combinations of the displayed homogeneous linear equations. Every other axiom has homogeneous quadratic part plus constant. At degree two, legal multiplications of the degree-one consequences generate precisely the quadratic part of the ideal generated by the linear equations. Thus pass to the quotient by this linear ideal. In this quotient the homogeneous quadratic parts of the form equations have bidegree $(2,0)$ in $(X,Y)$, whereas those of the inverse equations have bidegree $(1,1)$. These cannot cancel each other. A combination of form entries becomes a nonzero constant exactly in the first test. A combination of inverse entries has the form
$$
 \sum_v\big(\operatorname{tr}(C_v Y_vX_v)-\operatorname{tr}(D_v X_vY_v)\big)
 -\sum_v(\operatorname{tr}C_v-\operatorname{tr}D_v).
$$
The homogeneous part vanishes exactly when the coefficient of each independent $Y_v$ is zero identically on $H$. This is $X_v C_v=D_v X_v$ by nondegeneracy of the rectangular trace pairing. The combined trace gap is nonzero only if one vertex has a nonzero gap. Either test can therefore be converted back into a legal PC proof of degree at most two. In the pure bipartite specialization each form entry at an output coordinate pair uses independent row variables, with coefficients indexed by the ordered input coordinate pair. For each pair of vertices, coefficient cancellation is exactly a dependency of parallel-arrow matrices. The inverse criterion follows by testing the individual matrix units as $X_v$. No degree-one proof can reach a nonzero constant after the degree-zero case.

### Doubled quiver and upper bound

Make a doubled quiver with each vertex $v$ and a counterpart $v^*$. Duplicate $s\to t$ by the reverse-dual arrow $t^*\to s^*$ with transposed input matrix. A form edge with matrix $A_e$ of shape $A_t$ by $A_s$ gives an arrow $s^*\to t$ with matrix $A_e$ and the dual arrow $t^*\to s$ with matrix $A_e^T$. The doubled input is strictly selfadjoint for transpose duality. Put $f_v=X_v$ and $f_{v^*}=Y_v^T$. The formal adjoint has $f^*_v=Y_v$ and $f^*_{v^*}=X_v^T$. The node identities for the two products of $f,f^*$ are raw axioms or their transposes.

In degree three derive that $f$ intertwines the doubled arrows. For a form use
$$
 X_tA_e-B_eY_s^T=(X_tA_eX_s^T-B_e)Y_s^T-X_tA_e(Y_sX_s-I)^T .
$$
The same identity with the transposed form gives the other arrow. For each directed arrow with $G=X_tA_e-B_eX_s$ derive the reverse equation from
$$
 A_eY_s-Y_tB_e
 =Y_tG Y_s+(I-Y_tX_t)A_eY_s+Y_tB_e(X_sY_s-I).
$$
Transpose for its dual arrow. These identities work for unequal vertex dimensions. Every displayed product is implemented entrywise by successive multiplication by one variable, with scalar linear combinations. [Lean identities](MixedGraphIdentities.lean)

Project $f$ linearly onto $\operatorname{Hom}(R_A,R_B)$ of the doubled inputs. Its error entries are combinations of the prepared linear equations. If $\widehat f$ is the projection, both product equations for $\widehat f,\widehat f^*$ follow in degree three by telescoping. The adjoint preserves the radical in the finite-dimensional algebra $\operatorname{End}(R_A\oplus R_B)$, so pass to its semisimple quotient. This is a product of matrix algebras of scalar multiplicities indexed by indecomposable ordinary quiver types over $K$. On paired factors the adjoint exchanges the two. On a fixed factor it gives adjunction by the scalar multiplicity form. The quotient of each product equation is a scalar linear combination of prepared equations, extending each quotient coordinate from the endomorphism space to all node matrices.

In characteristic different from two, the selfadjoint decomposition theorem supplies the following normalized types. A non-selfdual type $U$ contributes the exchange-dual block $U\oplus U^*$. An isodual type has an invertible symmetric or skew-adjoint reference to its dual. To see the distinction, a reference $S$ has one of $S+S^*$ or $S-S^*$ invertible, since after identifying with the original local endomorphism algebra the nonunits form an ideal. On its scalar multiplicity spaces the first convention yields symmetric forms and the second yields alternating forms. Normalize the nonsingular symmetric forms to the identity and the alternating forms to standard hyperbolic planes. The selfadjoint decomposition theorem lifts these classifications from the scalar quotient and yields the stated direct sums, with one common representative of each type across the two inputs. It applies to representations with degenerate original bilinear edges and to diagrams with maps as well. [Sergeichuk, Sections 1–2](https://imath.kiev.ua/~sergeich/sergeichuk_files/1988_izvest.pdf)

In a mismatched dual pair, the projected equations give two ordinary rectangular inverse maps. In a mismatched symmetric factor they give the signed rectangle, where the second map is the transpose. In a mismatched alternating factor they give the symplectic rectangle and its adjoint. The ordinary, signed and symplectic rectangular degree results each supply a proof in degree $\delta(r_C,s_C)$, counting planes rather than scalar dimensions in the symplectic case. In a missing factor the larger identity specializes to a literal nonzero constant after taking the quotient. Take the cheapest discrepancy and replay its proof on the exposed linear coordinates. [Ordinary rectangles](ConjugacyDegree.md) · [Signed rectangles](SymmetricCongruenceDegree.md) · [Symplectic rectangles](GeneralCongruenceDegree.md)

For a pure bipartite form graph in any characteristic, skip the selfadjoint classification. Orient every arrow from the right part to the left and take just the vertex maps $Y_s^T$ on the right and $X_t$ on the left. The first identity above prepares these linear arrows. Their inverses are $X_s^T$ and $Y_t$, and reverse-arrow preparation follows either by the transposed form identity or by the directed-arrow identity. Krull–Schmidt on this ordinary quiver supplies scalar multiplicities. The projected product equations give two independent rectangular maps per type. This proves the same upper formula over every field.

### Matching lower bound

In normalized coordinates assign a separate rectangle to each discrepant type of positive multiplicities. For an exchange-dual type choose forward rectangular matrices $P,Q^T$ on the $U,U^*$ copy indices and reverse matrices $Q,P^T$ on their respective copy indices. Off-diagonal type entries are zero. Tensor these maps with identity on the fixed underlying indecomposable vertex spaces. Then the form axioms become scalar multiples of rectangular inverse entries, with constants from the shared normalized block. All directed-arrow equations vanish. For a symmetric type, apply the same construction with $Q=P^T$. For an alternating type use a single matrix on whole hyperbolic planes, tensoring with the two-dimensional identity on each plane, and use its transpose for the reverse. This again reduces to a signed rectangle on the **number of planes**. Matched types are fixed identically. In the pure bipartite case on the undoubled quiver use $P$ on left copy indices, $Q^T$ on right copy indices, and the corresponding reverse maps. Every form then yields $PQ-I$ on the target multiplicity index, while vertex inverses give the two rectangular identities wherever the type is supported.

An arbitrary basis change on each input is implemented by homogeneous linear substitutions on $X,Y$. A transformed raw axiom is a scalar linear combination of the matching raw axioms in the two bases. Substitutions preserve all degree bounds of at least two, including when intermediate degrees fall, by applying linearity at each multiplication. For a missing type the degree-three result is already exact after the low-degree tests, so suppose every discrepant type has positive counts. The above specialization replays any proof on a disjoint union of rectangular systems. Their least refutation degree is the minimum of the separate degrees. For completeness, through a fixed degree $d$ choose in each factor a degree-filtered vector-space basis adapted also to the degree-$d$ consequence space, taking the constant $1$ outside it if the factor has no such refutation. The total-degree-$d$ tensor basis elements with at least one consequence factor span a subspace closed under all legal steps, since multiplication into an already consequence-bearing factor is permitted through degree $d$. It contains the axioms and excludes $1$ if each factor excludes $1$. This proves the necessary lower bound. Finally the bounded-degree closure consists only of linear spans, degree-filtered intersections, and multiplication by coordinate variables on the appropriate filtered piece. These commute with scalar extension, so the formula holds over the input field. [Detailed rectangle lower bounds](ConjugacyDegree.md) · [Signed specialization](SymmetricCongruenceDegree.md)

### Matrix equivalence and simultaneous tuples

For one edge with fixed positive source and target sides $m,n$, let $r<s$ be the ranks of the two matrices in increasing order. The arrow of rank $r$ decomposes into $r$ identity arrows, $m-r$ sink simples and $n-r$ source simples, and similarly for rank $s$. Unless the displayed source matrix is zero and the target is nonzero, the exact minimum degree is
$$
\max\big(3,\;\min\{\delta(r,s),\delta(m-s,m-r),\delta(n-s,n-r)\}\big).
$$
Here the characteristic-two case is included. Galesi, Grochow, Pitassi and She give the degree-three upper bound for a trace-visible rank gap in Proposition 3.1. The formula also gives the exact answer for gaps divisible by the characteristic. For example take complementary ranks $r,r+q$ at side $2r+q$, with $r$ and the characteristic power $q$ proportional to the side. There is linear minimum PC degree already for matrix non-equivalence, even though equivalence itself is a rank computation. For parallel edges the same theorem applies to all based matrix tuples without a bound on the number of matrices or a normal-form promise. The general theorems are written proofs. The two polynomial preparation identities have independent Lean verification. [Earlier upper bound](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19320#S3.SS1)
