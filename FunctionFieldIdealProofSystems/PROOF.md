# Boolean Ideal Proof Systems and identity testing over function fields

Grochow asked in §7.2 whether the connections between Polynomial Identity Testing and the Ideal Proof System extend to function fields. For every fixed finitely generated field of positive characteristic we give the same-field equivalence for Boolean UNSAT. We also compare formal PIT and Boolean IPS over a finitely generated field with their versions over its prime field, in either characteristic. The statement uses a multiplication-basis representation of the fixed field, described below. [Grochow, Theorem 6.1 and §7.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.02184v1#S7.SS2)

## Theorem and input model

Let $F$ be a fixed finite field or $\mathbb Q$. Fix an algebraically independent tuple $t=(t_1,\ldots,t_d)$ over $F$, let $L=F(t)$, and let $K/L$ be a fixed finite field extension, possibly inseparable. Fix an $L$-basis $b_0=1,b_1,\ldots,b_{e-1}$ of $K$ and its multiplication table. Encode a field element as the $e$ coordinates of that element, with each coordinate given as a fraction of sparse polynomials over $F$, with binary-encoded exponents. A sparse polynomial can be put in collected form by sorting and combining duplicate exponents. The fixed basis and its structure constants are part of the field model. This applies to every fixed finitely generated field over $F$, by choosing a transcendence basis and then a vector space basis of the finite extension.

1. Formal PIT for division-free circuits over $K$ and over $F$ is polynomial-time many-one equivalent. The reduction from $K$ still works on legal circuits allowing inverse gates whose inputs are syntactically constant and nonzero, and outputs a division-free circuit over $F$.
2. For Boolean UNSAT in the full arithmetic-circuit IPS with the paper's bit-size measure, any proof over $K$ has a proof over $F$ of polynomially related size, and conversely. In characteristic zero the proof conversion allows the usual inverse gates on nonzero syntactically constant inputs. In positive characteristic the descended proof over $F$ can be taken division-free.
3. If $F$ is finite, a Cook–Reckhow proof system polynomially simulates Boolean IPS over $K$ if and only if formal PIT over $K$ belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$. In characteristic zero this same-field assertion for $K$ is equivalent to the corresponding assertion for $\mathbb Q$.

Part 2 concerns existence of proofs related by a polynomial size bound. The choice of a valid specialization can be verified in polynomial bit time in positive characteristic. A deterministic search for a good specialization of general circuit denominators is not required.

## 1. Multiplication coordinates and clearing denominators

Write $b_i b_j=\sum_\ell m_{ij\ell}(t)b_\ell$. Simulate a $K$-circuit by coordinate circuits over $L$. An indeterminate $x$ has coordinates $(x,0,\ldots,0)$. Addition is componentwise and coordinate $\ell$ of a product is $\sum_{i,j}m_{ij\ell}(t)u_i v_j$. The simulation preserves circuit sharing. If an inverse gate is allowed, its operand is syntactically constant. The corresponding multiplication matrix $M$ in the fixed basis is invertible, since its operand is a nonzero element of $K$. The coordinate vector of the inverse is $\operatorname{adj}(M)e_0/\det M$, using $e_0=(1,0,\ldots,0)$. This uses only a constant number of additional gates per such input gate for fixed $K$. The same construction is polynomial in explicit basis size using determinant circuits. [Grochow, Definition 5.1 and Lemma 5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.02184v1#S5.SS1)

Now keep all fraction numerators and denominators as pairs of division-free circuits over $F$, leaving $t$ as algebraic indeterminates. For $(a,b),(c,d)$ use $(ad+bc,bd)$ for addition and $(ac,bd)$ for multiplication. The inverse swap is applied only to a pair whose numerator is nonzero, as legal inverse gates have nonzero constant input. Every denominator is a nonzero polynomial in $t$ alone. At a scalar leaf, build the numerator and denominator circuits for the sparse representation by repeated squaring. The binary exponent encoding and gate sharing give size polynomial in the input length, even when the numerator has exponentially many terms or exponential degree after expansion.

If the coordinate numerators at the output are $A_0(t,x),\ldots,A_{e-1}(t,x)$, output the formal polynomial $\sum_{i=0}^{e-1} z^i A_i(t,x)$ over $F$, with $z$ a new indeterminate. Algebraic independence of the $t_i$, independence of the basis over $L$, and independence of the powers of $z$ ensure it is zero exactly when the original polynomial was zero. This gives the desired reduction. Inclusion of $F$ into $K$ is the reverse reduction, with division-free inputs sufficient for the equivalence. If the chosen syntax treats validity of arbitrary inversions as a separate decision problem, the first direction here is a statement about circuits whose inversions are legal, as in the field-arithmetic model. In identifying the NP language below it suffices to take division-free PIT. Inverses of syntactically constant subcircuits that occur in IPS proofs can be validated nondeterministically by certificates of nonzero, explained in §3.

## 2. Specialization of Boolean certificates

Let the CNF equations $P_i(x)$ and the Boolean axioms be viewed over $F$. Their coefficients are in the prime field. An IPS certificate is a circuit $C(x,y)$ satisfying $C(x,0)=0$ and $C(x,P(x))=1$. The output coordinate at $b_0=1$ of the simulation in §1 gives a certificate over $L$. Indeed each coordinate of polynomial coefficient substitution $y\mapsto P(x)$ is the substitution into the same coordinate polynomial, because $P_i$ have coefficients in the base. The two target polynomials have coordinate at $b_0$ equal to themselves. This applies also to deriving any polynomial over $F$ from equations over $F$.

Represent the intermediate $L$-coordinates by the numerator-denominator circuit pairs of §1. There are polynomially many nonzero denominators $D_i(t)$. Their product has degree at most $2^{\operatorname{poly}(s)}$, where $s$ is the original certificate bit length. Indeed the pair circuits use polynomially many fan-in-two multiplications, and their coefficient leaves are field constants or indeterminates after sparse inputs are expanded by repeated squaring. The degree has the indicated bound even if each pair is shared by later pairs. All inverse operations needed for the final $L$-circuit have been exposed in these denominators.

For finite $F$, choose a finite extension $F'/F$ whose cardinality exceeds this bound. Its degree over $F$ is polynomial in $s$. By the evaluation lemma there exists $a\in (F')^d$ at which the product of the $D_i$ is nonzero. This specializes every coefficient of the proof and of its intermediate arithmetic. The original two certificate identities persist because specialization is a ring map on the localization inverting those denominators. All syntactically constant subcircuits can be evaluated over $F'$ in polynomial bit time using a polynomial-basis representation. The resulting $F'$-certificate has polynomial bit length. Finally expand its gates in a basis of $F'/F$ starting with $1$, and take the unit coordinate again. We now have a proof over $F$. This proof uses division only when evaluating finite-field constants, so its circuit can be division-free.

When $F=\mathbb Q$, choose $a$ in an integer grid larger than the degree bound. Its coordinates have polynomial bit length. Preserve the circuits for the values $D_i(a)$ and use their inverses as legal syntactically constant inversion gates. This avoids writing down evaluated rational constants that could have exponential bit length. The same specialization homomorphism preserves the two required identities. In both cases scalar inclusion yields the reverse proof-size simulation.

## 3. The same-field equivalence over function fields of positive characteristic

Suppose first that $F$ is finite and a Cook–Reckhow proof system for Boolean UNSAT polynomially simulates $\mathrm{IPS}_K$. It simulates $\mathrm{IPS}_F$, since an $F$-certificate is a $K$-certificate under scalar inclusion. The finite-field same-field argument gives $\mathrm{PIT}_F\in\mathsf{NP}$. In that argument an input circuit over $F$ is tested over a finite extension of degree linear in the circuit size, sufficiently large to distinguish every nonzero formal polynomial of the input's possible degree from zero. The gate equations, an equation requiring the output to have an inverse, and the bit encoding in Grochow's Theorem 6.1 give a CNF satisfiable exactly for nonzero inputs. When the circuit is zero, the construction supplies a polynomial-size IPS certificate over the chosen extension. The two formal IPS identities and the prime-field CNF coefficients allow unit-coordinate descent to $F$, regardless of which extension polynomial was guessed. A simulation proof can therefore be guessed and verified in polynomial time. Conversely every allowed extension is large enough to detect nonzero, and irreducibility of the guessed polynomial is deterministically checkable by Frobenius powers and gcds. This is precisely the saved finite-field argument. Applying §1 gives $\mathrm{PIT}_K\in\mathsf{NP}$. [Grochow, Theorem 6.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.02184v1#S6)

Conversely, a verifier for $\mathrm{PIT}_K$ in $\mathsf{NP}$ turns a claimed IPS certificate together with verification witnesses for its two identities into a polynomial-time checkable proof. Guessing internal inverse legality can also be handled if the certificate syntax includes constant inversions: bottom-up numerator-denominator simulation yields a division-free coordinate-tagged polynomial over $F$ for each denominator's nonzero condition. Over fixed finite $F$ a nonzero polynomial presented by such a circuit has a polynomial-bit witness, an explicit evaluation in a finite extension larger than its degree upper bound. These can be checked deterministically after guessing valid extension presentations. For $F=\mathbb Q$, nonzero also has such a witness by reducing integer-cleared circuits modulo a suitable polynomial-bit prime and evaluating over a finite extension. Indeed denominators and at least one nonzero numerator coefficient have bit length at most $2^{\operatorname{poly}(s)}$ by gate induction after removing scalar fractions, so they have at most $2^{\operatorname{poly}(s)}$ bad primes. Some polynomial-bit prime avoids them, from the ordinary prime counting bound. This is only an existential witness for a **nonzero** outcome. Zero witnesses for the two certificate identities are supplied by the assumed PIT verifier, after valid inverses have been confirmed. Thus the verification test is sound and complete and the resulting Cook–Reckhow system polynomially simulates $\mathrm{IPS}_K$. The characteristic-zero assertion is equivalent to the unresolved rational one by §§1–2.

## 4. Variety Emptiness in both characteristics

Take a system of polynomials given by circuits over the fixed field $K$, and take emptiness over its algebraic closure. There is a Cook–Reckhow proof system polynomially simulating $\mathrm{IPS}_K$ for this problem if and only if formal PIT over $K$ is in $\mathsf{NP}$.

The PIT verifier checks the two IPS identities as in §3. Every empty system has an IPS certificate by the Nullstellensatz. If one first applies the theorem over an algebraic closure, finite-dimensional linear solvability over $K$ descends a certificate of the same finite degree. So the verifier's image is precisely the set of empty systems.

For the other implication, introduce a variable $g_v$ for each gate of a division-free circuit $U$ and add the equations specifying gate values, followed by $1-zg_{\mathrm{out}}=0$. If $U$ is nonzero, it attains a nonzero value over the infinite algebraic closure and the system has a solution. If $U$ is zero, a small certificate derives $g_v-U_v(x)$ for every gate, preserving the circuit's sharing. For a product gate add its gate equation to $(g_i-U_i)g_j+U_i(g_j-U_j)$, with the factored differences coming from the preceding derivations. Addition and subtraction use linear combinations. Finally add $z(g_{\mathrm{out}}-U)$ to $1-zg_{\mathrm{out}}$, giving 1. Each derivation uses equations as placeholders and vanishes when they are all set to zero. The certificate has polynomial bit length. A proof in the simulated system thus serves as an NP witness for PIT. This is Grochow's Lemma 3.1 and Theorem 3.3, with the same construction over $K$. [Grochow, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.02184v1#S3)

## Source audit of the characteristic-zero prime choice

The argument above does not use the rational-to-finite-field direction of Theorem 6.1 for arbitrary rational circuits. Existence of a good polynomial-bit prime gives an NP witness of nonidentity, as in §3. To accept a proof of zero modulo a guessed prime as an NP witness of identity, one needs a criterion guaranteeing that every accepted prime preserves nonzeroness. The coefficient-bit bound in Observation 2.3 cannot supply this guarantee as written. For example, the constant-free circuit $1+1$ has syntactic degree one, yet the prime 2 annihilates its output. We use the finite-field branch of the Boolean construction and the same-characteristic reductions independently of this issue. [Grochow, Observation 2.3 and Lemma 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.02184v1#S4)

There is also a direct counterexample to the specific assertion in Lemma 4.1 that *any* prime above $\max(2^s,d)$ works, for the usual compact binary gate-list encoding. A constant-free circuit for $2^{1024}+1$ has 13 gates: a 1 input, a doubling gate, ten squarings, and a final addition of 1. Four-bit references and two-bit opcodes suffice, so its bit length is below 200 in such an encoding. The tenth Fermat number has a proved prime factor of 252 decimal digits, above $2^{800}$, at which this nonzero constant vanishes. Good primes still exist. The example concerns the stated prime-size criterion and the soundness of guessing a prime by that criterion. [Brent, *Factorization of the tenth Fermat number*](https://maths-people.anu.edu.au/~brent/pub/pub161.html)

## Reuse and relationship to existing work

The multiplication coordinates are the finite-extension vectorization already used in Grochow's proof. The new step is the specialization of the coefficient variables of a function field after finite-degree descent. This avoids needing a field homomorphism from $K$ to a smaller field or a separable presentation for the finite part of $K$. It proves the same-field equivalence for Boolean UNSAT over finitely generated fields of positive characteristic and reduces its characteristic-zero version to $\mathbb Q$. The cited question leaves this function-field case open. Rational-function evaluation and the evaluation lemma used in the specialization are standard. [Grochow, §7.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.02184v1#S7.SS2) · [Hu–van Melkebeek–Morgan, *Polynomial Identity Testing via Evaluation of Rational Functions*](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v020a001/)

This is an informal proof.
