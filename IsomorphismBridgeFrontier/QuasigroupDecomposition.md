# Polynomial-time direct product decomposition of finite quasigroups

**Theorem.** Given the multiplication table of an arbitrary finite quasigroup, one can compute in deterministic polynomial time a direct product decomposition into directly indecomposable quasigroups. The output includes the factor tables and the coordinate map on the original labels. This gives a shorter proof of the answer to Question 7.4 of Johnson, Levet, Vojtěchovský and Widholm, and extends it to several jointly supplied multiary operations in §5. [Original question](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06478v3#S7) · [Earlier MathProofs answer](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/CayleyCircuitsAndDecompositions/QuasigroupDecomposition.md)

The proof uses permutation-group factorization with operators, and finite-abelian linear equations to turn one abstract group decomposition into an action-compatible decomposition. The latter step controls how the point stabilizer meets the factors.

## 1. A transitive group that respects quasigroup products

Let $Q$ be a quasigroup on $n$ points. For $x\in Q$, let $L_x(y)=xy$ and $R_x(y)=yx$ denote its two translations. Set $M=\langle L_x,R_x:x\in Q\rangle$. Define $D$ to be the normal closure in $M$ of all $L_xL_y^{-1}$ and $R_xR_y^{-1}$. Fix a point $e\in Q$, put $H=D_e$, and let $\Omega$ consist of the two automorphisms of $D$ given by conjugation by $L_e,R_e$.

Johnson, Levet, Vojtěchovský and Widholm use left translation differences to detect group isotopy in their Proposition 6.4. The group here uses differences on both sides and their normal closure to keep the action of every translation. [Displacement group in the question paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06478v3#S6)

The group $D$ acts transitively. Indeed, for fixed $y$, the points $L_xL_y^{-1}(e)$ exhaust $Q$ as $x$ varies. Also $M=\langle D,L_e,R_e\rangle$, since a left or right translation differs from the corresponding base translation by a generator of $D$.

Suppose $Q=Q_1\times Q_2$ in some product coordinates. Left and right translations act componentwise, so $M$ is inside $M(Q_1)\times M(Q_2)$ and projects onto both factors. A left translation difference with the same second coordinate is a difference in the first factor alone, and all first-factor left translation differences arise this way. Right differences and the second factor work the same way. Conjugation by $M$ on each individual factor realizes every necessary conjugation by the corresponding $M(Q_i)$. Conversely, every defining difference has both components in the corresponding normal closures. These two inclusions give
$$
D(Q_1\times Q_2)=D(Q_1)\times D(Q_2).
$$
Both factors are $\Omega$-stable, and the point stabilizer is the product of the two component stabilizers.

Conversely, suppose $D=A\times B$, where $A,B$ are nontrivial $\Omega$-stable subgroups and $H=(H\cap A)\times(H\cap B)$. The transitive $D$-set $Q\cong D/H$ is the Cartesian product of $A/(H\cap A)$ and $B/(H\cap B)$. The two equality-of-coordinate relations are the orbit relations of $B$ and $A$, respectively. Both are preserved by $M$ because $A$ and $B$ are normal in $D$ and are normalized by $L_e,R_e$. For either coordinate, $L_x(y)$ then shows that the coordinate of $xy$ depends on $y$ only through that coordinate of $y$. The identity $xy=R_y(x)$ shows the same for $x$. Thus multiplication is coordinatewise. All translations on the coordinate sets are permutations because the original translations are permutations preserving the equivalence relations in both directions. The resulting factors are quasigroups. Each has order greater than one. If, for example, $A=H\cap A$, then the normal subgroup $A$ fixes $e$, hence every point by transitivity, contrary to faithful action and $A\ne1$.

It remains to find such a split $D=A\times B$ in polynomial time whenever one exists.

## 2. Reducing to polynomially many abstract group splits

We prove the group step for any transitive permutation group $D$ of degree $n$ with a point stabilizer $H$, and operators given by conjugations by specified permutations normalizing $D$. Write $Z=Z(D)$. Any element of $Z$ fixing the base point fixes every point, so $H\cap Z=1$ and $|Z|\le n$.

Apply Wilson's Theorem 6.4 to find a Remak $\Omega$-decomposition, that is, a fully refined internal direct product of $\Omega$-stable factors $D_1,\ldots,D_t$. The permutation group and its operators are supplied by their generators and the images of generators. A constructive presentation and straight-line programs are available in the polynomial-time toolkit of the same paper. [Wilson, §2.6 and Theorem 6.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1005.0548)

We have $t\le2\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$. To see this, start from the trivial group and add one factor whose addition strictly enlarges the orbit of the base point. This remains possible until the selected product acts transitively. If no unselected factor enlarged the current orbit, each would preserve that orbit setwise, so their full product would preserve it. Each strict enlargement multiplies the orbit size by an integer at least two because the previous selected product is normal in the enlarged group, whose orbit is partitioned into equal-sized orbits of that previous product. The product of the remaining factors centralizes the selected transitive group, hence acts freely and has order at most $n$. There are at most $\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ remaining factors. Thus all partitions of this Remak decomposition into two unions of factors can be enumerated in polynomial time.

For a partition write $D=A_0\times B_0$ and let $p:D\to A_0$ be the projection. Use the partitions for which both $A_0$ and $B_0$ have nontrivial image in $D/Z$. If there is a desired split $D=A\times B$ whose factors are both noncentral, refine that split further as a Remak $\Omega$-decomposition. The $\Omega$-group Krull–Schmidt theorem takes the computed Remak decomposition to this refinement by an automorphism $\alpha$ commuting with both the operators and all inner automorphisms. Such an automorphism induces the identity on $D/Z$, since $\operatorname{Inn}(\alpha(g))=\operatorname{Inn}(g)$ for every $g$. So for some partition, the projection onto $A$ agrees with $p$ modulo $Z$. [Wilson, Theorem 2.10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1005.0548#page=5)

## 3. Correcting projections by finite-abelian linear equations

For each partition from §2, look for an $\Omega$-equivariant homomorphism $\delta:D\to Z$ such that $f(g)=p(g)\delta(g)$ maps $H$ into $H$. This is a system of finite-abelian linear equations. For each generator $h$ of $H$, scan $Z$ for $z$ with $p(h)z\in H$. If none exists, discard the partition. There is at most one such $z$, because $H\cap Z=1$. Impose $\delta(h)=z$ for the element found. Additionally require $\delta(\omega(g))=\omega(\delta(g))$ for each supplied operator and each generator $g$ of $D$.

Here is an explicit implementation of the linear systems. Compute a constructive presentation of $D$ on permutation generators $x_1,\dots,x_m$. Use one variable $v_i\in Z$ for $\delta(x_i)$. For every relator, add the zero equation on its signed exponent sum in the $v_i$, using additive notation on $Z$. The equations for $h$ and $\omega(x_i)$ use their constructive-membership straight-line programs. A straight-line program gives its exponent sums by adding or negating vectors at each instruction, with polynomial bit lengths. Compute a cyclic decomposition of the explicit abelian group $Z$, and express each conjugation operator on that basis. The resulting congruences form a system of integer linear equations with one modulus multiple per scalar equation, solved with Smith or Hermite normal form in polynomial bit time. To list $Z$, for each possible image $y$ of the base point test whether the rule $g(e)\mapsto g(y)$ gives a permutation centralizing $D$. Build the candidate using Schreier paths and verify commutation with every generator and membership in $D$. Transitivity shows this enumerates the whole center, with at most $n$ candidates. Schreier–Sims also supplies generators of $H$. [Wilson, §2.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1005.0548#page=8)

For the partition corresponding to a desired split, the difference between its desired projection $p'$ and $p$ is precisely such a central homomorphism. In detail, $p'(g)=p(g)\delta(g)$ with $\delta(g)\in Z$, and since $p,p'$ are homomorphisms and the differences are central, $\delta(gg')=\delta(g)\delta(g')$. The projections are equivariant, so $\delta$ is equivariant. The desired projection preserves $H$ by hypothesis. Therefore the constraints for this partition are feasible.

If a desired split has one central factor, we use the same linear system with $p=1$ (the trivial map in multiplicative notation). Impose $\delta(h)=1$ on each generator of $H$, and for some $z\in Z\setminus\{1\}$ require $\delta(z)=z$. Enumerate the at most $n-1$ possible $z$. If $D$ is abelian, also enumerate $w\in Z\setminus\{1\}$ and require $\delta(w)=1$. The projection to a nontrivial central factor satisfies the equations for a nonidentity $z$ in that factor. In the abelian case choose $w$ in the other factor. In either case the projection kills $H$ since it preserves $H$ and $H\cap Z=1$.

## 4. Fitting decomposition and output

Every returned $f=p\cdot\delta$ is a group endomorphism. It is normal in the sense that it commutes with all inner automorphisms of $D$, since $p$ is a direct product projection and $\delta$ is central. It also commutes with $\Omega$ and preserves $H$. Take $k$ such that the image of $f^k$ stabilizes. Then
$$
D=\operatorname{im}(f^k)\times\ker(f^k).
$$
Indeed $f$ is bijective on the stable image, so the two subgroups intersect trivially, and their orders multiply to $|D|$. They commute because for $a=f^k(x)$ and $b\in\ker(f^k)$, normality gives $[a,b]=f^k([x,b])=[f^k(x),f^k(b)]=1$. The factors are $\Omega$-stable. A sufficiently high power of $f$ is the identity on the stable image and kills the stable kernel, hence is the projection onto the stable image. That power preserves $H$, so the two factors split $H$ as required. This use of a high power proves correctness. The algorithm only computes the stable image and kernel.

In the noncentral partition case the induced endomorphism modulo $Z$ is the projection induced by $p$ at every positive power. Its image is nontrivial and proper, since $D/Z=(A_0/Z(A_0))\times(B_0/Z(B_0))$. It follows that the stable image and kernel are both nontrivial. In the central case $z$ lies in the stable image, and $\delta(D)\subseteq Z$ is proper when $D$ is nonabelian. When $D$ is abelian, the prescribed $w$ is a nontrivial kernel element. Thus the central case also yields two proper action factors.

The image stabilizes after at most $\lfloor\log_2|D|\rfloor$ strict drops, and $\log_2|D|\le\log_2(n!)$. Compute successive powers by evaluating $f$ on the previous images of its generators using constructive membership. Compare generated image orders with Schreier–Sims. For the kernel of the stable power, form its graph as the subgroup on two disjoint copies of the permutation domain generated by the pairs $(x_i,f^k(x_i))$. Fix the second copy pointwise and restrict the remaining generators to the first copy. This produces the kernel in polynomial time.

The normal closure defining $D$ is also polynomial-time computable. Start with the translation differences and repeatedly adjoin a conjugate of a current generator by one of the generators of $M$ or its inverse whenever that conjugate lies outside the current subgroup. Each such addition at least doubles the order, so at most $\lfloor\log_2(n!)\rfloor$ additions occur. At termination the subgroup is the required normal closure. Each membership check is a permutation-group calculation of polynomial size.

After finding a split, compute the two orbit partitions from the factor generators, build the two quotient quasigroup tables, and recurse. If every linear system is infeasible, §§2–3 exclude any nontrivial product decomposition. Every split replaces an order-$m$ input with factors of orders at least two whose product is $m$, so there are at most $\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ terminal factors when $n\ge2$ and at most twice as many recursive calls. The order-one table is immediate. Compose the coordinate maps to obtain the required output. This proves the theorem.

## 5. Several quasigroup operations

The same algorithm applies when the input has several explicitly tabulated operations, each of which is Latin in each argument and may have arity greater than two, and at least one operation has arity at least two. One can also include unary permutations and constants. Form $M$ from the translations in every argument and the unary permutations. Use the normal closure of differences between translations in the same operation and argument. It is transitive because one can vary a frozen argument in an operation of arity at least two. For each operation and argument one base translation suffices as an operator, in addition to the unary permutations. In a product, differences in any given operation and argument can be realized independently in each factor by holding every other factor coordinate fixed. Conversely a Cartesian split preserving all translations gives coordinatewise dependence by varying one argument at a time. Constants project coordinatewise. The group algorithm therefore yields jointly indecomposable factors in time polynomial in the total table size.

## 6. Prescribed coordinate equalities and all two-factor splits

The algorithm can find an ordered nontrivial two-factor split satisfying any supplied lists of pairs required to agree in the first or second coordinate. For a pair of points $g(e),g'(e)$, use $d=g^{-1}g'$ obtained from Schreier paths. For a true projection onto the first factor, agreement in that factor means $f(d)\in H$, and agreement in the second means $f(d)\in dH$. Each is one extra equation on $\delta(d)$, found by scanning $Z$. As above, $H\cap Z=1$ makes the answer unique when it exists. If the condition is $f(d)\in H$, all subsequent powers of $f$ preserve it. If the condition is $f(d)\in dH$, then $f(dh)\in f(d)H\subseteq dH$ for all $h\in H$, so all subsequent powers preserve that condition too. The stable projection therefore satisfies every requested agreement. For the central-factor tests, use both choices of which ordered factor is the central image, swapping the requirements before solving when the image is the second factor.

The finitely many affine systems give a polynomial-size description of all ordered nontrivial two-factor splits of the original table. A split here is specified by its ordered pair of coordinate congruences on the original elements. Each solution decodes to a split by stable image and kernel, and any split is the decoding of its own projection in one of the listed systems. This representation can give several descriptions of the same split.

The proof is informal. Wilson's theorem concerns abstract direct products of permutation groups with operators. The earlier MathProofs proof of the binary case uses a principal loop isotope and submodular minimization on the noncentral coordinates. The raw displacement action and the logarithmic bound on the number of Remak factors give an alternative direct search. Publication priority for the multioperation extension is unestablished.
