# Deterministic matrix-tuple symmetrization in characteristic two

This informal proof answers the symmetrization part of the first open problem following Corollary 9 in Gábor Ivanyos and Youming Qiao, *Algorithms based on *-algebras, and their applications to isomorphism of polynomials with one secret, group isomorphism, and polynomial identity testing*. Their Theorem 8 handles characteristic different from two. The same open problem also asks for a characteristic-two tuple-isometry algorithm, a separate task. [Ivanyos–Qiao, Theorem 8 and open problems](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.03495#page=8)

**Theorem.** Let $F$ be any field of characteristic two. Given $B_1,\dots,B_m\in M_n(F)$, a deterministic algorithm polynomial in $m,n$ in the field-arithmetic model decides whether there are $A,D\in\operatorname{GL}_n(F)$ with every $AB_iD$ symmetric, and returns such matrices on yes-instances. The same holds with “alternating” in place of “symmetric”. Over finite $F$ the algorithms run in deterministic polynomial bit time in the standard explicit field representation.

The two signs of the paper's transpose condition coincide in characteristic two. The alternating version explicitly requires zero diagonal in addition to symmetry.

## 1. Reduction to one multiplier

Write $L(B)$ for the linear space of matrices $Z$ such that every $ZB_i$ has the desired property. Use this notation independently for the symmetric and alternating variants. Both properties are invariant under congruence. Thus $AB_iD$ has the desired property exactly when $D^{-T}AB_i$ does, and it suffices to find an invertible member of $L(B)$.

The finite-field algorithm below handles degenerate tuples directly. For the large-field algorithm, let $R=\bigcap_i\ker B_i$ and $W=\sum_i\operatorname{im}B_i$. Any invertible symmetrizer takes $W$ onto the annihilator of $R$, so $\dim W=n-\dim R$ is necessary. When this equality holds, choose bases putting the tuple into blocks given by the induced maps from $F^n/R$ to $W$, with zero blocks on the remaining coordinates. An invertible symmetrizer for the original tuple induces one for the reduced tuple. A symmetrizer of the reduced tuple extends by any perfect pairing on the zero blocks. Return a witness in the original coordinates using the changes of basis. This reduces to zero common kernel and full span of the images. For a zero-dimensional reduced tuple the extended witness is immediate.

## 2. Finite fields and descent

Regard the matrices as the arrows of the quiver representation $M=(U,V,(B_i:U\to V))$, with dual $DM=(V^*,U^*,(B_i^T))$. The duality interchanges the vertices. For $f:M\to DM$, let $f^*=Df$ through the canonical double-dual identification. In components $f=(P,Q)$ the arrow conditions are $QB_i=B_i^TP$. Thus $f=f^*$ means $P=Q^T$ and is exactly the symmetry condition. Requiring the matrices $QB_i$ also to have zero diagonals gives the alternating condition. These are linear conditions on $f$, and for either variant $f+f^*$ satisfies them for every morphism $f:M\to DM$.

Compute a Krull–Schmidt decomposition and match indecomposable components by their isomorphism type. Use the standard module algorithms by realizing the quiver representation as a module via the two vertex projections and the arrow operators on $U\oplus V$. The path algebra has dimension $m+2$, with no nonzero paths of length two. It is provided explicitly, with the same labels in all module comparisons. Chistov–Ivanyos–Karpinski give deterministic polynomial-time constructive module isomorphism (Corollary 3 and §2.4) and decomposition in $(\text{input size}+p)^{O(1)}$ (Theorem 5), where $p$ is the characteristic. [Chistov–Ivanyos–Karpinski, *Polynomial Time Algorithms for Modules over Finite Dimensional Algebras*](https://theory.cs.uni-bonn.de/marek/publications/85169-cs.pdf)

For each non-self-dual type $X$, an isomorphism $M\cong DM$ requires equal multiplicities of $X$ and $DX$. Equal multiplicities permit pairing a copy of each using the isomorphism $X\oplus DX\to DX\oplus X$ that interchanges the two summands by their identity maps. This is self-dual. Its associated arrow forms have zero diagonal blocks, so it works for the alternating condition as well.

For each self-dual indecomposable type $X$, compute a basis of the linear space of allowable $f:X\to DX$ by Gaussian elimination. If a basis element is invertible, its direct sums supply allowable isomorphisms for every multiplicity. Otherwise choose an ordinary isomorphism $h:X\to DX$, let $E=\operatorname{End}(X)$, and let $J$ be its Jacobson radical. By Fitting's lemma $E$ is local, so $hJ$ is precisely the space of non-isomorphisms. The division ring $E/J$ is a field by Wedderburn's little theorem. If all allowable basis vectors are singular, every allowable $f$ projects to zero in $\operatorname{Hom}(X,DX)/hJ$. The map $f\mapsto f^*$ preserves non-isomorphisms, and since $f+f^*$ is allowable, it induces the identity on this quotient in characteristic two.

On $r$ copies of $X$, the diagonal entries of any allowable isomorphism project to zero and the residues of the off-diagonal entries are symmetric, since dual transpose exchanges them. This also applies in the alternating case because restricting an alternating form to one summand is alternating. Under $h$ the residue matrix is therefore alternating over $E/J$, so it is singular when $r$ is odd. The same obstruction applies when other types are present. Identify $DM$ with $M$ by matching the indecomposables. The semisimple quotient of $\operatorname{End}(M)$ is the product, over types $Y$, of the full matrix algebras of their multiplicities over $\operatorname{End}(Y)/\operatorname{rad}\operatorname{End}(Y)$. A unit must give a unit in each component. This quotient follows from the local endomorphism rings and the Krull–Schmidt decomposition. A composition through a non-isomorphic indecomposable cannot be a unit on $Y$, since that would exhibit $Y$ as a direct summand of it.

If $r$ is even, pair the copies using the invertible off-diagonal blocks $h,h^*$ and zero diagonal blocks, which supplies the required isomorphism, including for the alternating variant. These operations give the entire algorithm and produce $Q$ explicitly. All the decomposition, pairing, isomorphism, linear-system and reconstruction calls use polynomial-size data.

**Descent from extensions of finite fields.** For a tuple defined over a finite field $k$, existence of a solution over any extension implies existence over $k$. The symmetrizer space commutes with extension of scalars by linearity. If its determinant polynomial is nonzero over some extension, it is formally nonzero and has a nonvanishing evaluation in the algebraic closure of $k$, hence in some finite extension $K$. Ordinary self-duality $M_K\cong (DM)_K$ implies $M\cong DM$. Restriction of scalars gives an isomorphism of $[K:k]$ copies of $M$ with $[K:k]$ copies of $DM$, and one cancels the multiplicities by Krull–Schmidt.

The only possible remaining obstruction over $k$ is an odd multiplicity of a self-dual type with no allowable individual isomorphism. The quotient to that type's residue matrices is a homomorphism of unital algebras after choosing an ordinary self-duality as above, and its scalar extension still sends units to units. The linear equations imposing alternating residue matrices hold after extension too. Over the commutative ring $(E/J)\otimes_k K$ an odd alternating matrix has determinant zero. Permutation terms with a fixed point vanish, terms paired by inversion of a permutation cancel in characteristic two, and an odd set has no fixed-point-free involution. This excludes an extended solution in the obstructed case and proves descent.

## 3. The adjoint algebra and an effective score

Assume the nondegenerate reduction of §1. Compute the unital $F$-algebra
$$
\mathcal A=\{Q^T\mid \exists P,\ P B_i=B_iQ\text{ for every }i\}
$$
by linear algebra. The left component $P$ is unique given $Q$ since the images span. Write $d=\dim_F\mathcal A$. For a candidate $Z\in L(B)$, compute the linear span $I_Z=ZL(B^T)\mathcal A$. Test its inclusion in $\mathcal A$. It is closed under right multiplication by $\mathcal A$.

Suppose a solution exists and write $B_i=EC_i$ with $C_i$ all symmetric, or all alternating in the alternating variant. Conjugating $P$ by $E$ shows that $\mathcal A$ is the same algebra for $C$ as for $B$. For $C$ the formula $(Q^T)^*=P$, where $PC_i=C_iQ$, defines an anti-involution. Transposing swaps the two components and multiplication of adjoints reverses after passage to the transposed $Q$. Put $H=L(C)$. This is a subspace of the fixed points of $*$. It contains $1$ and all $x+x^*$ with $x\in\mathcal A$. Indeed $(x+x^*)C_i=x C_i+(x C_i)^T$ is alternating, so this holds in both variants. Congruence yields $L(B)=HE^{-1}$ and $L(B^T)=EH$. Consequently $I_Z=(ZE)\mathcal A$ on yes-instances. In particular, rejecting when a computed $I_Z$ is outside $\mathcal A$ is sound.

For a right ideal $I\subseteq\mathcal A$, start at $e=0$ and maintain an idempotent $e\in I$. If the algebra $(1-e)I$ contains a non-nilpotent element, find an idempotent $f$ in $(1-e)I$. Then $ef=0$ and $g=f(1-e)$ is a nonzero idempotent with $eg=ge=0$. Replace $e$ by $e+g$, increasing $\dim e\mathcal A$.

The idempotent search uses elementary linear algebra. Any basis of a non-nilpotent finite-dimensional algebra contains a non-nilpotent element. After scalar extension to an algebraic closure, a non-nilpotent algebra has a full matrix algebra quotient, and a spanning collection of nilpotent matrices would have zero trace on its entire span. For a nonunital algebra, unitize and project the ideal onto a nonzero simple factor of the semisimple quotient. Test basis elements by powering to exponent $d$. From a non-nilpotent $x$ with minimal polynomial $t^v q(t)$, $q(0)\ne0$, compute an idempotent polynomial congruent to zero modulo $t^{\max(v,1)}$ and one modulo $q(t)$, so it has zero constant term and evaluates to a nonzero idempotent of the ideal.

The iteration stops in at most $d$ increases. The residual right ideal $(1-e)I$ then lies in the Jacobson radical $J$ of $\mathcal A$: for each of its elements $x$, every $xa$ is nilpotent, hence $1-xa$ is invertible for all $a\in\mathcal A$. Thus $e\mathcal A$ and $I$ have equal images in $\mathcal A/J$. Use $\dim_F e\mathcal A$ as the score. If an ideal's image in each simple factor of $\mathcal A/J$ increases or stays the same in multiplicity of its simple right module, and increases in at least one factor, its score increases. The indecomposable projective summands of $e\mathcal A$ have multiplicities equal to those of their simple tops in its semisimple image, and each such summand has positive dimension. This is the projective-right-ideal comparison of Ivanyos–Qiao §4.2, with the orthogonal idempotent update made explicit here. [Ivanyos–Qiao, §4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.03495#page=28)

## 4. Rank augmentation in two directions

Suppose a solution exists. Let $a=ZE\in H$ be noninvertible and $(b_i)$ be the basis of $H$ given by $b_i=Z_iE$ for our computed basis $(Z_i)$ of $L(B)$. We prove that a generic perturbation $a+s b_i+t b_j$ increases the rank in a deficient simple component of $\mathcal A/J$, for some $i,j$. For every other component its generic rank is at least the rank at $s=t=0$. Measure rank after splitting each simple algebra over an algebraic closure of its center, where it is matrix rank. It is proportional, with a positive factor depending only on the simple algebra, to the multiplicity of the simple right module in the image right ideal.

The involution descends to the semisimple quotient. When it interchanges simple factors, projection of the trace space $\{x+x^*\}$ onto either factor is surjective by taking $x$ supported on that factor. When it preserves a simple factor and its restriction to the center $K$ is nontrivial, extend that factor over the fixed field of $K$ to an algebraic closure. The quadratic extension of the fixed field is separable and the factor splits into two matrix algebras interchanged by the involution. The trace-space argument applies again. The original basis images span the projected trace space over the splitting field, because the trace is linear over the fixed field and extends by linearity. In these cases one basis direction raises the rank in a deficient component. In bases putting the current matrix in block form with a nonsingular leading block and zeros elsewhere, a basis direction with a nonzero bottom-block entry gives a larger minor with nonzero linear term.

For a preserved simple factor on whose center the involution is trivial, extend it over its center to an algebraic closure. Its involution is $x^*=G^{-1}x^TG$ for a nonsingular symmetric matrix $G$. An automorphism of the matrix algebra is inner, so composition with transpose has this form. Squaring the involution implies $G^T=\lambda G$ with $\lambda^2=1$, hence $\lambda=1$. Multiplication by $G$ identifies the span of the basis images with a subspace of symmetric matrices containing $G$ and all alternating matrices. The latter are exactly the images $Gx+(Gx)^T=G(x+x^*)$ of the extended trace space. Its extension is justified by linearity over the center.

To increase the rank of a singular matrix $S$ in this subspace, put it in the shape $\operatorname{diag}(C,0)$ by congruence. Here $C$ is the nonsingular restriction to a complement of the radical. Let $r$ be the full size.

1. If the radical has dimension at least two, the alternating subspace has a nonzero restriction to it. Some basis direction therefore has a nonzero bottom-block entry, giving a larger minor with nonzero linear term.
2. Suppose the radical has dimension one. A direction with nonzero bottom diagonal entry gives a nonzero linear term of the determinant. If all directions have zero bottom diagonal, write them as $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}D&u\\u^T&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. When $C$ is nonalternating, so is $C^{-1}$, which is congruent to $C$. The alternating directions realize every vector $u$. The map $u\mapsto u^T C^{-1}u$ is additive and scales by squares, so it is nonzero for some basis direction. The block determinant identity gives a nonzero quadratic coefficient $\det(C)u^T C^{-1}u$ in that direction.
3. Otherwise $C$ is alternating and its size is even, so $r$ is odd and $G$ is nonalternating. If all bottom diagonals are zero, some basis direction has a nonalternating upper block. Add this direction with indeterminate $s$. The upper block stays nonsingular and becomes nonalternating over the rational function field in $s$. If the full matrix remains singular, its radical still has dimension one. Apply the preceding argument using its current radical and the nonsingular nonalternating complement to find a second basis direction and indeterminate $t$. In size one the assumption that every bottom diagonal vanishes contradicts the presence of $G$.

This proves the rank increase. The algorithm can enumerate the entire polynomial-size set of pairs, without identifying the simple factors.

For a pair witnessing an increase, take one minor witnessing the increased generic rank in the chosen component and one minor witnessing at least the old rank in every other component. For the split factors from a nontrivial center involution, either embedding of the center into an algebraic closure of $F$ measures the rank of elements specialized at $s,t\in F$. View all the witness polynomials over an algebraic closure of $F$. Their product is nonzero with total degree at most the sum of the sizes of the chosen split matrix algebras, which is at most $d$. Over any $\Lambda\subseteq F$ of size greater than $d$, some pair in $\Lambda^2$ makes the product nonzero by the univariate root bound in each variable. Thus an enumerated perturbation increases the score of §3.

## 5. Deterministic algorithm over any field

In the nondegenerate case with a set $\Lambda$ of $d+1$ distinct scalars, start at $Z=0$ and test it for invertibility. At each singular iterate enumerate the candidates $Z+sZ_i+tZ_j$ over all basis pairs and all $s,t\in\Lambda$. Allow a zero basis direction for a one-direction candidate. Continue with any strict increase in the score of §3. Reject if an ideal computed for the test fails the inclusion check or if all candidates fail to increase the score. The score is an integer bounded by $d$, and §§3–4 ensure a strict increase until an invertible $Z$ is found on a yes-instance. Check the invertibility and membership of the resulting $Z$ directly before returning $(Z,I_n)$, transported to the original coordinates when needed.

Obtain the scalars uniformly using only field operations and equality. Start with the explicit list for the $\mathbb F_2$-span of $1$. Add input entries outside the list and close under products, adjoining an element $x$ by replacing the list $S$ with $S\cup(x+S)$. Keep this list only while its size is at most $d$. Each enlargement doubles the list. The number of membership and product tests is polynomial. If the list grows past $d$, take any $d+1$ elements from it. Otherwise the list is a finite subring of $F$ containing $1$ and the input entries, hence a field. Encode it by an explicit field representation and apply §2. A power basis can be found by checking the listed field elements until one spans the field with its powers. Descent proves that the result over the subfield decides the problem over $F$.

All steps take polynomially many field operations and equality tests. In particular the smaller-field branch uses only a field of cardinality at most $d$, with an explicit table of all its elements available from the list. For general finite input fields, §2 directly provides a uniform deterministic polynomial bit-time bound.
