# Isomorphism of quotients of sloped genus-two groups

**Theorem.** For odd $p$, there is a deterministic polynomial-bit-time isomorphism test for two groups promised to be quotients of centrally indecomposable sloped genus-two groups of exponent $p$. The covers and their quotient maps can differ and need not be supplied. The input is the commutator matrices over $\mathbb F_p$ and the group order in binary, or Schrock's generating-set input from which these data are obtained in polynomial time. This answers the sloped central-quotient isomorphism problem in Schrock's §4.5. The same procedure includes noncentral kernels. The proof is informal. [Schrock, §2.5.3](https://math.colorado.edu/~tysc3584/Tyler_Schrock-PhD_Thesis.pdf#page=49) · [Schrock, §4.5](https://math.colorado.edu/~tysc3584/Tyler_Schrock-PhD_Thesis.pdf#page=120)

## 1. A single primary sloped block

Write $K$ for the finite field of the cover. The sloped classification gives
$$
 R=K[t]/(f(t)^h),\qquad V=R^2,\qquad P=K^2,
$$
where $f$ is irreducible and $h\geq1$. The commutator on $V$ has components $\lambda(x_1y_2-x_2y_1)$ and $\lambda(t(x_1y_2-x_2y_1))$, with $\lambda:R\to K$ chosen so that $\lambda(uv)$ is a perfect $K$-pairing. Indeed, one can take the top coefficient in the usual polynomial representatives. The companion-matrix sequence for multiplication by $t$ on one coordinate and its dual on the other realizes the two alternating forms in the classification. The genus-two hypothesis ensures the two forms are linearly independent. [Brooksbank, Maglione and Wilson, Theorem 3.8 and Proposition 3.9](https://joshmaglione.com/papers/Genus2.pdf#page=12)

Work in Baer coordinates on $V\oplus P$, so that the product adds the coordinates and adds half the commutator to the last coordinate. For a normal subgroup $N$, put $C=N\cap P$ and let $W$ be its projection to $V$. In these coordinates $N$ is an $\mathbb F_p$-subspace since powers give scalar multiples and group products differ from sums by half of a commutator in $N$. Its graph modulo $C$ is removed by a central shear, obtained by extending its linear map $W\to P/C$ to a linear map $V\to P$. Normality gives $[W,V]\subseteq C$. The quotient is therefore the Baer group on $V/W$ and $P/C$, with the projected commutator. Its form space is parametrized by $S=C^\perp\subseteq K^2$ via the trace pairing. In particular the scalar form for $w=(a,b)\in S$ on the original $V$ is
$$
 \omega_w(x,y)=\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb F_p}
       \lambda\big((a+bt)(x_1y_2-x_2y_1)\big).
$$
These forms descend to $V/W$. They present the quotient group up to isomorphism by simultaneous congruence of the form space. For compact input giving forms on $G/Z(G)$, adjoin the zero directions of $Z(G)/G'$ to obtain the form space on $G/G'$. Their number is $\log_p|G|-\dim_{\mathbb F_p}(G')-\dim_{\mathbb F_p}(G/Z(G))$.

Split off the common radical of the input forms, remembering its dimension. Any complement has the same induced form space as the quotient by the common radical, and the removed space gives an elementary abelian direct factor in Baer coordinates. Two such form spaces are isomorphic precisely when the common radical dimensions agree and the reduced form spaces are isomorphic. Empty form spaces give elementary abelian groups, which can be compared by their orders. In the rest of the proof $X$ denotes the reduced domain.

Let $D=R/(f)$, identified with the canonical coefficient field inside $R$. This field consists of the roots of $x^{|D|}-x$ in $R$. Each residue class has a unique such root by Hensel lifting in the nilpotent ideal $(f)$, since the derivative of that polynomial is $-1$. The roots are closed under the field operations by the Frobenius identity. Write $t_0\in D$ for the residue representative of $t$. If $h>1$, then $t-t_0$ generates the maximal ideal, since $f(t)=(t-t_0)g(t)$ with $g(t)$ invertible by separability of $f$. Thus $R=D[t-t_0]\cong D[u]/(u^h)$. The same presentation for $h=1$ just means $R=D$.

The rank of $\omega_w$ is full precisely when $a+bt$ is a unit. This is because its radical is the annihilator of $a+bt$ on $R^2$, by perfection of the prime-field pairing $(u,v)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}\lambda(uv)$. That pairing is perfect because after finding a nonzero value over $K$ one can scale the second argument to make the trace nonzero. The nonunit weights belong to the $K$-linear subspace defined by $a+bt_0=0$. If $S$ contains a unit weight, at least one of its basis weights is a unit, and $W=0$ since it lies in the common radical. If every weight is a nonunit and $S\ne0$, then $t_0\in K$, the weights all have the form $b(t-t_0)$, and the common radical is the direct sum of two copies of $\operatorname{Ann}_R(t-t_0)$ before passage to $V/W$. In this case every nonzero form becomes nonsingular after removing the common radical, and the relative operators obtained by dividing by a chosen form are scalar multiplications. Either way a nonsingular reference form $\omega$ on $X$ can be found by scanning the supplied form basis.

## 2. Recovering the constant and linear coefficients

For each $\beta$ in the reduced form space compute $s\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb F_p}(X)$ by
$$
                \beta(x,y)=\omega(sx,y).
$$
These relative operators form a linear space $\mathcal S$ containing the identity. In the all-nonunit case they are scalar multiplications on $X$, as just shown. In the unit case they are multiplications by $(a+bt)/(c+dt)$ on $R^2$, where $c+dt$ is the weight of the reference form. All of them have a common expression
$$
 \frac{a+bt}{c+dt}
 =\frac{a+bt_0}{c+dt_0}
  +\left(b-d\frac{a+bt_0}{c+dt_0}\right)
       \frac{t-t_0}{c+dt}.
$$
When $h>1$, the last fraction is a parameter generating the maximal ideal. Thus every relative operator consists of a constant plus a $D$-scalar multiple of the same parameter.

Compute the unital $\mathbb F_p$-algebra $A$ generated by the relative operators, by repeatedly adjoining independent products of current basis elements inside the ambient matrix space. In the unit case it lies in the scalar multiplication operators of $R$. In the all-nonunit case it is a field. In either case, take a Frobenius power $Frob^k:x\mapsto x^{p^k}$ with $p^k\ge \max(1,\dim_{\mathbb F_p}X)$. Because $A$ is commutative this map is linear, and its image is precisely the field $F$ of constant parts in $A$. Indeed the power kills the nilpotent part and permutes the finite field of residues. This computes $F$ as a matrix subalgebra. Inverting the restriction of the same Frobenius power to $F$ recovers the constant part $a\in F$ of any $s\in A$ from $s^{p^k}$.

If every basis element of $\mathcal S$ equals its constant part, retain the coefficient subspace $S_F\subseteq F$ and use the field case below. Otherwise select one basis element $s_0$ with a nonzero nilpotent part, and set $\eta=s_0-a_0$. This is multiplication by a parameter generating the maximal ideal of $R$. Each $s\in\mathcal S$ has the unique form $s=a+b\eta$ with $a,b\in D$. The formula holds in these normalized coordinates because $\eta$ is a nonzero constant multiple of the common parameter in the previous display.

The coefficients $a,b$ can be recovered without constructing $D$ or any cover. Compute $Y=X/\eta X$ and $Y_1=\eta X/\eta^2X$. The map $Y\to Y_1$ induced by multiplication by $\eta$ is an isomorphism because $X=R^2$ in the nilpotent case. The operator $a$ on $Y$ is induced by the computed constant part. The operator $b$ on $Y$ comes from the map $Y\to Y_1$ induced by $s-a$, composed with the inverse of the map induced by $\eta$. These are the scalar multiplications by $a,b$ in cover coordinates. Let $L$ be their generated unital algebra acting on $Y$. It is a finite field inside $D$, because it consists of scalar multiplications. Generating it from the operators for a basis of $\mathcal S$ gives its structure constants and a coefficient subspace $S_L\subseteq L^2$, which contains $(1,0)$ and a vector $(a_0,1)$. Also retain $h$, the index of nilpotence of $\eta$, and $d_0=\dim_{\mathbb F_p}Y=2[D\colon\mathbb F_p]$. All these operations use linear algebra and repeated squaring.

## 3. The nilpotent case

For two normalized inputs with nonzero nilpotent parts in their relative operator spaces, compare the primes, reduced domain dimensions, common radical dimensions, form-space dimensions, nilpotence indices $h$, and dimensions $d_0$. Subject to these matching, the criterion is that there be a field isomorphism $\sigma:L\to L'$ and a matrix over $L'$
$$
 M=\begin{pmatrix}c_0&0\\ c_1&d_1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad c_0d_1\ne0,\qquad M\,\sigma(S_L)=S_{L'}'.
$$

For necessity, let $g:X\to X'$ be an isomorphism of form spaces, and write an image form by its relative operator $s'$. The image of the source marked form has relative operator $c=c_0+c_1\eta'$, with $c_0\ne0$ since the form is nonsingular. The operator relation is $gsg^{-1}=c^{-1}s'$. The algebras generated by $c^{-1}\mathcal S'$ and by $\mathcal S'$ coincide. Indeed $c\in\mathcal S'$, $1\in\mathcal S'$, and the algebra generated by $c^{-1}\mathcal S'$ contains $c^{-1}$ and hence $c$ by finiteness. Conjugation therefore identifies the two algebras and maps their constant fields onto each other by an isomorphism $\sigma_0$.

The image of $\eta=s_0-a_0$ is $c^{-1}(s_0'-c\sigma_0(a_0))$. It is nilpotent, so the numerator has zero constant part, making the numerator $d_1\eta'$ with $d_1\in L'$. It is nonzero, so $d_1\ne0$. Consequently $g$ identifies both the first and second image spaces of $\eta$ with those of $\eta'$. In particular $h$ and $d_0$ match. For any $s=a+b\eta$, conjugation sends $s-a$ to $c^{-1}(s'-c\sigma_0(a))$, whose numerator again has zero constant part. Write $s'=a'+b'\eta'$. The constants satisfy $a'=c_0\sigma_0(a)$. On the induced map between $Y=X/\eta X$ and $Y'=X'/\eta'X'$, the coefficient operator $b$ conjugates to multiplication by $(b'-c_1\sigma_0(a))/d_1$. This follows by dividing the induced map to the first image layer by the induced map of $\eta$. Both use the same divisor $c$, which cancels. Thus the induced conjugation on $Y$ maps all the generators of $L$ to the operators in $L'$ and extends $\sigma_0$. Applying the same inclusion argument to the inverse isomorphism shows that the field degrees agree, so this is an isomorphism $\sigma:L\to L'$. The displayed coefficient transformation follows. A case whose algebra has a nonzero nilpotent part cannot be isomorphic to a field case, because the generated algebra was invariant under changing the mark as above.

For sufficiency, use abstract cover coordinates in the proof. The matching dimensions $d_0$ give ambient fields $D,D'$ of the same cardinality. The given $\sigma:L\to L'$ extends to an isomorphism $D\to D'$, since any restriction on the unique subfield of that cardinality can be adjusted by a suitable Frobenius power. Extend this to a ring isomorphism $\rho:R\to R'$ by
$$
             \rho(\eta)=\frac{d_1\eta'}{c_0+c_1\eta'}.
$$
This is an isomorphism because the coefficient of $\eta'$ is nonzero and the two truncated polynomial rings have the same nilpotence index. It follows that $c\rho(s)$ has exactly the target coefficients given by $M\sigma(a,b)$.

In these coordinates the marked forms are $\Lambda(x_1y_2-x_2y_1)$ and $\Lambda'(x_1'y_2'-x_2'y_1')$, with perfect multiplication pairings for the prime-field functionals $\Lambda,\Lambda'$. The functional $\Lambda\circ\rho^{-1}$ is therefore uniquely $z\mapsto\Lambda'(vz)$ for an element $v\in R'$. This $v$ is a unit. Otherwise its nonzero annihilator would make the induced multiplication pairing degenerate. Now set $g(x_1,x_2)=((v/c)\rho(x_1),\rho(x_2))$. For every $s$, the form with target relative operator $c\rho(s)$ pulls back to the form with source relative operator $s$. This gives the desired isomorphism of form spaces.

## 4. The field case and the algorithm

When all relative operators belong to the constant field $F$, the analogue of the criterion is a field isomorphism $\sigma:F\to F'$ and a nonzero $c\in F'$ with $c\sigma(S_F)=S_{F'}'$, along with equality of the primes and the common radical, reduced domain and form-space dimensions. Necessity follows by the same conjugation argument. For sufficiency, the marked form is the prime-field trace of a nondegenerate $F$-valued alternating form $B$ on $X$. Explicitly define $B$ by
$$
 \operatorname{Tr}_{F/\mathbb F_p}(a B(x,y))=\omega(ax,y)\qquad(a\in F).
$$
Existence and uniqueness follow from the perfect trace pairing. The scalar operators are self-adjoint for $\omega$, because the relative operators are self-adjoint and generate a commutative algebra. This gives $F$-bilinearity in both arguments and alternation, using odd characteristic. Nondegeneracy follows from that of $\omega$. Symplectic bases for $B,B'$ give a $\sigma$-semilinear map $g$ with $B'(gx,gy)=c^{-1}\sigma(B(x,y))$, using the equal dimensions of $X,X'$ over the respective fields. Thus the fields, matrices, and forms in the two cases give isomorphism of the reduced form spaces and extend over the common radicals.

For the algorithm construct one isomorphism of the matching finite coefficient fields from their structure constants using Lenstra's deterministic polynomial-time algorithm. The other isomorphisms are obtained by composing it with the powers of Frobenius. For each $\sigma$, solve the linear equations in the entries of $M$ which impose $M\sigma(S_L)\subseteq S_{L'}'$ and its triangular shape. The scalar field case uses the corresponding linear equations in $c$ alone. The comparison of form-space dimensions makes inclusion by an invertible map equivalent to equality. For a basis $M_1,\ldots,M_\ell$ of the solution space, some invertible matrix exists if and only if some $M_i$ or some $M_i+M_j$, $i<j$, has nonzero determinant. Indeed all coefficients of the determinant as a homogeneous quadratic in the basis coordinates are tested this way. For the scalar case some nonzero solution suffices. [Lenstra, *Finding Isomorphisms Between Finite Fields*](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~lenstrahw/PUBLICATIONS/1991b/art.pdf)

The required matrix sizes are polynomial in the original input size. The algebra computations require at most the square of the matrix dimension many independent additions. Bounds on the powers for Frobenius and nilpotence tests are polynomial in bit length. The number of Frobenius maps is the degree of the coefficient field over $\mathbb F_p$, bounded by the input domain dimension. The determinant test uses quadratically many candidates in the dimension of a linear solution space. No loop enumerates the elements of the cover field, the group, or its form space. This proves the compact-input bound.

## 5. Comparison with a flat cover

Combining this result with the polynomial-time flat-block algorithm covers arbitrary input pairs whose covers are centrally indecomposable of genus two, even when one cover is flat and the other is sloped. In a nonzero family of scalar forms, first remove its common radical and remember the abelian dimension removed, as in Section 1. A flat quotient whose weights span the full cover plane has no nonsingular form on the reduced domain. Indeed the radical of each nonzero form is one full extension-field coordinate line, and two independent weights have disjoint radicals, so the common radical is zero. Therefore it cannot be isomorphic to a sloped quotient, which always has a nonsingular reference form on its reduced domain. A flat quotient whose nonzero weights lie on one extension-field line has only relative scalar multiplications after removing the common radical. It is compared with a sloped quotient by the common field criterion in Section 4. Thus the case split uses the raw matrices and does not require supplied cover types. [Source classification](https://joshmaglione.com/papers/Genus2.pdf#page=12)

## 6. Scope and prior work

Schrock distinguishes centrally indecomposable flat and sloped covers, gives compact polynomial-time isomorphism for quotients by noncentral subgroups in Theorem E, and leaves sloped central quotients open in §4.5. The proof above handles the latter and includes the noncentral case by the same reductions. The genus-one algorithms of Lewis and Wilson treat quotients of Heisenberg groups over fields, and the genus-two algorithm of Brooksbank, Maglione and Wilson treats the original genus-two inputs and supplies the primary-block normal form used here. Lewis and Maglione also used quotients of Heisenberg groups over local rings to construct and enumerate families of genus-two groups. In the present theorem the normalized relative operators of a quotient can have a nonreduced generated algebra. The comparison recovers coefficients on the first image quotient and allows the quotient itself to have genus greater than two. The separate question in §4.5 on arbitrary centrally decomposable genus-two covers is outside this theorem. A targeted priority search found the cited results and did not locate an earlier compact isomorphism algorithm for arbitrary central quotients of sloped blocks.
