# Sparse determinant equivalence in the characteristic dividing the determinant size

**Theorem.** Fix a finite field \(k\). A sparse polynomial presented by its nonzero monomials, with binary exponents, can be tested deterministically in polynomial bit time for formal polynomial equality

\[
f(x)=\det_n(Lx+B)
\]

with a surjective \(k\)-linear map \(L:k^N\to M_n(k)\) and \(B\in M_n(k)\). The algorithm outputs \(L,B\) on acceptance. The linear version has \(B=0\). Here \(n\) can be supplied or inferred from the degree. The theorem covers arbitrary \(N\), characteristic two, and characteristic dividing \(n\). For \(n\ge3\) and an explicitly presented finite field \(\mathbb F_q\) of characteristic \(p\), the algorithm is uniform in time polynomial in the encoding length, \(\log q\), and \(p\).

This combines the linear centroid computation in [Sparse determinant equivalence](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/SparseDeterminantEquivalence/PROOF.md) with coordinate recovery from split algebras as in [Tensor isomorphism composition](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/TensorIsomorphismComposition/Results.md), and exact coefficient extraction akin to the [Mathlib-only formula factorization](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/FormulaFactorization.lean). The rank-one cone is recovered before computing the Lie algebra, and the final test detects the extra Frobenius powers invisible to infinitesimal invariants. The [Garg–Gupta–Kayal–Saha algorithm](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ICALP.2019.62) gives a randomized black-box theorem under size and characteristic restrictions. Its Remark 29 explicitly exhibits the failure of infinitesimal recognition when the characteristic divides the determinant size. The theorem here applies to sparse white-box inputs. It extends the setting of [Grochow’s sparse determinant question](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~billey/classes/lie/bulletins/grochow.2012.pdf#page=10), whose main field is \(\mathbb C\).

## 1. The quadratic space computed from the top form

Reject zero or constant inputs. Write \(P=f_n\) for the top homogeneous part. A positive instance has degree exactly \(n\) by surjectivity of \(L\). If the sparse list of \(P\) has \(s\) terms, it mentions at most \(ns\) distinct variables. For a determinant under a surjective map the space of its ordinary first derivatives has dimension \(n^2\), because the \(n^2\) cofactors are linearly independent over any field. Indeed their nonzero monomial supports distinguish the deleted row and column. It follows that \(n\le s\) on every positive instance. Test this necessary bound before any computation polynomial in the numerical degree. Work with the variables that occur in the input. If ambient unused variable names are encoded implicitly, the output map has zero coefficient at those names.

Compute the kernel \(K\) of the ordinary directional derivative map \(v\mapsto D_vP\) by equating sparse coefficients. Reject unless its codimension is \(n^2\), and choose a complement \(W\) spanned by a subset of the input coordinate vectors. This choice follows by choosing independent columns of the derivative map. On a positive instance \(K=\ker L\), so \(P|_W\) is a determinant under an invertible linear map. Restricting to \(W\) sets the other input variables equal to zero. In arbitrary characteristic the derivative kernel alone is insufficient to show independence of \(K\), so it is checked later.

For a polynomial \(H\), write \(\partial^{[\alpha]}H(x)=[y^\alpha]H(x+y)\). Let \(Q\) be the linear span of the homogeneous quadrics \(\partial^{[\alpha]}(P|_W)\) for \(|\alpha|=n-2\). To construct it from a monomial \(c x^\gamma\), enumerate quadratic residual indices \(|\beta|=2\) with \(\beta\le\gamma\), and add \(c\binom{\gamma}{\beta}x^\beta\) to the column for \(\alpha=\gamma-\beta\). There are only \(O((\dim W)^2)\) possible residual indices per input monomial. Covariance of the span under invertible linear substitutions follows by applying the same substitution to both \(x\) and \(y\) in \(H(x+y)\). For \(H=\det_n\) the space is precisely the span of all \(2\times2\) minors. The derivative with respect to a squarefree product of \(n-2\) entries in different rows and columns is a complementary minor up to sign. Other derivatives of that order vanish. Every complementary row and column pair is attained.

## 2. Recovering matrix coordinates

For \(A\in\operatorname{End}(W)\) let \(\mathcal D_A H(x)=\sum_i(Ax)_i\partial_iH(x)\). Compute the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak g=\{A\mid\mathcal D_A Q\subseteq Q\}\) by linear equations. Consider the calculation when \(Q\) is the minor space on \(W=U\otimes V\) with both factors of dimension \(n\). In this case

\[
\mathfrak g=\{A\otimes I+I\otimes C:A\in\operatorname{End}(U),\ C\in\operatorname{End}(V)\}.
\]

For inclusion from right to left, expand the minors after an infinitesimal left or right multiplication. Each derivative is a linear combination of minors, either by the bilinearity in rows and columns or by the induced action on the two exterior squares. For the other inclusion, \(\mathcal D_D Q\subseteq Q\) implies \(D(u\otimes v)\in U\otimes v+u\otimes V\) for each nonzero pure tensor. This is because the derivatives of the minors cut out exactly the indicated tangent space. At \(e_1\otimes f_1\), those derivatives say exactly that the lower-right block is zero. Other pure tensors reduce to that point by changes of bases in both factors.

To extract the matrix coefficients of \(D\), first test \(e_i\otimes f_j\). Only one index is allowed to change in any nonzero output coefficient. Testing \((e_i+e_h)\otimes f_j\) shows that every coefficient changing \(j\) to \(\ell\ne j\) is independent of \(i\), since the component in second coordinate \(\ell\) must be proportional to \(e_i+e_h\). Testing \(e_i\otimes(f_j+f_\ell)\) gives the analogous independence in the other factor. Subtract these two off-diagonal operators. The remaining diagonal coefficients \(d_{ij}\) satisfy \(d_{ij}-d_{i\ell}-d_{hj}+d_{h\ell}=0\) by testing \((e_i+e_h)\otimes(f_j+f_\ell)\). Thus \(d_{ij}=a_i+c_j\), as claimed. These tests apply over every field.

Compute \(\mathfrak h=[\mathfrak g,\mathfrak g]\) and its center by linear algebra. In the minor case \(\mathfrak h\) is the sum of the two commuting traceless left and right Lie algebras, since \([\mathfrak{gl}_n,\mathfrak{gl}_n]=\mathfrak{sl}_n\). An element of the sum commutes with one traceless factor exactly when its component in that factor is scalar, because the commutant of the elementary off-diagonal matrices consists of scalars. Accordingly \(\mathfrak h/Z(\mathfrak h)\) is the direct sum of two copies of \(\mathfrak{sl}_n/Z(\mathfrak{sl}_n)\), also when \(p\mid n\).

For \(n\ge3\) the two factors are absolutely simple. If a Lie ideal in \(\mathfrak{sl}_n\) contains a matrix \(A\) whose off-diagonal entry \(a_{ki}\ne0\), choose \(j\) distinct from \(i,k\). The bracket

\[
[E_{ik},[E_{ij},[E_{jk},A]]]=-a_{ki}E_{ik}
\]

then finds an off-diagonal unit in the ideal, over every characteristic. If instead \(A\) is diagonal and nonscalar, one commutator with an elementary unit produces an off-diagonal unit. A single such unit generates all of \(\mathfrak{sl}_n\) as Lie ideal by repeated elementary commutators. Indeed, an initial \(E_{ik}\) produces \(E_{i\ell}\) and \(E_{\ell k}\) for \(\ell\ne i,k\) by bracketing with \(E_{k\ell}\) and \(E_{\ell i}\), then produces \(E_{\ell i}\) from \(E_{\ell k}\) and \(E_{ki}\), and \(E_{ki}\) from \(E_{k\ell}\) and \(E_{\ell i}\). These give every column and row and the diagonal differences. The argument works after arbitrary field extension.

The centroid of \(\mathfrak h/Z(\mathfrak h)\) is therefore \(k^2\) in the minor case. Each simple factor is perfect so no centroid map crosses between them. The centroid on each factor is scalar after algebraic closure by Schur’s lemma, and the computation descends by linear equations. Compute the centroid using \(J[a,b]=[Ja,b]=[a,Jb]\). Require dimension two and split its two eigenspaces by factoring the quadratic minimal polynomial of an element independent of the identity, requiring distinct roots in \(k\). On each candidate lift of an eigenspace to \(\mathfrak h\), generate its unital associative algebra \(\mathcal A\) in \(\operatorname{End}(W)\) by iterating products with the generators and keeping a linear basis. In the minor case these are the two full matrix algebras of left and right actions. Scalar identity directions arising from the lift do not affect the generated algebras. Test \(\mathcal A\cong M_n(k)\) and compute matrix units \(F_{ij}\) on each successful candidate. For this step use the finite-algebra structure and lifting algorithms in [Ivanyos–Qiao, Theorem 18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.03495), which give an explicit quotient algebra and deterministic preimages in time polynomial in the representation dimension, \(\log q\), and \(p\). Require zero radical and one simple component \(M_n(k)\) with center \(k\). Quadratic splitting in the centroid admits the same bound, for example by deterministic finite-field polynomial factorization in time polynomial in \(p\) and \(\log q\).

Choose a \(k\)-basis \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) in \(F_{11}W\). The vectors \(F_{i1}w_j\) give a basis of \(W\) in which \(\mathcal A\) acts by ordinary left multiplication on the coordinate matrix \(Y\). The matrix-unit identities prove this by decomposing \(W=\bigoplus_i F_{ii}W\). Extend the resulting coordinate map \(Y=L_0x\) by zero on \(K\). On a positive instance this new matrix coordinate map differs from the unknown one by invertible left and right factors and possibly transposition. To see this, the full algebra on one matrix factor has been recovered. Changing its matrix units conjugates the factor by an invertible matrix: choosing a vector in the first diagonal matrix-unit image and translating with the units constructs the conjugation. The independent freedom in the choice of \(w_j\) is a basis change of the other factor. The determinant in the new coordinates therefore differs only by a nonzero scalar. In particular, a genuine positive instance passes either valid choice of side.

## 3. Exact recognition of an affine determinant in these coordinates

For each split-algebra candidate, first test \(D_bP=f_{n-1}\) for some \(b\in W\), by equating sparse coefficients. Reject that candidate on failure. On a positive affine instance the solution exists with \(L_0b\) equal to its translation in the recovered matrix coordinates. The solution is unique because \(W\cap K=0\). For the linear problem additionally require \(b=0\).

Test that every \(D_v f=0\) for \(v\) in a basis of \(K\). Also test the trace-zero vector fields \(\mathcal D_{A,b}f=\sum_i (A(x+b))_i\partial_i f=0\) for a basis of the left \(\mathfrak{sl}_n\) in the recovered coordinates, extended by zero on \(K\). These tests are sparse because each differential operator has affine-linear coefficients. They are necessary for a positive instance.

If these tests pass, use coordinates \(Y=L_0(x+b)\) and any linear coordinates \(z\) along \(K\), for analysis only. In these coordinates a polynomial \(H(Y)\) of degree at most \(n\) annihilated by every left trace-zero operator is of the form \(c\det(Y)+G(Y^p)\). To prove the assertion, the diagonal trace-zero operators require row degrees \(r_i\) of every nonzero row-multihomogeneous component to satisfy \(r_i\equiv r_j\pmod p\). Row replacement derivations preserve the equations componentwise because a fixed operator shifts multidegrees by a fixed vector. If their common residue is positive, the bound on the total degree forces all row degrees to be one. Vanishing of the off-diagonal derivatives then says that replacing one row by another makes this row-multilinear form vanish. Thus the form is alternating and a scalar multiple of determinant, also in characteristic two. If all row degrees are divisible by \(p\), its gradient in row \(i\) pairs to zero with every other row by the off-diagonal derivatives, and with row \(i\) by the Euler identity in that row. The rows of the generic matrix form a basis over the rational function field. All the partial derivatives therefore vanish, so the component is in \(k[Y^p]\).

Apply this argument to each coefficient in the inactive variables \(z\). A determinant term already uses degree \(n\) and therefore its coefficient is a scalar independent of \(z\). The equations \(D_v f=0\) along \(K\) imply that every exponent in \(z\) is also divisible by \(p\) in the remainder. The subring of \(p\)-powers is invariant under affine coordinate changes over this perfect field. It follows in the original variables that

\[
 f(x)=c\det(L_0(x+b))+R(x),\qquad R\in k[x_1^p,\ldots,x_N^p].
\]

Reject if all first derivatives of \(P\) vanish. On the remaining candidates \(c\ne0\), because the top part of a Frobenius polynomial still has all first derivatives zero. The computation of this structural identity required no expansion in the recovered matrix basis.

## 4. Removing Frobenius terms in polynomial bit time

Choose as directional test vectors the recovered coordinate vectors of the \(n\times n\) matrix entries, together with the basis of \(K\). These form a basis of the input space. The affine determinant has degree at most one as a polynomial in \(t\) after any substitution \(x\mapsto x+tv\) for such a vector \(v\), because the first kind changes one matrix entry and the second kind changes none.

Suppose inductively \(R\in k[x_1^M,\ldots,x_N^M]\) for \(M=p^e\) and \(e\ge1\). For each basis vector \(v\), test the polynomial \([t^M]f(x+tv)\) for zero. It equals \([t^M]R(x+tv)\) and has all (x)-exponents divisible by \(M\) by Frobenius. Thus it is enough to compute the coefficients with output exponents divisible by \(M\). For an input monomial with exponent vector \(\alpha\), a contribution removes \(\beta\le\alpha\) with \(|\beta|=M\) and \(\beta\equiv\alpha\pmod M\) coordinatewise. Write \(r_i=\alpha_i\bmod M\). If \(\sum_i r_i=M\), the only possibility is \(\beta=r\). If \(r=0\), the possibilities are \(\beta=M e_i\) for coordinates with \(\alpha_i\ge M\). Otherwise there are no contributions. Each valid choice contributes its input coefficient times \(\binom{\alpha}{\beta}v^\beta\) to exponent \(\alpha-\beta\). This computes the exact projected derivative in polynomially many sparse operations.

If all tests at stage \(M\) vanish, write \(R=S(x)^M\) using the field’s perfection. Then \([t^M]R(x+tv)=(D_v S(x))^M\), so all ordinary directional derivatives of \(S\) vanish. Hence \(R\in k[x_1^{pM},\ldots,x_N^{pM}]\). If any test fails, the candidate cannot be a determinant with the recovered shift. Repeat for the powers \(p^e\le n\). Since \(\deg R\le n\), the remainder is then constant. Check \(f(-b)=0\) to eliminate the constant. Now extract (c) by evaluating at \(x=x_I-b\) with \(L_0 x_I=I_n\). Scaling the first row of the affine matrix map by \(c\) provides the required witness.

The cases \(n=1,2\) complete the algorithm over a fixed field. For \(n=1\) test a nonconstant affine or linear polynomial directly. For \(n=2\), compute the same derivative kernel and four-dimensional complement. There are only a field-dependent constant number of invertible linear maps on that complement and translations in four variables. For each, extend the linear map by zero on \(K\) and check the formal equality by expanding a (2\times2) determinant into its degree-two coefficients. A positive instance necessarily has derivative kernel \(\ker L\), so this search is complete. All higher-dimensional stages use polynomial-sized linear systems and bounded exponent arithmetic, and their costs are polynomial in the encoding length and in the stated finite-field parameters.

## 5. Constructive black-box recognition with extension evaluations

**Corollary.** Fix a finite field \(k\) and assume black-box access to the formal polynomial \(P\in k[x_1,\ldots,x_{n^2}]\), homogeneous of degree \(n\), with evaluation available over explicitly constructed extensions of \(k\). There is a randomized polynomial-time algorithm to decide linear determinant equivalence over \(k\) and return an invertible transformation over \(k\) on yes-instances, with error probability at most \(1/4\). There is a deterministic polynomial-time Turing reduction to black-box polynomial identity testing in this extension-evaluation model. For the randomized algorithm and \(n\ge3\), the base field may also be explicitly presented and variable, with time polynomial in \(n\) and \(\log |k|\). The oracle for a polynomial function restricted to the base field when it has few elements gives a different input model, since equality of polynomial functions need not imply formal equality.

Put \(N=n^2\) and \(d=N(N+1)/2\). Choose an extension \(E/k\) with \(|E|>16nd\), taking \(E=k\) if it is already that large. For fixed \(k\) this takes degree \(O(\log n)\) and construction by enumerating monic polynomials and testing irreducibility in polynomial time, since the smallest adequate size is \(O_k(nd)\). For a variable base field when the extension is needed, \(|k|\le16nd\), so the same enumeration of an extension of size at most \(|k|(16nd+1)\) is polynomial in \(n\) and \(\log|k|\). For each \(v\in E^N\), the quadratic form \(q_v(x)=[t^{n-2}]P(x+tv)\) can be evaluated by univariate interpolation of degree at most \(n\). Its coefficients are recovered by evaluating at the basis vectors and their pairwise sums. In detail, the \(x_i^2\) coefficient is \(q_v(e_i)\) and the \(x_ix_j\) coefficient is \(q_v(e_i+e_j)-q_v(e_i)-q_v(e_j)\) for \(i<j\), in every characteristic. Thus a single sample is a vector in \(E^d\) computed using polynomially many oracle queries.

The \(q_v\) span \(Q\otimes_k E\), where \(Q\) is the quadratic space in Section 1. In fact a linear functional on quadrics pairs to zero with every \(q_v\) precisely when it annihilates the coefficients of the polynomial map \(v\mapsto q_v\). Its scalar pairing has degree at most \(n-2<|E|\), so a nonzero pairing cannot vanish at every \(v\). Sample \(d\) independent uniform vectors. If the sampled span is still a proper subspace of \(Q\otimes_k E\), a separating functional gives a nonzero polynomial pairing of degree at most \(n-2\). By Schwartz–Zippel the next sample fails to increase rank with conditional probability at most \((n-2)/|E|\). A union bound shows that the samples recover the full span with probability at least \(15/16\). Its reduced row echelon form descends to \(k\) since the true space is defined over \(k\). Reject if the computed row echelon form has entries outside \(k\). Run Section 2 over \(k\) on the resulting space. For a variable base field, use the randomized polynomial-time finite-algebra structure algorithm in [Ivanyos–Qiao, Theorem 18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.03495) with amplification, and randomized quadratic factorization as needed. Check the returned matrix-unit relations explicitly. For \(n=1,2\) and fixed \(k\), the constant-size exhaustive recognition can interpolate all coefficients from extension evaluations.

For each of the at most two candidates for \(n\ge3\), evaluate at the recovered identity matrix to choose \(c\) and reject that candidate if \(c=0\). Verify \(P(x)=c\det(L_0x)\) by evaluating the difference at random points of \(E^N\). Every false candidate has a nonzero difference of degree at most \(n\), so a single trial misses with probability at most \(n/|E|\). There are at most two candidates. The YES witness over \(k\) exists on the good sampling and algebra recovery events by Section 2. Thus the total error bound is at most \(1/4\), using the stated field size and amplification. This extends the witness-producing finite-field result of [Garg–Gupta–Kayal–Saha, Theorem 1](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ICALP.2019.62) to the excluded characteristics in the extension-query model. Their theorem assumes \(|k|\ge10n^4\) and \(\operatorname{char}(k)\nmid n(n-1)\) and uses only base-field queries. When \(|k|>16nd\), the present algorithm also uses only base-field queries.

For the deterministic reduction, replace random span recovery with PIT queries on the scalar polynomials \(v\mapsto\ell(q_v)\) for a basis of the annihilator of the current span, all over \(E\). Each such polynomial has the evaluation procedure just given and also allows extension evaluations. If all pairings are zero polynomials the current span is complete. Otherwise take a nonzero pairing and fix its variables one at a time, trying \(n+1\) distinct field elements and using PIT to retain a nonzero restricted polynomial at each step. A nonzero coefficient polynomial in the next variable has degree at most \(n-2\), so some trial remains nonzero. This yields a rank-increasing sample, and at most \(d\) such samples are needed. Finally apply PIT to the recovered determinant candidate. This construction for fixed \(k\) uses deterministic algebra splitting and polynomially many PIT queries.

The reverse reduction from PIT is also valid in this extension-evaluation model. Take a polynomial \(h\) of degree at most \(D\), where \(D\ge1\), and let \(H(Y,u)\) be its degree-\(D\) homogenization. Interpolation of \(h(tY)\) evaluates this homogenization even at \(u=0\). Inside an \(m\times m\) matrix \(X\), choose distinct coordinates for \(Y,u,w,v\), with \(m\ge D+3\), and define \(F(X)=\det_m X+w^{m-D-1}vH(Y,u)\). If \(h=0\), this is the determinant. Otherwise, at the rank-one base point with \(w=1\) and all other entries zero, the lowest nonzero Taylor form is the reducible \(vH\) of degree \(D+1<m-1\). A determinant at a rank-\(r\) singular point has an irreducible lowest form. Row and column changes give the base point \(\operatorname{diag}(I_r,0)\), where that form is the determinant of the generic bottom-right \((m-r)\)-square block. A generic square determinant is irreducible in every characteristic, since its zero set is the irreducible image of the matrix factorization variety and a corank-one point is smooth. Invertible coordinate changes preserve this local property. Thus \(F\) cannot be determinant-equivalent when \(h\ne0\), even over the algebraic closure. This is the [MathProofs reducible-initial-form reduction](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/SparseDeterminantEquivalence/PROOF.md) with its interpolation performed in an explicitly presented extension. Combined with the preceding algorithm, the decision problem is PIT-equivalent under deterministic polynomial-time Turing reductions in this model for each fixed finite field.

## 6. Uniform deterministic decision over explicitly presented finite fields

The sparse **decision** problem has a uniform polynomial bit-time algorithm in the input length and \(\log|k|\), even when the characteristic \(p\) varies. For \(3\le n\) and \(p\le n\), use Sections 1–4, since surviving inputs have \(n\) bounded by the listed term count. For \(p>n\), use the [large-characteristic test, Section 4](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/SparseDeterminantEquivalence/PROOF.md), with the following explicit redundancy check. If all directional derivatives of \(f\) along the computed kernel \(K\) vanish, the degree bound \(n<p\) implies that \(f\) is independent of \(K\) in any coordinates adapted to \(W\oplus K\). It is then enough to run that test on \(W\). For \(n=1\) the direct check is uniform.

For \(n=2\) in characteristic two, compute \(K\) and its four-dimensional complement as in Section 1. Compute the polar bilinear form \(P(x+y)-P(x)-P(y)\) of the top quadratic \(P\). A vector in \(K\) is in its radical and the restriction to \(W\) is nondegenerate, because this polar evaluation is the ordinary directional derivative. Test that \(P\) vanishes on a basis of \(K\) and that the linear part of \(f\) vanishes there. These tests are equivalent to independence of \(K\), since inside this radical the quadratic form adds without cross-terms. Find the unique shift on \(W\) using the degree-one part as in Section 3 and check the constant term by evaluation at the negative shift. It remains to recognize the direct sum of two hyperbolic planes, which is the determinant quadratic form in four variables in characteristic two.

Use symplectic elimination to split the polar form into two two-dimensional planes. On each plane the quadratic has form \(ax^2+xy+by^2\) in its symplectic basis. It has a nonzero isotropic vector exactly when \(ab\) lies in the image of \(z\mapsto z^2+z\), as follows by rescaling the equation for the coordinate ratio, with \(a=0\) or \(b=0\) immediate. This image test is linear algebra over the prime field. An isotropic plane has \(2|k|-1\) zeros and an anisotropic plane has one. All their nonzero level sets have equal size within a given plane by scaling and the bijectivity of squaring. It follows that the sum of the two planes has \(|k|^3+|k|^2-|k|\) zeros when their types agree, and \(|k|^3-|k|^2+|k|\) otherwise. Two isotropic planes are already hyperbolic. If both are anisotropic, choose one nonzero vector from each and scale to match their values, yielding a nonzero isotropic vector in their sum. Split off a hyperbolic plane using a vector paired to one with this isotropic vector and adjusting its quadratic value to zero. The zero count forces the complementary plane to be hyperbolic as well. The unequal-type case cannot be split by the zero count. This completes the uniform decision bound. In the \(p>n\) branch the result here asserts decision, with witness construction in the other branches as proved above.

The proofs are informal. Publication priority for the full small-characteristic algorithms has yet to be established.
