# Reverse reductions for two marked-permutation comparisons

All reductions are deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions. Marked positions in an input have their displayed order, and parity is the parity of the induced bijection relative to those orders. Parity is tested on the marked permutation. Ambient linear maps are unrestricted.

## Monomial-label tensor isomorphism reduces to subspace-arrangement isomorphism

Over F₃, let M be the following decision problem. Two systems of square or rectangular matrices are given by lists of slices, with labeled positions colored and a union of colors marked. A transporter consists of independent invertible maps on the matrix input and output spaces, and a color-preserving monomial map on the slice-label space. Its underlying permutation on the marked labels must be even. Let U be the analogous comparison of two colored, indexed lists of subspaces of a common ambient vector space, using one ambient invertible map and requiring an even permutation of the marked subspace indices. Lists may contain repeated equal subspaces and zero subspaces.

**Theorem.** M and U have the same many-one degree. The reduction from M to U preserves exactly the possible permutations of the original marked labels.

Write the source slices as A_i:X→Y. Use the ambient direct sum X⊕Y⊕Z, where Z has a distinct basis vector e_i for each label. Put X,Y,Z into the arrangement with three separate reserved colors of multiplicity one. For each i put the line ℓ_i=span(e_i) into the arrangement, inheriting the color and marking of the label in a palette disjoint from the three reserved colors. Put these tag-line positions in the same order as the original labels. Finally include the two subspaces

    S_i^+ = { (x,A_ix,te_i) : x∈X, t∈F₃ },
    S_i^− = { (x,−A_ix,te_i) : x∈X, t∈F₃ }.

All these auxiliary entries have one new common color and are unmarked. For a pair of input tensors, apply this construction independently to the two lists with the same palettes. Malformed input and mismatched ambient shapes can be sent to a fixed no-instance.

An arrangement transporter must act block diagonally on X,Y,Z because the three reserved entries are individually preserved. Let its matrix maps be P on X and Q on Y, and let the map on Z permute the tag lines by π. The intersection of either S_i^+ or S_i^− with Z is ℓ_i. Hence its image must be an auxiliary space indexed by the same target label π(i). Intersecting with X⊕Y shows that the graph of Q A_i P⁻¹ equals the graph of ±A'_{π(i)}. Projecting a graph to X recovers its unique linear map, so Q A_i P⁻¹=±A'_{π(i)}. These independent signs are exactly the possible nonzero monomial scalars over F₃. The tag permutation carries the prescribed colors, marks and parity.

Conversely, any monomial-label transporter provides P,Q and π satisfying these equalities. On Z take the permutation matrix for π. The block diagonal map takes the two auxiliary graphs at i to the two graphs at π(i), swapping them when the chosen scalar is −1. If a slice is zero, the identical pair of auxiliary spaces can be matched in either order as indexed copies. The zero-dimensional matrix spaces or empty label list cause no problem, since separate colors preserve the three reserved roles even if some denote the zero space. The only parity-checked entries are the tag lines in the original order. The construction writes 3+3n subspace entries with basis data in dimension dim X+dim Y+n, polynomial in the displayed input.

For the reverse U→M, take W=⊕_i S_i for a list (S_i) in V and use the slice W→V obtained by projection onto S_i followed by inclusion. The image recovers S_i and any ambient transporter extends on the summands according to its restrictions. Carry the original label positions, colors and mark. This is the earlier forward reduction. It follows that the adjacent rungs O∧U and O∧M of the determinant comparison chain are equal, simply keeping the independent ordinary TI comparison unchanged. Both constructions are explicit, use no isomorphism oracle, and apply to unrestricted raw inputs over F₃. The first construction also works over any fixed finite field by including all nonzero scalar multiples of each graph.

## Marked graphic-matroid isomorphism reduces to marked graph isomorphism

A graphic-matroid input is a graph with ordered, colored edges and marked edge positions given by a union of colors. Its isomorphisms are bijections on edges preserving independence in the cycle matroid. They must preserve colors and induce an even permutation of the marked edge positions. There may be parallel edges, loops, isolated vertices, or arbitrary 1- and 2-separations. A marked graph input is a vertex-colored simple graph with a marked union of vertex colors and a displayed vertex order. We construct one marked graph with one identified vertex per original edge in order, such that its isomorphisms induce precisely the input matroid isomorphisms, preserving these marks.

Obtain the cycle matroid's connected components from the biconnected graph blocks, ignoring isolated vertices, with every loop and bridge in a singleton component. On each nonsingleton component with at least three edges compute the standard triconnected decomposition of its representing graph. Its pieces are bonds, polygons, or simple 3-connected graphs, with a matched virtual edge pair for each tree edge. The theorem connecting the two standard decompositions states that the pieces' cycle matroids with their common virtual markers constitute the standard matroid decomposition. Thus an isomorphism of original cycle matroids carries these pieces, markers and incidence to each other, preserving the exact action on all original edges. This follows from uniqueness up to relabeling virtual markers, with original elements held fixed, by transporting the decomposition along the given isomorphism. This uses Cunningham and Edmonds, Theorems 1 and 18 and the comparison of the two decompositions in Section 5. Their discussion of Hopcroft–Tarjan gives polynomial computation on graph input. For two parallel edges use a single rank-one, two-element piece; for singleton components use a loop or coloop piece according to the edge type. [Cunningham and Edmonds, A Combinatorial Decomposition Theory](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-1980-057-7) · [Hopcroft and Tarjan, Dividing a Graph into Triconnected Components](https://doi.org/10.1137/0202012)

Create a simple colored graph from this representation as follows.

- Make one piece vertex for each piece, colored by its type. Use different colors for polygons, bonds, 3-connected pieces, single loops, single coloops and the two-edge case.
- Make a vertex for every occurrence of an edge of a piece and join it to its piece vertex. Edge occurrences are marked as real or virtual by disjoint palettes. For real occurrences propagate the original color and mark. A real edge has exactly one occurrence.
- For a virtual pair add a distinct connector vertex joined to its two edge-occurrence vertices. Connectors have their own color. In particular this does not identify endpoints of the two virtual edges.
- In each 3-connected piece make a separate vertex per skeleton vertex, using a further color. Attach it to its piece vertex and to precisely the edge-occurrence vertices incident to it in that skeleton. For every other type use no original graph vertices. The port set of a bond or polygon has its full symmetric group as its intrinsic matroid automorphism group. It is important to discard the cyclic ordering of the edges in a polygon.

For a matroid isomorphism the decomposition uniqueness induces a bijection of pieces and virtual pairs with the given real edge map. Within each simple 3-connected piece, Whitney's edge-respecting theorem gives a skeleton vertex isomorphism inducing the specified edge map. Within a polygon or bond any prescribed port bijection is valid. The construction therefore extends the matroid isomorphism to a color-preserving graph isomorphism. Conversely every constructed graph isomorphism preserves pieces and virtual matching. It respects the local matroid by incidence in each 3-connected piece and by uniformity in each other piece. Their compatible maps combine through 2-sums to preserve the original matroid. Explicitly, at a gluing marker the circuits of the 2-sum are the circuits of either piece avoiding the marker and the unions, with the marker deleted, of a circuit from each side containing that marker. The paired edge map preserves precisely those combinations. Iterating through the tree and taking the direct sum of matroid connected components gives the original edge bijection. Vertex permutations of the constructed graph on the marked set are exactly the marked real-edge permutations, since real vertices carry the input edge order and no other vertex lies in a marked color. All objects have polynomial size. [Whitney, Congruent Graphs and the Connectivity of Graphs](https://doi.org/10.2307/2371086)

Conversely, to reduce marked graph comparison to marked graphic-matroid comparison, add three anchor vertices forming a triangle, and join each anchor to every original vertex. For a nonempty graph the resulting simple graph is 3-connected. Color the triangle edges individually. Color each anchor-to-original edge by its anchor and the original vertex color. Give the original edges a separate color. Mark only the links from the first anchor to the original marked set, in the corresponding original order. The triangle colors fix every anchor under a color-preserving edge map. The Whitney theorem now makes matroid isomorphisms exactly the graph isomorphisms of the augmentation, so they have the same marked permutation as the original graph. Empty graphs can be compared directly and sent to fixed yes or no instances.

Thus marked graphic-matroid parity and marked graph parity are equivalent. As an unsigned corollary, arbitrary graph-input graphic matroid isomorphism reduces to a single ordinary graph isomorphism instance. One can remove graph colors by the standard polynomial vertex-color gadgets in the unsigned problem. Rao and Sarma give a polynomial-time Turing reduction in their Theorem 5.3 and explicitly ask for a many-one reduction in their concluding open-problems section. The construction here instead represents all compatible piece maps in one colored graph and preserves the precise original edge actions. A closely related construction was given by Bläsius, Krug, Rutter and Wagner for planar graphs. Their skeleton graph in §6, Lemma 6 uses the virtual-edge pairing and unrestricted permutations within polygon skeletons, and §7 applies it to planar 2-isomorphism. The argument here uses the same kind of decomposition encoding for arbitrary graph inputs and tracks the marked original edge action. [Rao and Sarma, On the Complexity of Matroid Isomorphism Problems](https://www.cse.iitm.ac.in/~bvrr/jour/RS1.pdf) · [Bläsius et al., Testing Mutual Duality of Planar Graphs](https://hpi.de/friedrich/docs/publications/2014/CGA.pdf)
