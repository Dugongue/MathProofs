# Recovering bilinear-forms graphs from adjacency

For any finite field and any dimensions \(m,n\), there is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm on a raw graph that recognizes the bilinear-forms graph on \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrices with rank-one differences as edges. All parameters may vary. It recovers an explicit isomorphism to a standard model, handles complements, and solves isomorphism with one input promised to be such a graph. For \(\min(m,n)=2\) this applies to a full family of affine rank-three graphs relevant to Grochow–Urisman Question 1.9. The method combines the earlier rank-based reconstruction and the projective incidence descent with one scalar-recovery linear system.

## The projective coordinates

Fix a vertex as origin, using translation invariance in the model. Graph distance is the rank of the difference, by rank subadditivity and a decomposition into rank-one summands. For \(m,n\ge2\), a rank-one matrix is a nonzero scalar times \(u_L v_M^{\mathsf T}\), where \(L,M\) are its column and row lines. In the rank-one shell, two different vertices are adjacent exactly when they have the same \(L\) or the same \(M\). The equal closed neighborhoods inside this shell give the scalar classes on each pair \((L,M)\). Distinct pairs have different closed neighborhoods because both line sets have at least three members. The quotient is a rook graph. Its maximal cliques are its two types of coordinate fibers, and the clique intersection graph gives the two sets of lines.

For a vertex \(X\) at distance \(r\ge2\) from the origin, use the rank-one vertices \(R\) for which \(\operatorname{dist}(X,R)=r-1\). Such a vertex must have its column and row lines in the corresponding spaces of \(X\), by equality in rank subadditivity for \(X=(X-R)+R\). On those spaces one has a nondegenerate pairing \(\beta_X\) given by inversion of the restricted linear map from the dual of the row space to the column space. The determinant lemma on that support gives the exact drop criterion
\[
R=z u_Lv_M^{\mathsf T},\qquad z\beta_X(u_L,v_M)=1.
\]
In particular for each pair of lines in the support there is one descending scalar choice if the pairing is nonzero, and none otherwise. The projections of the descending set give all lines in both support spaces, by nondegeneracy. The rank-two vertices therefore supply all the projective lines of both projective geometries, and on each pair of support planes the zero pairing defines a projectivity between their point sets.

If both dimensions are at least three, coordinatize from the recovered projective point-line incidence using the affine frame procedure for Grassmann graphs. Try the isomorphisms of the two recovered fields, enumerating possible images of a field generator and checking all products and sums. The number tested is at most the field size. This includes the true relative semilinear choice. If one dimension is two, use any rank-two zero-pairing projectivity to transfer coordinates from the other side to that projective line.

If both dimensions are two, fix one zero-pairing matching to identify the two line sets. The set of all such matchings, composed with that fixed identification, is precisely \(\operatorname{PGL}_2(\mathbb F_q)\). Every perfect bilinear pairing on two planes is available as the inverse of some rank-two matrix, and its zero matcher is a projectivity. The projectivities are thus available explicitly as permutations from the rank-two shell. Name two distinct points \(\infty,0\) and a third point 1. Among the stabilizers of \(\infty\), the identity and those fixing no other point are precisely the translations. This gives addition by applying the unique translation sending 0 to the desired addend. The stabilizers of both \(\infty,0\) are the nonzero scalings and give multiplication by the image of 1. The resulting field and projective coordinate identifications agree with the model up to a permitted projective change of coordinates.

## Recapturing the scalar labels

Choose a nonzero representative vector of each coordinate line. Give every rank-one vertex \(R\) a scalar variable \(s_R\), and every vertex \(X\) of rank at least two a bilinear form \(b_X\) on the two recovered support spaces, represented by its coefficients on bases. Solve the homogeneous linear equations
\[
b_X(u_L,v_M)=
\begin{cases}
s_R,&\text{if the scalar vertex }R\text{ descends at }(L,M),\\
0,&\text{if none descends}.
\end{cases}
\]
For the correct relative field identification the true reciprocal scalars \(s_R=1/z_R\) and the inverse pairings give a nonzero solution. Every solution is a common scalar multiple of this one. To see this, fix a row line \(M\) and two independent column lines \(L,L'\). For any prescribed nonzero values \(a,b\) on their chosen representatives against \(v_M\), choose a nondegenerate rank-two pairing with that column of coefficients, completing it by an independent second column. A graph vertex realizes that pairing on the chosen support planes. The candidate functional \(b_X(-,v_M)\) vanishes on the true functional's kernel, by the zero equations on all support points. It is therefore proportional to that true functional, including if it is zero. In terms of the assigned reciprocal scalars on the two cells, this equates their assigned-to-true ratios for arbitrary \(a,b\). Moving through column lines and then, by the symmetric argument, through row lines forces one global ratio for every scalar vertex. The equations then force all of \(b_X\), since evaluation on all pairs includes bases.

For each candidate coordinatization use any nonzero solution when the solution space is one-dimensional. Recover the matrices by scalar inversion on the rank-one shell and pairings inversion on higher supports. Verify a bijection to the model and every edge. A common nonzero scalar on the pairings simply rescales all the output matrices by the inverse scalar. Use caps on point and vertex enumeration and reject unless the predicted model order \(q^{mn}\) equals the input order. All loops are polynomial in that order, including the linear system. The case of one-dimensional row or column space is a complete graph of prime-power size, and the zero-dimensional singleton can be treated directly. Running on graph complements handles the other convention. This proof is informal. [Grochow–Urisman, Question 1.9](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26244v2#S1.SS3)

## Exact automorphism cycle index over prime fields

Assume \(q\) is prime and \(m,n\ge2\). The reconstructed coordinates also give the exact cycle index in time polynomial in the vertex count \(N=q^{mn}\). Translation reduces automorphisms to those fixing the origin. These preserve the two projective sides of the rank-one shell, up to interchange if \(m=n\). On a side of vector dimension at least three, projective coordinatization gives a linear projectivity because the field is prime. On a two-dimensional side paired with a calibrated side, the family of zero-pairing matchings to each recovered projective line forces that side's action to be a projectivity too. If both sides have dimension two, identify them by a fixed matching. The two actions preserve the matching set \(\operatorname{PGL}_2\) under left and right composition. Align one action with the other using a member of this group, and normalize it to fix \(\infty,0,1\) by a projectivity. Its conjugation preserves the point stabilizers and the characterization of translations by their fixed points. Thus it preserves the reconstructed field addition and multiplication, and is the identity over a prime field.

After undoing the resulting row and column projectivities and any transpose, an automorphism fixes every projective shell cell. Pulling the true scalar and inverse-pairing assignments back by this automorphism gives a solution of the same linear system. Uniqueness up to scale forces it to act by one common scalar on the matrices. Hence all automorphisms are generated by translations, maps \(X\mapsto AXB^{-1}\), and transpose in the square case. The product linear action has a kernel of size \(q-1\), given by the equal scalar pairs.

Enumerate the rational canonical conjugacy classes in both general linear groups, choosing representatives and computing their sizes from the centralizers of the associated finite modules. There are \(q^{O(m+n)}\) pairs of classes, since characteristic polynomials contribute at most \(q^{m+n}\) choices and the primary partitions contribute at most \(2^{m+n}\). For each representative pair enumerate the \(N\) translations, compute the cycles of the resulting affine permutation by a table scan, and weight by the product of the class sizes divided by \(q-1\). Conjugation by a fixed linear transformation permutes the translations, so these weights count the full coset. In the square case a linear map followed by transpose, written \((A,B)\tau\), is conjugate to \((AB^{-\mathsf T},I)\tau\) by \((I,B^{-1})\). Ordinary conjugacy classes of \(AB^{-\mathsf T}\) suffice to sum over these cases, since \((P,P^{-\mathsf T})\) acts by conjugation on its first component and fixes its second component at \(I\). Each fixed product has exactly \(|\operatorname{GL}_n(q)|\) pairs \((A,B)\), and the same scalar kernel applies. This produces the cycle index as a polynomial-size list of signatures and counts, normalizing by the automorphism group order if desired. It computes the Gassmann data for the prime-field bilinear rank-three family and leaves the comparison across all strongly regular graphs as a separate question. [Grochow–Urisman, Questions 1.8–1.9](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26244v2#S1.SS3)
