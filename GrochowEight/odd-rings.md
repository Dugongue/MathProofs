# Uniform deterministic isometry decisions over odd local principal ideal rings

There is a deterministic bit-polynomial **decision** algorithm for simultaneous congruence of two ordered tuples of symmetric or alternating bilinear forms on finitely generated modules over an explicitly presented finite commutative local principal ideal ring \(R\) of odd residue characteristic. The bound is polynomial in the input length and \(\log |R|\), with no polynomial dependence on the numerical residue characteristic. Forms may have arbitrary common radicals, and the \(i\)-th form may take values in \(R/\mathfrak m^{z_i}\). This proves GP015 in the stated decision model. The result allows an explicit additive-coordinate ring presentation or the arithmetic/division presentation of Part V with a uniformizer \(\pi\), residue cardinality \(q\), and polynomial bit-cost ring operations, including comparison of elements and division when an element divides another. The assertion is a decision algorithm; a search algorithm would in particular need to extract square roots over odd prime fields in uniform deterministic time. [Grochow catalog GP015](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/GrochowOpenProblems/ISOMORPHISM.md#gp015) · [Grochow–Qiao–Stange–Sun, Theorem 5.22 and §2.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00735v1#S5.SS3)

For the additive-coordinate ring presentation, the residue data can also be computed. The characteristic of \(R\) is the additive order of \(1\), a power of the residue prime \(p\), so \(p\) follows by integer perfect-power tests. The quotient \(R/pR\) is an explicitly presented commutative \(\mathbb F_p\)-algebra and \(pR\) is nilpotent. The kernel of sufficiently high Frobenius on \(R/pR\) is its nilradical: an iterate count equal to the \(\mathbb F_p\)-dimension suffices, and the iterate is \(\mathbb F_p\)-linear. Its inverse image in \(R\) is the maximal ideal. Compute the square of this ideal using generators and choose \(\pi\) in the ideal outside its square, unless the ideal is zero. Such \(\pi\) generates it, by Nakayama's lemma for ideals in the local ring. In the zero case take \(\pi=0,e=1\). Count \(q=|R/\mathfrak m|\) from additive quotient orders. All these computations have polynomial bit cost and also make ring division an additive linear congruence computation.

## Exact reduction to one norm

Write \(\mathfrak m=(\pi)\), with \(\pi^e=0\), and embed \(R/\mathfrak m^{z_i}\) into \(R\) by multiplying by \(\pi^{e-z_i}\). The map is well defined and injective because the annihilator of \(\pi^{e-z_i}\) is \(\mathfrak m^{z_i}\). It preserves and reflects the form constraints. In this way regard the forms as arrows \(B_i,C_i:M\to M^\vee\), where \(M^\vee=\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,R)\). The natural map \(M\to M^{\vee\vee}\) is an isomorphism. This follows on each cyclic summand \(R/\mathfrak m^s\) from \(\operatorname{Ann}(\pi^s)=\pi^{e-s}R\), and hence on all finite \(R\)-modules.

Find two automorphisms \(P,Q\) of \(M\) with \(P^\vee B_i Q=C_i\), or report that none exist. This is ordinary module isomorphism on the two explicit representations of the two-vertex \(m\)-arrow quiver, with source \(M\) and target \(M^\vee\). Its path algebra over \(R\) has rank \(m+2\) and no length-two paths. The arrow equations give the left/right maps, and every automorphism of the target dual is itself dual to an automorphism of \(M\). The field characteristic need not be enumerated. The compact ring and module formats for the deterministic finite-ring module isomorphism theorem are additive invariant factors and multiplication/action structure constants. This covers the explicit additive coordinate model. [Ciocănea-Teodorescu, Corollary 1.2 and input encoding](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.08365)

For the arithmetic-only presentation one can run the splitter proof of that same theorem using \(R\) as central scalar ring. All Hom and End spaces are kernels of linear maps between coordinate modules, so generators and basic submodule operations come from Smith computations. For completeness, for a trial map \(f:U\to V\) the products \(g f\), \(g\in\operatorname{Hom}(V,U)\), form a left ideal \(I\) of \(E=\operatorname{End}(U)\). Test nilpotency by successively squaring \(I\) until it vanishes or stabilizes at a nonzero \(L=L^2\). In the latter case choose \(a\) with \(La\ne0\). If \(Ea=La\), solve \(xa=a\) for \(x\in L\); the solution is not nilpotent and its expression in the generators \(gf\) recovers a corresponding \(g\). If \(La\subsetneq Ea\), choose \(c\in La\) with \(Lc\ne0\) using \(L^2a=La\), and replace \(a\) by \(c\). The left ideals \(Ea\) strictly shrink in the latter steps. Fitting decomposition on \(gf,fg\) splits a common nonzero summand whenever a nonnilpotent product is found. It suffices to test an \(R\)-generating list of \(\operatorname{Hom}(U,V)\): if no generator admits a nonnilpotent product, all their product left ideals lie in the radical of \(E\), as do all such products for every linear combination. A module isomorphism is then impossible for nonzero \(U,V\). Recursively remove the matched direct summands. Krull–Schmidt cancellation gives correctness. Every chain length is bounded by \(\log_2\) of the cardinality of the represented module, and no step requires a list of field elements.

Set \(H_i=P^\vee B_iP\) and \(D=P^{-1}Q\). Then \(H_iD=D^\vee H_i=C_i\), because the two compared forms have the same symmetry sign. Use the directly presented pair adjoint algebra
\[
 A=\{(X,Y):X^\vee H_i=H_iY\text{ for all }i\},\quad
 (X,Y)(U,V)=(XU,VY),\quad (X,Y)^*=(Y,X).
\]
It exists with this involution even when all forms are singular. Let \(h=(D,D)\), a symmetric unit. For a unit \(v=(X,Y)\), \(v^*v=(YX,YX)\). The norm equation \(v^*v=h\) implies \(X^\vee H_i X=C_i\). Conversely if \(X^\vee H_iX=C_i\), the pair \((X,DX^{-1})\) lies in \(A^\times\) and has norm \(h\). Thus deciding the norm decides the original comparison. All linear operations used on \(A\) and its embedding are over explicit finite \(R\)-modules using their cyclic summands.

Let \(J=\operatorname{Jac}(A)\). A norm in \(A/J\) lifts to a norm in \(A\). Indeed lift a residue norm witness to a unit \(v_0\), pass to \(h'=(v_0^{-1})^* h v_0^{-1}=1+j\) with symmetric nilpotent \(j\), and take the truncated square root in \(R[j]\). Its coefficients are obtained recursively using only division by \(2\). The square root is symmetric, so gives the required norm after returning to \(h\). The other direction is immediate by reduction. This is the odd-residue lift from Part V, Proposition 5.29.

## Find the radical without a large-prime cost

Put \(k=R/\mathfrak m\), \(p=\operatorname{char}k\), \(T=A/\mathfrak m A\), and \(d=\dim_k T\). The ideal \(\mathfrak m A\) is nilpotent, so \(A/J=T/\operatorname{Jac}(T)\). A basis and multiplication table of \(T\) are obtained by Smith operations in the displayed \(R\)-modules. We now compute \(\operatorname{Jac}(T)\) without splitting any algebra.

Filter the left regular \(T\)-module by the powers of its radical. On the resulting semisimple layers a natural simple module \(K_i^{a_i}\) for a factor \(M_{a_i}(K_i)\) of the semisimple quotient has positive total multiplicity \(1\le \mu_i\le d\), because it appears already in the regular module of the quotient. If \(z_i\) is the projection of \(z\in T\) to that factor, multiplicativity of the determinant along a preserved filtration gives
\[
 P_z(u):=\det_k(1-uL_z)
  =\prod_i \operatorname{N}_{K_i/k}\big(\det_{K_i}(1-u z_i)\big)^{\mu_i}.
\]
Here the norm of the polynomial is its product under all embeddings of the finite separable extension, and the identity follows by restricting scalars on each natural simple module. This formula is used to prove the algorithm. The algorithm computes the left-hand side from the regular matrices, without computing the factors on the right.

Maintain an ideal \(I_r\subseteq T\) whose image in the semisimple quotient consists of the factors with \(p^r\mid\mu_i\), and that contains \(\operatorname{Jac}(T)\). Begin with \(I_0=T\). For \(x\in I_r\), \(y\in T\), the matrix of \(xy\) vanishes in all other factors. Therefore \(P_{xy}\) is a \(p^r\)-th power in \(k[u]\), and the inverse Frobenius of its coefficient of \(u^{p^r}\) equals
\[
 B_r(x,y)=
 -\sum_{i: \,p^r\mid\mu_i}
 \frac{\mu_i}{p^r}
 \operatorname{Tr}_{K_i/k}(\operatorname{tr}(x_i y_i)).
\]
Indeed the indicated coefficient of a \(p^r\)-th power is the \(p^r\)-th power of the linear coefficient of its root. Thus \(B_r\) is \(k\)-bilinear. Evaluate it on bases of \(I_r,T\) and compute its left kernel. The trace pairing on each full matrix algebra over the separable field \(K_i\) is nondegenerate over \(k\). Therefore this kernel is exactly \(I_{r+1}\). Once \(p^r>d\), the remaining ideal equals the radical, since no multiplicity is divisible by \(p^r\).

The determinant polynomial can be computed by division-free characteristic polynomial arithmetic over \(k\). Inverse Frobenius on \(k\) is exponentiation by \(q/p\) for the inverse of the \(p\)-th power. At most \(\lfloor\log_p d\rfloor+1\) kernel steps are used. For \(p>d\), the first step alone is just the usual regular trace kernel, with all positive multiplicities nonzero in \(k\). For small \(p\) the higher coefficients replace that step, with polynomial cost also in the residue extension degree. This supplies the structure constants of \(S=A/J\) and its involution for every \(p\). For comparison, Part V gives a deterministic bound polynomial in \(p\) for full factor splitting, and Ivanyos–Qiao state the underlying finite-field dependence as polynomial in the dimension, \(p\), and \(\log q\). Those full splitting routines are unnecessary here. [Berkowitz, division-free characteristic polynomial](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0190(84)90018-8) · [Part V, Theorem 5.27](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00735v1#S5.SS3) · [Ivanyos–Qiao, Theorem 18](https://arxiv.org/html/1708.03495#S3.SS5)

## Identify and test the discriminants without splitting the center

The center \(C=Z(S)\) is a product of finite field extensions of \(k\). Compute it by linear equations. Let \(I\) be the ideal of \(C\) generated by \(c-c^*\) for all \(c\) in a \(k\)-basis of \(C\). On any ambient field factor, either all these differences are zero or the ideal is the whole field. Thus the annihilator of \(I\) is precisely the product of factors with no exchange and trivial center involution. Exchanged factors and fixed factors with a nontrivial center involution always admit the norm of a symmetric unit. For the former, choose freely one coordinate and determine the other. For the latter, the question is equivalence of two nondegenerate Hermitian forms of equal dimension over a finite extension with quadratic involution, which always are equivalent. On the remaining first-kind factors, the involution is of orthogonal or symplectic type. Symplectic factors again have no obstruction because the affected two nondegenerate alternating forms have equal dimension. Orthogonal factors pass exactly when the reduced determinant of \(h\) is a square, since this determinant is the ratio of the two discriminants. These are the same individual-factor criteria as in the usual semisimple norm algorithm. Over an odd finite field the symmetric criterion follows by diagonalization: every binary nonsingular diagonal form \(a x^2+b y^2\) represents \(1\), because the two sets of possible \(a x^2\) and \(1-b y^2\) each have \((q+1)/2\) elements and therefore intersect. Split off such unit lines until only the determinant square class remains. In the alternating case, symplectic elimination gives the standard form. In the Hermitian case, diagonalization and surjectivity of the finite-field norm give the standard form. These are existence arguments and require no constructive root routines. [Ivanyos–Qiao, §§3.5.2–3.5.6](https://arxiv.org/html/1708.03495#S3.SS5)

Here are polynomial operations to determine just these orthogonal components. An ideal of \(C\) is an idempotent direct summand. Given its \(k\)-linear basis, compute its annihilator by linear equations and solve \(1=e+(1-e)\) with terms in the two summands to obtain the corresponding idempotent. For any explicitly presented finite \(C\)-module \(N\), extract one nonzero vector \(\bar v\) in the quotient by the summands already extracted. Compute the annihilator of \(\bar v\) in \(C\) and let \(e\) be the complementary support idempotent. Lift \(\bar v\) and multiply the lift by \(e\) to get \(v\). The new cyclic summand \(Cv\cong Ce\) maps injectively into the quotient, because \(c\bar v=0\) implies \(ce=0\). Iterate to obtain \(N=\bigoplus_i Cv_i\) with known support idempotents \(e_i\), in at most \(\dim_k N\) iterations. For each integer rank \(r\), compute the idempotent \(d_r\) on which exactly \(r\) of the \(e_i\) have value one. Start \(d_0=1\), and on adjoining \(e_i\) update \(d'_r=(1-e_i)d_r+e_i d_{r-1}\). All these equations are in the commutative algebra \(C\); they compute the integer ranks without reducing them modulo the characteristic. If needed, obtain a free basis on a constant-rank stratum by taking, at each component, the \(j\)-th active \(v_i\) as the \(j\)-th basis element. The same prefix idempotents give the formula for this element without explicitly identifying the separate fields: if \(p_{i-1,j-1}\) records exactly \(j-1\) active supports before index \(i\), sum \(d_re_i p_{i-1,j-1}v_i\) over \(i\).

Restrict to the first-kind portion of \(S\), where both \(S\) and \(\mathrm{Sym}(S)\) are modules over the surviving center. At a matrix factor of degree \(a\) the first of these has rank \(a^2\). The second has rank \(a(a+1)/2\) in the orthogonal case and \(a(a-1)/2\) in the symplectic case. These ranks follow from multiplying a self-adjoint element by its symmetric or skew-symmetric Gram matrix. The preceding rank idempotents distinguish the cases and the matrix degrees. Work separately for each surviving orthogonal stratum of matrix degree \(a\), at most \(d\) strata.

On an orthogonal stratum with center \(C'\), choose a free \(C'\)-basis of the corresponding summand of \(S\). Use linear algebra in its displayed \(k\)-coordinates to obtain the matrix of \(L_h\) over \(C'\); all systems are \(k\)-linear. Over each actual full matrix factor of degree \(a\), this left regular action is \(a\) copies of the natural action, so
\[
   \det_{C'}(1-uL_h)=F(u)^a,\qquad F(0)=1,
\]
where the coordinates of \(F(u)\) are the natural determinants \(\det(1-u h_i)\) of the unknown factors. Compute the left side by the division-free characteristic polynomial algorithm over the commutative ring \(C'\).

Write \(a=p^r b\) with \(p\nmid b\). Remove the \(p^r\)-th power by dividing the exponents by \(p^r\) and applying inverse Frobenius to the surviving coefficients. Inverse Frobenius can be computed on \(C'\) without knowing the field factors. For a \(k\)-basis \(e_j\) of \(C'\), the vectors \(e_j^p\) are again a \(k\)-basis by semilinearity and bijectivity of Frobenius on the finite étale algebra. Express the given element in this basis and apply inverse Frobenius in \(k\) to each coefficient. Repeat \(r\) times. The resulting polynomial is \(F(u)^b\). Its coefficient of \(u^j\) is \(b [u^j]F\) plus an expression using only lower coefficients of \(F\), because \(F(0)=1\). Since \(b\) is a unit in \(k\), compute these coefficients consecutively for \(j=1,\ldots,a\), using polynomial multiplication. The reduced determinant \(z\in(C')^\times\) is \((-1)^a [u^a]F\), coordinatewise the determinant of \(h_i\). No factoring is performed, including when \(p\) divides \(a\).

Finally let \(q=|k|\). For \(1\le \ell\le\dim_k C'\), the ideal generated by \(x^{q^\ell}-x\) for \(x\) running through a \(k\)-basis of \(C'\) is zero at exactly those field factors whose degree \(j\) divides \(\ell\). Indeed Frobenius to this power is \(k\)-linear, and it is the identity on \(F_{q^j}\) precisely when \(j\mid\ell\). Compute the annihilator idempotent and remove the idempotents of proper divisors already found. This yields the idempotent \(f_\ell\) supported on degree-\(\ell\) factors. Those factors pass simultaneously exactly when
\[
                   f_\ell z^{(q^\ell-1)/2}=f_\ell .
\]
The power is calculated by repeated squaring, with \(O(\ell\log q)\) exponent bits. Testing all strata is equivalent to all orthogonal discriminants being squares. This gives exact YES and NO decisions by the radical lift and the norm reduction.

Every operation above is a polynomial number of linear algebra or ring arithmetic operations, with exponents of polynomial bit length. The number \(q\) is part of the residue-field encoding or follows from \(|R|=q^e\), and the prime base \(p\) follows by integer perfect-power calculation when it is not supplied. The only root operation is inverse Frobenius in a finite field or étale algebra, implemented by the stated exponentiation and linear algebra. Square testing never extracts a square root. The proof and stated complexity therefore apply uniformly, including \(R=\mathbb Z/p^e\mathbb Z\) with \(p\) encoded in binary.
