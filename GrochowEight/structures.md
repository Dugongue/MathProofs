# Polynomial-time isometry for binary matrix and quadratic tuples

**GP013.** Given two ordered tuples of symmetric matrices over \(\mathbb F_2\), there is a deterministic bit-polynomial algorithm that finds a simultaneous congruence or decides that none exists. This allows arbitrary singular slices and arbitrary adjoint radicals.

**GP014.** The same search and decision conclusion holds for ordered tuples of quadratic forms over \(\mathbb F_2\), including tuples with nonzero common polar radical.

We prove symmetric tuple congruence over \(\mathbb Z/2^t\mathbb Z\) in time polynomial in the ordinary dense bit input and \(t\), and give a witness-preserving reduction of binary quadratic tuple isometry to the case \(t=2\). The two problem formulations are GP013 and GP014 in the existing Grochow catalog. The former is also explicitly posed by Grochow–Qiao–Stange–Sun. The previous unconditional general quadratic algorithm has running time \(2^{O((m+n)^{3/2})}\). The result concerns **ordered tuples**. [Grochow catalog, GP013 and GP014](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/GrochowOpenProblems/ISOMORPHISM.md#gp013) · [Grochow–Qiao–Stange–Sun, §1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00735v1#S1.SS4) · [Grochow–Ivanyos–Qiao–Sun, Theorem 1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00874)

## 1. The explicit left–right comparison and the norm equation

Put \(R=\mathbb Z/2^t\mathbb Z\), \(t\ge1\). Begin with two symmetric tuples \((B_i)\) and \((C_i)\) of common matrix order \(n>0\). All additive operations in submodules and quotients of finite free \(R\)-modules can be implemented by modular Smith normal form. For a unital finite \(R\)-algebra \(E\) so presented, \(2E\) is nilpotent and lies in the Jacobson radical. Compute the radical \(J(E)\) as the inverse image of the radical of the explicitly presented finite \(\mathbb F_2\)-algebra \(E/2E\). The finite-algebra algorithms give an effective decomposition of \(E/J(E)\) into full matrix algebras over finite fields, including coordinates, matrix units, and preimages. For this step use the deterministic small-characteristic version of the finite-algebra algorithms. Its dependence on the base characteristic is harmless over \(\mathbb F_2\). [Rónyai, Computing the structure of finite algebras](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0747-7171(08)80017-X) · [Brooksbank–Wilson, Theorem 2.5 and Remark 2.1](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9947-2011-05388-2)

Find \(P,Q\in\operatorname{GL}_n(R)\) with \(P^T B_i Q=C_i\), or reject if none exist. Here is an explicit construction from the preceding algebra routines. Form the endomorphism algebra of the direct sum of the two representations of the ordered arrows \(B_i,C_i:R^n\to R^n\). This algebra consists of matrix pairs \((L,M)\) with \(L(B_i\oplus C_i)=(B_i\oplus C_i)M\) for every \(i\), with coordinatewise multiplication. Let \(e,f\) be the complementary idempotents projecting onto the two summands. The summands are isomorphic exactly when \(e,f\) are conjugate by a unit. In the semisimple quotient, this is the rank comparison of their idempotent images in every full matrix factor. Construct a conjugator in that quotient by choosing bases of the corresponding images and kernels, and lift it to a unit \(u\) of the algebra. A lift of a semisimple unit is a unit because the radical is nilpotent. Now \(a=ueu^{-1}\equiv f\bmod J(E)\), and \(c=f a+(1-f)(1-a)\) satisfies \(c a=f c\) and \(c\equiv1\bmod J(E)\). Thus \(cu\) gives exact conjugacy. Restricting its two role maps to the summands and inverting the necessary map gives \(P,Q\). All assertions hold for degenerate tuples, since the two role modules and their idempotent images are explicit. The case \(n=0\) or an empty tuple has the identity solution.

Write \(H_i=P^T B_i P\), \(D=P^{-1}Q\). Then \(H_iD=D^TH_i=C_i\). Define the involutive adjoint algebra
\[
 A=\{(X,Y):X^T H_i=H_iY\text{ for every }i\},\quad
 (X,Y)(X',Y')=(XX',Y'Y),\quad (X,Y)^*=(Y,X).
\]
It is given by linear equations and explicit matrix multiplication. The element \(c=(D,D)\) is a self-adjoint unit. For a unit \(v=(X,Y)\) of \(A\), \(v^*v=(YX,YX)\). Consequently \(v^*v=c\) gives \(X^T H_i X=C_i\). Conversely such an \(X\) makes \((X,DX^{-1})\) a unit of \(A\) of norm \(c\), by the same identity. The desired original congruence is \(PX\).

## 2. The radical and the exact lifting criterion

For a finite involutive algebra \(A\), put \(J=J(A)\), \(S=A/J\), \(\mathrm{Sym}(A)=\{x:x=x^*\}\), and \(Q(x)=x^*x\). We use the additive subgroup
\[
 D_0=\{j+j^*+k^*k:j,k\in J\}\subseteq \mathrm{Sym}(A)\cap J.
\]
The identity \(Q(k+l)=Q(k)+Q(l)+k^*l+l^*k\) proves closure under addition, and \(-Q(k)=Q(k)-\operatorname{Tr}(Q(k))\) gives additive inverses, with \(\operatorname{Tr}(z)=z+z^*\). The subgroup is obtained from traces and norms of additive generators of \(J\). It is stable under \(d\mapsto a^*da\) for every \(a\in A\). Indeed the trace term becomes \(\operatorname{Tr}(a^*ja)\) and the norm term becomes \(Q(ka)\).

Let \(\Gamma=\mathrm{Sym}(A)/D_0\). The map \(\Phi_1:S\to\Gamma\), \(\bar x\mapsto[x^*x]\), is well defined by the norm expansion. We show that, for \(S\) a semisimple algebra over a finite field of characteristic two,
\[
 \operatorname{im}\big(U(A)\to U(S)\big)
   =\{\bar g\in U(S):\Phi_1(\bar g\bar x)=\Phi_1(\bar x)\text{ for every }\bar x\},
 \qquad U(A)=\{u\in A^\times:u^*u=1\}. \tag{1}
\]
The corresponding statement for \(U(S)\) uses the induced involution.

It suffices to lift the factors for the involution-orbits of simple summands. Cross products between different such summands vanish in \(S\), and therefore cross terms in the norm expansion are traces from \(J\) and vanish in \(\Gamma\). If \(\bar g\) preserves \(\Phi_1\), each factor embedded with identity elsewhere also preserves it. Necessity in (1) follows directly from a unitary lift.

On one involution-orbit summand, every unitary \(\bar g\) has a presentation
\[
 \bar g=1+\bar x\beta\bar x^*,\qquad
 \bar x\in S\bar e,\quad \beta\in\bar e S\bar e^*,\quad
 \beta\beta'=\bar e,\quad\beta'\beta=\bar e^* \tag{2}
\]
for an idempotent \(\bar e\), with \(\bar x^*\bar y=\bar e^*\) for some \(\bar y\in S\bar e^*\). For a fixed full matrix component, realize the involution as \(M^*=H^{-1}M^{\sigma T}H\) for a nondegenerate symmetric or Hermitian form over its center \(k\), where \(\sigma\) is trivial or its involutory field automorphism. The kernel of \(\bar g-I\) is the orthogonal complement of its image. Use a full column matrix \(P_0\) on that image and write \(\bar g-I=P_0 C P_0^{\sigma T}H\), with \(C\) invertible of order \(r=\operatorname{rank}(\bar g-I)\). Take \(\bar e\) to be the first-\(r\)-coordinate idempotent, \(\bar x\) to have \(P_0\) in those columns and zero elsewhere, and \(\beta=\operatorname{diag}(C,0)H\), with partial inverse \(H^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(C^{-1},0)\). Full column rank gives the claimed right inverse of \(\bar x^*\) on its corner. On an exchanged pair, use coordinates \(M_a(k)\times M_a(k)^{\mathrm{op}}\) with swap involution. Write \(\bar g=(G,G^{-1})\). The matrices \(G-I\) and \(G^{-1}-I\) have the same kernel and image. Factor both as \(P_0 C_i Q_0\) using the same full column and row matrices and invertible \(C_i\). Put \(P_0,Q_0\) into the selected columns and rows of the two components of \(\bar x\), use the same coordinate idempotent in each, and put \(C_i\) in the corresponding corners of \(\beta\). This gives (2) and the same partial right inverse. The identity factor needs no lift construction. For an individual factor, use zeros in all other components for \(\bar e,\bar x,\beta\).

Lift \(\bar e\) to an idempotent \(e\) across the nilpotent ideal \(J\), and lift \(\bar x\) to \(x\in Ae\). Choose a lift \(b\in eAe^*\) of \(\beta\). An inverse in the opposite corner exists by lifting the two partial inverse equations and correcting their products with finite geometric series in the respective corner radicals. Write this inverse \(b'\in e^*Ae\). Put \(w=bx^*\). Then
\[
 (1+xbx^*)^*(1+xbx^*)-1=w^*r w,\qquad
 r=b'+b'^*+x^*x\in e^*Ae.
\]
The condition on \(\bar g\), evaluated at \(1\), makes \(w^*rw\in D_0\). Lift \(\bar y\) to \(y\in Ae^*\). The element \(g=wy\in eAe^*\) has residue \(\beta\), so it has a partial inverse \(g'\in e^*Ae\). Multiplying by \(y^*,y\) and then by \((g')^*,g'\) shows
\[
 r\in\operatorname{Tr}(e^*Je)+Q(Je).
\]
In fact \(g^*rg\in D_0\), and \((g')^*D_0g'\) lies in the displayed subgroup. As the subgroup is closed under negation, choose \(r=\operatorname{Tr}(s)-Q(k)\) with \(s\in e^*Je\) and \(k\in Je\). Replace \(x\) by \(x+k\) and \(b'\) by \(b'-s-x^*k\), using the old \(x\) in the second replacement. The new \(r\) is \(r-\operatorname{Tr}(s)+Q(k)=0\). The residue \(\beta'\) of \(b'\) is unchanged, so a partial inverse still exists. The resulting \(1+xbx^*\) is a unit (its residue is a unit) and is now unitary. Products of these factor lifts give (1). The argument employs signed corrections and applies in mixed characteristic.

### A necessary check for relative markings

After finding a semisimple solution to \(\bar v^*\bar v=\bar c\), lift it to a unit \(v_0\) and replace \(c\) by \(h=v_0^{-*}c v_0^{-1}\equiv1\bmod J\). Before constructing a map \(\Phi_h:S\to\Gamma\) by \(\bar x\mapsto[x^* h x]\), test \(j^*hj\in D_0\) for all additive generators \(j\) of \(J\). Modulo \(D_0\), this is an additive map in \(j\), because its mixed terms are traces from \(J\). A norm \(h=v^*v\) always passes, since \(vj\in J\). If the test passes, all \(j\in J\) pass and the mixed terms between \(x\) and \(j\) are again traces from \(J\). Thus \(\Phi_h\) is well defined. An earlier workspace sketch omitted this check. For example, in \(\mathbb F_2[t]/(t^4)\) with identity involution, \(h=1+t\) gives \(t^*ht=t^2+t^3\notin D_0=\langle t^2\rangle\).

Find a semisimple unitary \(\bar w\) such that
\[
             \Phi_1(\bar w\bar x)=\Phi_h(\bar x)
                  \quad\text{for every }\bar x\in S.                  \tag{3}
\]
The next section proves that this is a constructive polynomial-time residue problem. If no such \(\bar w\) exists, \(h\) is no norm. Otherwise lift any such \(\bar w\) to a unit \(w\). It then suffices to test
\[
       w^{-*}h w^{-1}\in\{n^*n:n\in1+J\}.                            \tag{4}
\]
Indeed, if \(h=v^*v\), then \(\bar v\bar w^{-1}\) preserves \(\Phi_1\). By (1), \(v=uwn\) for a unitary \(u\) and \(n\in1+J\). The left side of (4) is the norm of \(wnw^{-1}\). Conversely a witness \(n'\) for (4) gives a witness \(n'wv_0\) for \(c\).

For (4) use a transporter orbit of the group \(1+J\) on the pair matrix module of the adjoint algebra, via \(a\mapsto n^*a n\). The ambient module is free over \(R\), and the action of every unit \(n\) extends linearly and invertibly to it. The group \(1+J\) is a finite \(2\)-group. Its generators are obtained by refining the powers of \(J\) by additive generators with factors of size two and choosing \(1+j\) at each step. The unipotent affine vector transporter algorithm of Sun finds a group word, or reports no transporter, in time polynomial in the ambient rank, \(t\), and the generator count. A word can be lifted directly to the adjoint units during the generator operations. [Sun, Appendix A, Lemmas A.21–A.24](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.05920v1)

## 3. Constructing the residue solution

We give the residue algorithm including the mixed-characteristic marking case. The involution permutes simple matrix factors. On an exchanged pair choose coordinates with a swap. On a fixed factor \(M_a(k)\) obtain the center involution \(\sigma\); solve \(H M^*=M^{\sigma T}H\) on matrix-unit generators. The defining description of anti-automorphisms of full matrix algebras gives a nonzero solution, and its solution space consists of the scalar multiples of one invertible \(H\). Normalize \(H^{\sigma T}=H\) by a scalar. When \(\sigma\ne1\), the scalar equation is a linear equation over the fixed field with a nonzero solution by the degree-two Hilbert 90 identity. If \(\sigma=1\), the equality holds already, because the possible multiplier is a square root of one in characteristic two.

The preliminary semisimple norm equation on an exchanged pair is solved by choosing the factor in one component freely and determining the other. On a fixed component it is equivalence of the nondegenerate forms of matrices \(H\) and \(H\bar c\), of the same symmetry. The elementary comparison methods described below check their types and construct the required change of basis. This gives \(v_0\), or an exact rejection.

For (3) treat each involution-orbit of simple factors separately, since cross traces vanish. In each fixed component take the first coordinate idempotent \(\bar e\) of rank one, and in an exchanged pair take rank \((1,1)\). It suffices to check (3) on \(S\bar e\) for that component. Every element of the component is a sum of right translates of elements of \(S\bar e\) by matrix units. More precisely, equality on \(S\bar e\) implies equality on every individual column space \(S\bar e_i\) by conjugating values by lifts of the appropriate matrix units on the right. For cross contributions between distinct column spaces, equality follows from \(\bar w^*\bar w=1\) and \(\bar h=1\). Indeed, the difference of the two mixed sesquilinear terms has zero residue and its trace lies in \(D_0\). This proves the sufficiency on sums as well.

On the column space write \(\phi_1,\phi_h\) for the two maps into \(\Gamma\). In a fixed component identify \(S\bar e\) with \(k^a\) and \(\bar e^*S\bar e\) with one copy of \(k\) so that \(\bar x^*\bar y\) corresponds to a nondegenerate symmetric or Hermitian form \(b(x,y)\). In an exchanged pair the corresponding space is \(k^a\oplus(k^a)^*\), with an ordered pair of coordinate pairings \(b((p,q),(p',q'))=(qp',q'p)\). This identification follows by multiplying the rank-one column and row matrices, using the opposite multiplication in the second factor.

Define \(\tau\) on the residue corner by \(\tau(\bar a)=[a+a^*]\) for any lift \(a\). The lift does not matter. Its image has exponent two: \(2A\subseteq J\), so twice the trace is the trace of an element of \(J\). Both \(\phi\)'s have the same polarization \(\tau(b(x,y))\) since \(h\equiv1\bmod J\). In particular \(2\phi(x)=-\tau(b(x,x))\). The ambient group \(\Gamma\) has exponent dividing four because \(4s=\operatorname{Tr}(2s)\) for \(s=s^*\) and \(2s\in J\). All computations of values, of the subgroup \(\Gamma[2]\) killed by two, and in elementary abelian quotients use the explicit additive coordinates.

The kernel of \(\tau\) is invariant under scaling the residue corner by scalars \(a^\sigma a\) in the fixed case, and by diagonal scalars in the exchange case. This is because the operation of right scalar multiplication on the column corner acts on its trace by congruence, under which \(D_0\) is stable. Here and below, when the rank-one idempotent is not self-adjoint, use \(a\bar e\) in its right scalar corner and \((a\bar e)^*\) in the left corner. Therefore, for a fixed component of the first kind, \(\tau\) is either zero or injective on \(k\): squares in a finite field of characteristic two fill \(k\). For a Hermitian factor with fixed field \(k_0\), \(\tau(a^\sigma)=\tau(a)\), so it kills the image of \(a\mapsto a+a^\sigma\), namely \(k_0\). Scaling by \(a^\sigma a\) covers \(k_0\), so the induced map on \(k/k_0\) is zero or injective. For an exchange factor the same argument kills the diagonal, with the quotient identified by \((a,b)\mapsto a+b\). Scaling both coordinates by any common scalar is possible by taking differing scalars in the two right corners. The induced map is again zero or injective.

### Additive, exchanged and Hermitian markings

When the polarization is zero, both maps are additive and land in \(\Gamma[2]\). For a Hermitian factor, \(\tau(b(x,y))\) factors through the field trace \(k\to k_0\). Subtract the common-reference function \(x\mapsto\iota(b(x,x))\), where \(\iota:k_0\to\Gamma[2]\) is the additive map induced from \(\tau\) via this trace. This function is invariant under the unitary group and has the required polarization. For an exchanged pair subtract \((p,q)\mapsto\iota(qp)\), where \(\iota\) is induced from \(\tau\) via the coordinate sum. This is invariant under \((p,q)\mapsto(Gp,qG^{-1})\). The remaining maps are additive in both cases.

Compare the additive maps by expanding their values in an \(\mathbb F_2\)-basis of the relevant elementary abelian group. Each resulting \(\mathbb F_2\)-functional on \(k^a\) is uniquely \(\operatorname{tr}_{k/\mathbb F_2}(b(v,\cdot))\) for a vector \(v\), since the trace pairing of the finite field is nondegenerate. An isometry comparison of the additive maps is therefore a comparison with ordered vector marks. On an exchanged pair restrict the additive maps to the two summands separately. The resulting conditions require an invertible \(G\) carrying ordered vectors and ordered covectors to their corresponding values. This is linear algebra: combine the desired vector constraints into a map on their span \(U\), and the covector constraints into a condition \(L'G=L\). Check consistency, that the prescribed map is an injection onto the target marked span, that the images of \(L,L'\) agree, and that the ranks and the marked-span restrictions match. Extend first on the kernels using the prescribed bases of their intersections with the marked spans. Extend next on lifts of the remaining basis of the common image. These checks and basis extensions are both necessary and sufficient.

### An alternating residue with nonzero trace marking

On a fixed symplectic component the underlying \(b\) is alternating. If \(\tau\ne0\), the maps \(\phi\) land in \(\Gamma[2]\) and their polar takes values in \(\tau(k)\cong_{\mathbb F_2}k\). Split this elementary abelian subgroup inside \(\Gamma[2]\). The complementary coordinates are additive vector markings. In the \(k\)-coordinate choose one genuine \(k\)-quadratic form \(q_0\) with polar \(b\), for example by writing the standard polynomial in a symplectic basis. The difference of each \(k\)-coordinate with \(q_0\) is \(\mathbb F_2\)-linear. Every such map \(k^a\to k\) is uniquely a sum of semilinear terms \(\sum_{j=0}^{e-1}\sum_i c_{ij}x_i^{2^j}\), where \(e=[k: \mathbb F_2]\). This follows from counting dimensions and from the usual bound on roots of a nonzero univariate polynomial of degree \(2^{e-1}<|k|\); the coefficients are computed by linear algebra. Under \(k\)-linear substitutions the terms stay in their respective semilinear spaces. The difference \(q_0\circ G-q_0\) for a symplectic \(G\) has zero polar and is homogeneous with the square scaling law. Thus the term with \(j\equiv1\bmod e\) incorporates that difference and gives one ordinary \(k\)-quadratic comparison with polar \(b\). All other terms give vector markings, by taking the inverse Frobenius on their values. This includes \(e=1\).

### A nonalternating residue with nonzero trace marking

For a fixed nonalternating symmetric \(b\) define its characteristic vector \(z\) by \(b(z,x)=\sqrt{b(x,x)}\). It is nonzero and every isometry fixes it. The norm \(b(z,z)\) is zero or one from its defining equation. If it is one, \(z^\perp\) is a nondegenerate alternating space. If it is zero, \(z^\perp\) is alternating with one-dimensional radical spanned by \(z\). This proves that the norm is one exactly in odd dimension.

In odd dimension compare \(\phi_1,\phi_h\) on the fixed line \(kz\) outright. Their difference is additive since the polars agree, so it suffices to check an \(\mathbb F_2\)-basis. On \(z^\perp\), both maps have values in \(\Gamma[2]\) because the restricted \(b\) is alternating. The preceding alternating-mark algorithm applies. Cross terms between the line and its perpendicular vanish, so these checks are equivalent to (3).

In even dimension append the same perpendicular unit-length line \(kz_0\) to both sides and require its basis vector \(z_0\) to be fixed. On it extend both maps by the common function \(a z_0\mapsto\phi_1(a u)\), using any \(u\) in the original space with \(b(u,u)=1\). Its polar is exactly \(\tau(ab)\) on the new line. The larger symmetric space has odd dimension. An isometry of it fixing \(z_0\) restricts to the original space, and any original isometry extends. In the odd-dimensional reduction the added vector constraint becomes a prescribed vector constraint in the alternating perpendicular after projection along its fixed characteristic vector. Thus this case, too, is a constructive ordinary symplectic or marked quadratic comparison. This argument handles the order-four values without dividing them by two.

### Constructive comparisons with vector marks

For clarity, the classical marked comparisons used here have direct deterministic algorithms. Convert frames of ordered vector marks into a proposed partial linear map, reject inconsistent dependencies, and test the prescribed map on the marked span. For bilinear or Hermitian forms this is a Gram matrix comparison. For quadratic forms check the quadratic values on a basis as well as the polar Gram matrix. A nondegenerate alternating or Hermitian form allows a nondegenerate summand of the marked space to be split off by elimination, leaving its bilinear radical. This radical is isotropic for the bilinear or Hermitian pairing. For each basis vector \(e\) of that radical, solve the linear equations for a partner \(f\) paired to \(e\) by one and paired to the other marked radical vectors by zero. In the Hermitian case make \(f\) isotropic by adding a multiple of \(e\) with the prescribed field trace. Peel off the resulting hyperbolic pair and continue. Unmarked alternating spaces have a standard symplectic basis. Unmarked Hermitian spaces have a diagonal basis, scaled to ones using the norm map of the quadratic field extension. To find a nonisotropic Hermitian vector, use a basis vector with nonzero value or combine two basis vectors with nonzero pairing using a scalar with prescribed trace. Norm equations \(a^\sigma a=c\) with \(c\) in the fixed field are solved by taking a square root in the fixed field. Nonalternating symmetric-bilinear comparisons reduce to the alternating case via the characteristic-vector decomposition, adding the same unit line in even dimension as above. In such even dimension a mark on the new line is equivalent to a nonzero vector mark on the alternating perpendicular. This also proves that all nonalternating nondegenerate symmetric forms over the same finite field of characteristic two and in the same dimension are isometric.

For a quadratic form with nondegenerate polar pairing, split the marked nondegenerate polar summand first. The restriction of the quadratic form to the remaining marked polar radical is Frobenius-linear, with its square root a \(k\)-linear functional. Choose a basis of that radical consisting of vectors of value zero, and at most one final vector of nonzero value \(a\). For each of the former vectors \(e\), take a polar partner \(f\) orthogonal to all the other marked radical vectors. Add \(q(f)e\) to \(f\) to make its value zero. Peel off this hyperbolic pair and continue. If a final marked vector \(e\) has value \(a\ne0\), take any polar partner \(f\). If the current space has dimension at least four, add a vector from the nondegenerate perpendicular of \(\langle e,f\rangle\) to give \(f\) any desired quadratic value. Such a perpendicular represents all field values: find any nonzero quadratic value by checking its basis and a paired sum, and scale by square roots. If the current space has dimension two, matching the quadratic values of the two partners is equivalent to solving \(a x^2+x=c\) on one side. This is an \(\mathbb F_2\)-linear equation. It is solvable exactly when the two planes have the same Arf bit \(\operatorname{tr}_{k/\mathbb F_2}(a q(f))\). For the remaining unmarked spaces one can split hyperbolic pairs while the dimension is at least four by choosing vectors of equal nonzero value from two orthogonal polar planes. Their sum is an isotropic vector. The final plane is compared by the same binary equation. The Arf bit on the original space is computed as the sum of the bits of its polar planes and is invariant: for a plane the sum of \((-1)^{\operatorname{tr} q(v)}\) over its vectors is \(|k|\) times the sign of that bit, and the sums multiply under orthogonal direct sums. Thus a matching choice of marked pairs leaves complements of matching type if and only if the entire spaces had matching type. These constructions extend every feasible partial isometry and also give exact failures. All work is linear algebra, field operations, Frobenius, or the solution of additive equations over \(\mathbb F_2\).

Together with the four residue cases, this constructs a solution of (3) or certifies its absence. No list of semisimple unitary elements is enumerated. The algebra dimensions, basis computations, quadratic coordinate lists, and vector markings have size polynomial in the written matrix data and \(t\). One can compute the value of each marking on a binary basis and its pairwise sums directly using any lifts in \(A\). The finite field degrees are at most the \(\mathbb F_2\)-dimension of \(S\). The only group orbit computation is (4). This proves the symmetric matrix-tuple congruence theorem over \(R\). For the stated bit bound, the modulus \(2^t\) is an ordinary integer modulus of bit length \(t+1\), and arithmetic has cost polynomial in \(t\).

## 4. A witness-preserving compiler for binary quadratic tuples

Write each input quadratic as \(q_i(v)=\sum_j a_{ij}v_j^2+\sum_{j<k}b_{ijk}v_jv_k\). Over \(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\) define a symmetric matrix \(M_i\) with diagonal entries \(2a_{ij}\) and with \(jk,kj\) entry \(b_{ijk}\) for \(j<k\). For all integral lifts,
\[
 v^T M_i v=2q_i(\bar v)\pmod 4,
\]
and \(M_i\bmod2\) is the polar matrix of \(q_i\).

Enlarge the free role \(V\) by \(m+2\) independent partner roles \(W_i\) of the same rank \(n\). For \(1\le i\le m\), the output symmetric matrix \(B_i\) has \(M_i\) in its \(V,V\) block and a mixed block \(2I\) in each of the \(V,W_i\) and \(W_i,V\) positions, with every other block zero. There are two additional ordered matrices \(B_{m+1},B_{m+2}\) having only their own mixed \(2I\) blocks. Use identical roles and marker matrices for the other input. The size is \(m+2\) matrices of order \((m+3)n\).

If an invertible matrix over \(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\) simultaneously transports the tuple, its reduction preserves each of the last two mixed forms after dividing their even entries by two. The radicals of those two reduced forms consist of the partner roles other than their respective partner. Their sum is precisely the full partner space \(\bigoplus_i\overline W_i\) since the two marked partners are distinct. Thus a congruence induces an invertible map \(S\) on the quotient \(\overline V\). The diagonal value of \(B_i\) is always \(2q_i(\bar v)\), independent of the partners, since the mixed \(2I\) terms contribute multiples of four. Reading this value after the map proves that \(S\) is a simultaneous quadratic isometry.

Conversely, let \(S\) satisfy \(q'_i(Sv)=q_i(v)\). Lift \(S\) to an invertible matrix \(\widetilde S\) over \(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\), and use its inverse transpose on each partner role. The mixed blocks are already matched. Before adding any shear, the discrepancy in the \(V,V\) block of matrix \(i\) is \(M_i-\widetilde S^T M'_i\widetilde S\). Each entry is even by equality of the polar forms, and each diagonal entry is zero since the quadratic values on basis vectors agree. It is therefore \(2A_i\) for an alternating symmetric binary matrix \(A_i\). Pick a strictly upper triangular \(U_i\) with \(U_i+U_i^T=A_i\). Add a shear \(C_i:V\to W_i\) to the lift, with \(S^T\bar C_i=U_i\). Its change to the \(i\)-th \(V,V\) block is exactly \(2(U_i+U_i^T)\). It affects no other matrix, and the two marker-partner shears are zero. The resulting block triangular map is invertible and gives the required congruence. The lift and decoder are polynomial time. Applying the main theorem at \(t=2\) proves GP014 for every ordered input.

## Verification and scope

The proof independently rederives the local Wall lifting route, corrects its missing relative-marking well-definedness test, makes the residue procedure constructive, and extends it to the order-four marks needed for the quadratic reduction. The radical transporter and finite-algebra algorithms are published dependencies. GP013 and GP014 as stated are covered in full; arbitrary changes of basis on a space of forms are a different problem. These are paper proofs.
