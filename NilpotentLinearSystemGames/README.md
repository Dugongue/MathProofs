# Nilpotent solutions of linear-system games

These AI-assisted written proofs concern solution-group homomorphisms into a specified group. Given a system $Ax=b$ over $\mathbb Z_d$, write $\Gamma(A,b)$ for the group with generators $J,x_1,\ldots,x_n$. All generators have order dividing $d$, $J$ is central, and variables occurring in a single equation commute pairwise. Each equation gives the relation $\prod_j x_j^{A_{ij}}=J^{b_i}$. For a fixed group $G$ and fixed central element $j$ of order $d$, write $\operatorname{Sol}(G,j)$ for the decision problem of whether there is a homomorphism $\Gamma(A,b)\to G$ sending $J$ to $j$.

## Classical solvability below the least prime divisor

**Theorem.** Let $\ell$ be the least prime divisor of $d\ge2$, and let $1\le c<\ell$. The order of $J$ in $\Gamma(A,b)/\gamma_{c+1}\Gamma(A,b)$ equals its order in the abelianization. If there is a homomorphism to a nilpotent group of class at most $c$ in which the image of $J$ has order $d$, then $Ax=b$ has a classical solution over $\mathbb Z_d$. In particular, existence of such a nilpotent solution is decidable by Smith normal form.

Any nilpotent image $H$ of $\Gamma(A,b)$ is finite, with prime divisors among those dividing $d$. Indeed the image is generated by finitely many elements of order dividing $d$. The torsion elements with prime factors dividing $d$ form a subgroup of a nilpotent group, and a finitely generated torsion nilpotent group is finite. A finite nilpotent group is a direct product of its Sylow groups. Under the class bound each Sylow $p$-group has class less than $p$, so Lazard's correspondence supplies a bijection $\log$ to a Lie ring. Apply it componentwise. It preserves order, sends powers to integer multiples, and sends a product of commuting elements to their additive sum, since the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff law on an abelian subring is addition.

For a homomorphism $\varphi$ with image $H$, put $y_i=\log\varphi(x_i)$ and $u=\log\varphi(J)$. We have $dy_i=du=0$, and the commutation relations within each equation give $\sum_j A_{ij}y_j=b_i u$. Sending $x_i$ to $y_i$ and $J$ to $u$ therefore defines a homomorphism from the abelianization to the additive group of the Lie ring. When $H$ is the class-$c$ quotient, this gives an image of $J$ with order equal to its order in that quotient. The abelianization is a quotient of the class-$c$ quotient, so the two orders are equal.

When $\varphi(J)$ has order $d$, the cyclic submodule generated by $u$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb Z_d$ in the finite $\mathbb Z_d$-module generated by $u$ and the $y_i$. Since $\mathbb Z_d$ is self-injective, the map sending $u$ to $1$ extends to a module homomorphism on the full module. Applying it to the $y_i$ gives a classical solution. Conversely, a classical solution gives a homomorphism to the additive group $\mathbb Z_d$ with $J\mapsto 1$.

## Fixed-template hardness from the magic square

**Theorem.** Let $d=2$. Suppose $G$ is finite or finitely generated nilpotent, $j$ is a central involution, and the Mermin–Peres magic square has a $G$-solution with $J\mapsto j$. Then $\operatorname{Sol}(G,j)$ is NP-complete.

The magic square has variables $a_{ij}$, with rows and columns imposing pairwise commutation and product $1$ except for column 3, which has product $J$. In its solution group, column 2, row 3, column 3, and row 2 give successively

$$
a_{22}=a_{12}a_{32}=a_{12}a_{31}a_{33}=a_{12}a_{31}Ja_{13}a_{23}=a_{12}a_{31}Ja_{13}a_{21}a_{22}.
$$

Cancel $a_{22}$ and substitute $a_{31}=a_{11}a_{21}$ and $a_{13}=a_{11}a_{12}$. As $a_{11}$ commutes with $a_{21}$, the result is $(a_{12}a_{21})^2=J$.

Use $G$ as the finite target if $G$ is finite. For a finitely generated nilpotent target, all images lie in its finite subgroup of elements of 2-power order. Use this finite subgroup as the target in that case. Make a graph on elements of square $1$ in the selected finite target, with an ordered edge $(g,h)$ when some square solution gives $a_{12}=g$ and $a_{21}=h$. The identity just proved excludes loops. Transposition followed by multiplication of $a_{33}$ by $j$ reverses an edge, so the graph is undirected.

For a solution with entries $g=a_{12}$, $h=a_{21}$ and $k=a_{33}$, the graph also contains edges between $g$ and $k$, and between $k$ and $h$. For the first, interchange rows 2 and 3 and columns 1 and 3, then multiply the new entries $a_{31},a_{33}$ by $j$. This moves the odd column back to column 3 and places $g,k$ in positions $(1,2),(2,1)$. For the second, interchange rows 1 and 3 and columns 2 and 3, then multiply $a_{32},a_{33}$ by $j$. This restores the odd column and places $k,h$ at those positions. Since the graph has no loops, these edges give a triangle.

By Hell and Nešetřil's graph-homomorphism dichotomy, homomorphism to this fixed finite loopless nonbipartite graph is NP-complete. Introduce one variable for each vertex of the input graph. For each input edge introduce a fresh magic square, using its two endpoint variables at positions $(1,2),(2,1)$ and seven fresh variables elsewhere. Assignments restrict to target edges on each square. Conversely a graph homomorphism supplies an admissible ordered pair for each square, which extends within the square by definition of the graph. Copies share only the vertex variables, so these local assignments combine. This proves hardness. A certificate is an assignment from the fixed finite target, and all commutation and product constraints can be checked in polynomial time.

For every fixed $N\ge2$, the $N$-qubit Pauli group with $j=-I$ contains the two-qubit magic-square solution. For example, the array below uses the usual one-qubit Pauli matrices, juxtaposition for tensor products, and $II$ for the two-qubit identity.

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
XI & IX & XX\\
IZ & ZI & ZZ\\
XZ & ZX & YY
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Each row and column is commuting, all products are $II$ except the last column whose product is $-II$. This proves the fixed-$N$ NP-completeness claim. For $N=1$, non-scalar involutions in each commuting equation all have the same Pauli letter up to signs. Because the product of the equation is scalar, that letter occurs an even number of times and the sign bits solve the classical system. This gives a polynomial-time algorithm for one qubit.
