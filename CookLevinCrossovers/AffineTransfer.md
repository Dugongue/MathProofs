# Quantitative Cook–Levin for smooth affine point counting

The block decomposition of Strassle and Subercaseaux gives a linear number of Boolean coordinates by allowing constraints to depend on whole computation epochs. Combining it with exact affine smoothing gives linear ambient dimension and linear hypersurface degree over every fixed global field. An explicit additive-polynomial construction supplies finite-field versions, including simultaneous preservation over several extension fields. A separate circuit presentation gives degree two and bounded incidence. All statements below have informal proofs.

## 1. One linear-degree smooth hypersurface for bounded-time computation

**Theorem 1.** Fix a global field or finite field \(k\), and a multitape nondeterministic machine \(M\). Given an input \(x\), a time bound \(t\ge |x|\) on all branches, and a block size \(1\le b\le t\), one can construct a smooth geometrically integral hypersurface \(H_x\) over \(k\) with \(O(t)\) ambient coordinates and degree \(O_k(t)\). Its \(k\)-points are in explicit bijection with the accepting computation paths of \(M\) on \(x\). The defining polynomial is supplied by a straight-line circuit. The construction time and circuit length are at most \(2^{O(t/b+b)}\operatorname{poly}(t)\); choosing \(b=\lceil\sqrt t\rceil\) gives \(2^{O(\sqrt t)}\operatorname{poly}(t)\). The constants may depend on \(M\) and \(k\).

In the alternative output format with a separate hypersurface for each head pattern, there are \(2^{O(t/b)}\) hypersurfaces, each with a defining circuit of length \(2^{O(b)}\operatorname{poly}(t)\). Accepting paths biject with the disjoint union of their \(k\)-points. Each hypersurface still has \(O(t)\) ambient coordinates and degree \(O_k(t)\).

**The block reduction.** Use the epoch decomposition and last-visit substitution of [Strassle and Subercaseaux, Lemma 2.1 and §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.11832v1) in the following direct window variant. Pad the computation to a multiple of \(b\) with deterministic absorbing steps. Split each tape into blocks of \(b\) cells. Guess the block occupied by each head at the start of each epoch, beginning at the actual initial blocks and giving each subsequent block by a relative shift in \(\{-1,0,1\}\). In \(b\) steps a head can visit only the starting block and its two neighbors. There are therefore \(2^{O(t/b)}\) head patterns. An epoch predicate gives the starting contents of the three-block window on each tape, the state and head offsets, the transition codes, and the output contents of each of the same three blocks, together with the ending state and head data. It verifies the \(b\) steps within the windows and checks that the final head positions agree with the shifts guessed for the next epoch, or checks acceptance in the last epoch.

For every block use its initial contents the first time its window is visited and otherwise substitute its output from the last epoch whose window contains it. Each output block is thus consumed by at most one next epoch. Starting state and normalized head offsets come from the previous epoch or are initially fixed. The resulting instance has \(O(t)\) variables and \(O(t/b)\) predicates on \(O(b)\) bits, with each variable in at most two predicates. A local predicate is computed by an \(O(b)\)-time multitape verifier: it can initialize the windows, put the heads in the supplied offsets, simulate the prescribed sequence, then compare each output window, all with a constant number of linear passes. Applying the time-to-circuit simulation cited as Lemma 3.3 of the paper gives a predicate circuit of size \(O(b\log(b+1))\). The truth-table construction below can also use the local verifier directly.

For path counting, label the original outgoing transitions distinctly, fix a binary code for each transition, reject invalid codes, and require the unique zero code whenever there is no branching and after halting. Every path specifies unique head patterns, tapes, offsets, states and codes. Conversely the initial data and the transition codes recover the computation step by step, since every epoch in the guessed pattern sees the correct initial contents of its entire window. This gives a bijection between paths and satisfying assignments across the patterns, explicitly implementing the counting convention noted in Remark 1.4 of the cited paper. The direct windows avoid a separate nondeterministic simulation step.

For a Boolean predicate \(C\) with \(r\) inputs use the polynomial
\[
  G_C(X)=\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{r}:C(a)=0}\ \prod_{i:a_i=1} X_i\prod_{i:a_i=0}(1-X_i).
\]
It vanishes on exactly the satisfying Boolean words, has degree at most \(r\), and has a circuit constructible in time \(2^{O(r)}\operatorname{poly}(|C|)\). Add the Booleanity equations \(X_i(X_i-1)=0\) for all coordinates. Pad all patterns to a common number of coordinates with zero constraints for the excess coordinates. Add a single designated coordinate required to be zero in every pattern; this adds just one coordinate and makes the final system nonconstant even for a computation with no genuine choices.

**Weighted norm compression.** Every fixed field under consideration has a separable quadratic extension \(L/k\). For global fields this follows by lifting a monic separable irreducible quadratic from a finite residue field. Take \(\theta\in L\setminus k\). The form \(N(U,V)=\operatorname{Norm}_{L/k}(U+\theta V)\) is quadratic and has only the trivial zero on \(k^2\). For a list of polynomials \(g_i\) with degree bounds \(d_i\ge1\), set \(D=\sum_i d_i\). Choose a binary prefix tree with code lengths \(\lceil\log_2(D/d_i)\rceil\), which exists since the sum of their inverse powers of two is at most one. Delete unused branches and contract unary nodes. Put \(g_i\) at the leaves and apply \(N\) at each internal node. This costs \(O(\#\{i\})\) extra operations, has the simultaneous zero set over \(k\), and has degree at most \(2D\). In fact a term from a leaf at depth \(h\) contributes degree at most \(2^h d_i\) to this estimate. The resulting polynomial is nonconstant if at least one leaf polynomial is nonconstant. Over \(L[X]\), a norm that is a nonzero constant is a product of two units, so both norm arguments were constant. A zero norm polynomial forces both arguments to vanish, by the same factorization and linear independence of \(1,\theta\) over \(k\).

Apply this to the equations of a pattern. Their total degree bound is \(O(t)\), since there are \(O(t/b)\) epoch predicates of degree \(O(b)\) and \(O(t)\) bit equations. To make one equation for all patterns, code the pattern by \(r=O(t/b)\) Boolean selector coordinates \(Y\). For a selector word \(a\) let \(L_a(Y)=\prod_{i:a_i=1}Y_i\prod_{i:a_i=0}(1-Y_i)\). Write \(F_a\) for the compressed polynomial for pattern \(a\), and \(F_a=1\) for an unused word. Put \(G=\sum_a L_a F_a\). Combine \(G\), the selector Booleanity equations, and the designated zero-coordinate equation by weighted norm. The resulting polynomial \(F\) is nonconstant, has degree \(O(t)\), and describes precisely the unique path encodings over \(k\). The combined circuit size and construction time are bounded as claimed.

**Exact geometric smoothing.** For finite fields, apply Theorem 2 below with the degree bound already established. For a fixed global field, the exact smoothing construction from the Top 30 affine scheme route gives a fixed plane polynomial \(Q(T,Z)\) over \(k\) with the following property: for every nonconstant \(F\in k[X_1,\ldots,X_m]\), \(Q(F(X),Z)=0\) defines a smooth geometrically integral hypersurface whose \(k\)-points project bijectively to \(F=0\). Its degree is \(O_k(\deg F+1)\) and it costs \(O_k(1)\) additional arithmetic gates.

Here is the geometric reason for the fixed substitution. Start from an effective smooth projective geometrically integral curve over \(k\) with one \(k\)-point \(P\), using [Poonen](https://arxiv.org/abs/0902.3965). Choose a finite function \(T\) with geometrically reduced fibers over zero and infinity and \(T(P)=0\). To do this, choose one section of a sufficiently positive line bundle vanishing simply at \(P\) and with reduced remaining zero divisor, and a second with reduced zero divisor disjoint from it, then take their ratio. Such choices exist by very ampleness and the infinitude of \(k\). In the finite free \(k[T]\)-algebra of the affine curve \(T\ne\infty\), choose a function whose values at the geometric points over 0 are all different. Riemann–Roch gives surjectivity onto that fiber for a sufficiently large pole bound. Its monic characteristic polynomial \(P_0(T,Y)\) is a minimal polynomial, since its specialized roots over zero are distinct. Its discriminant \(\Delta(T)\) is nonzero at zero. After inverting \(\Delta\), this equation defines the given affine curve by normality of a finite étale algebra over the smooth base and equality of fraction fields. To impose that open condition inside one equation, if \(d=\deg_Y P_0\), take \(Q(T,Z)=\Delta(T)^dP_0(T,Z+1/\Delta(T))\). This is a polynomial congruent to 1 modulo \(\Delta\).

The pullback to \(T=F(X)\) is étale over the open where \(\Delta(F)\ne0\), so is smooth, and every \(k\)-point maps to \(P\). Conversely, over \(F=0\) the point \(P\) supplies a unique \(k\)-point in the added coordinate. For geometric integrality, change spatial linear coordinates over the algebraic closure so the highest \(X_1\)-coefficient of \(F\), in degree \(\deg F\), is a scalar unit. At any simple geometric pole of \(T\) on the base curve, take its discrete valuation and the Gauss extension in \(X_2,\ldots,X_m\). The polynomial obtained by reversing \(F(X)-T\) in \(X_1\) and multiplying by \(T^{-1}\) is Eisenstein. Its leading coefficient has valuation zero, nonleading coefficients have positive valuation, and its constant coefficient has valuation one. Thus the pullback is generically integral over the coordinate ring of the curve with the remaining spatial coordinates, and is integral because the relation is monic up to a unit in \(X_1\). The bound follows since \(Q\) is fixed for \(k\).

**Transfer consequence.** If a point-counting algorithm for these linear-degree smooth hypersurfaces cost \(2^{O(m/s(m))}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) on \(m\) ambient coordinates and circuit length \(L\), it would count accepting paths of every machine in time \(2^{O(\sqrt t+t/s(t))}\operatorname{poly}(t)\), assuming \(s\) is nondecreasing. For the bound at argument \(t\), pad the Boolean coordinates by forced-zero variables before smoothing so that the ambient count lies between \(t\) and \(O(t)\). The proof uses the one-hypersurface version and the degree remains linear. This is a transfer to a geometrically restricted oracle, with the extent of improvement determined by that oracle.

## 2. Explicit finite-field smoothing and finite zeta prefixes

**Theorem 2.** Let \(k=\mathbb F_q\), fix a nonempty finite set \(S\) of positive integers, and let \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^m_k\), \(m\ge1\), be defined by a finite list of equations \(g_i=0\). There is a smooth geometrically integral hypersurface \(H\subseteq\mathbb A^{m+1}_k\) with coordinate projection inducing a bijection
\[
H(\mathbb F_{q^r})\longrightarrow X(\mathbb F_{q^r})\qquad(r\in S).
\]
The inverse maps every tuple to that tuple with last coordinate zero. The polynomial defining \(H\) has last-coordinate derivative 1. If input circuits and positive degree upper bounds \(d_i\) are supplied, its degree is \(O_{k,S}(1+\sum_i d_i)\) and its circuit size is \(O_{k,S}(1+\sum_i |g_i|+\log(1+\sum_i d_i))\), with \(|g_i|\) the circuit description length and with at least one unit cost per supplied polynomial. The construction is also uniform as the extension degrees vary: for \(D=\deg\operatorname{lcm}_{r\in S}(W^r-1)\) over \(k\), the degree bound is \(O(q^{2D}(1+\sum_i d_i))\), and the additional circuit size is polynomial in the number of input equations, \(D\), \(\log q\) and \(\log(1+\sum_i d_i)\). Computing the least common multiple takes time polynomial in \(\sum_{r\in S}r\) and the bit size of the field presentation. In particular, taking \(S=\{1,\ldots,R\}\) preserves the finite zeta prefix through degree \(R\). For a single input polynomial it is enough simply to preserve that polynomial's zero set in the padding step.

**Proof.** Write \(P(W)=\operatorname{lcm}_{r\in S}(W^r-1)=\sum_{i=0}^D c_i W^i\). Its constant term \(c_0\) is nonzero. Let \(L(U)=c_0^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^D c_i U^{q^i}\). It has derivative 1 and degree \(M=q^D\). Its root space in an algebraic closure contains each \(\mathbb F_{q^r}\): viewing \(P\) as an operator in the \(q\)-Frobenius, it is a multiple of the operator \(W^r-1\) which kills that field. In fact its root space is the sum \(V=\sum_{r\in S}\mathbb F_{q^r}\). For two Frobenius polynomials with nonzero constant terms the kernel intersection is the kernel of their greatest common divisor by Bézout's identity. The kernel dimension of each polynomial, including their least common multiple, equals its ordinary \(W\)-degree because the corresponding linearized polynomial is separable. Hence the kernel sum and the kernel of the least common multiple have the same dimension, giving the claim by induction. The function \(J(U)=1-L(U)/U\) is polynomial, is zero at zero, and is one on \(V\setminus\{0\}\).

Let \(g(X)=1-\prod_i (1-J(g_i(X)))\). This is zero exactly at \(X(\mathbb F_{q^r})\) on the fields under consideration. Let \(B\) be a known degree upper bound for \(g\), such as \((M-1)\sum_i d_i\). Set
\[
f(X)=g(X)+X_1^jL(X_1).
\]
Choose the least \(j\ge0\) making the degree \(e\) of the second term greater than \(B\) and prime to \(p=\operatorname{char}k\). The added term vanishes over every chosen extension. Repeated squaring constructs \(X_1^j\) with \(O(\log(j+1))\) gates. Choose a fixed integer \(a\ge2\) prime to \(p\). Define
\[
H(X,Z)=\frac{L(f(X)Z)}{f(X)}+f(X)^{a-1}.
\]
The quotient is a polynomial by termwise expansion. The derivative in \(Z\) is 1. Where \(f=0\) the equation is \(Z=0\), since the normalized linear term of \(L\) is \(U\). Over a chosen field where \(f\ne0\), the first term evaluates to zero, and the second cannot vanish.

For geometric integrality, localize at \(f\) and set \(U=fZ\). This gives the polynomial \(L(U)+f^a\). The degree valuation on \(\bar k(X)\) satisfies \(v(f^a)=-ae\), prime to \(p\). Any root \(u\) in a valued algebraic extension has negative valuation, since otherwise \(L(u)\) could not have negative valuation. Then the unique lowest term of \(L(u)\) is its leading term, so \(M v(u)=-ae\). The valuation denominator in lowest terms is \(M\). The ramification index, and hence the extension degree of \(\bar k(X)(u)\), is at least \(M\). Thus \(L(U)+f^a\) is irreducible over \(\bar k(X)\), and its nonzero scalar leading coefficient makes the localized hypersurface integral. Any component of \(H=0\) contained in \(f=0\) would be contained in \(f=Z=0\), which has dimension at most \(m-1\). Hypersurface components have dimension \(m\). Smoothness already gives reducedness, so the dense integral open proves geometric integrality.

For the uniform bounds, choose \(a=2\) for odd \(p\) and \(a=3\) for \(p=2\). Then \(e\le \max(B+1,M)+p\) and the degree of \(H\) is at most \(\max((M-1)e+M,(a-1)e)\). Each list \(U^{q^i}\) uses \(O(D\log q)\) gates by repeated \(q\)-th powering. The list \(U^{q^i-1}\) has the same asymptotic cost, using \(U^{q^{i+1}-1}=(U^{q^i-1})^q U^{q-1}\). This handles \(L\), \(J\), and the expanded quotient in \(H\). The zeta coefficient conclusion follows by expanding \(\exp(\sum_{r\ge1}\#X(\mathbb F_{q^r})T^r/r)\) modulo \(T^{R+1}\).

**Theorem 3.** Every prescribed subset of \(\mathbb F_q^m\), \(m\ge1\), is projected bijectively in this manner from a smooth geometrically integral hypersurface of degree \(O_q(m)\), with last-coordinate derivative 1. For \(q=2\) and \(m\ge3\) the degree can be at most \(m+2\). If the prescribed set has odd cardinality, \(m+2\) is minimal among such derivative-one equations with all lifted last coordinates zero.

**Proof.** The \(\{0,1\}\)-valued rejection function is represented by a polynomial of total degree at most \((q-1)m\), by the indicators \(\prod_i(1-(X_i-a_i)^{q-1})\) of individual tuples \(a\). Apply the single-polynomial version of Theorem 2. Over \(\mathbb F_2\) let \(A\) be the unique multilinear polynomial for the rejection function. If \(A=0\), take \(H=Z\). If \(\deg A\) is odd, set \(G=A\). If \(A\ne0\) has even degree, set \(G=A+h\) for \(h=X_1(X_1^2-X_1)\). In either case \(G=A\) on all binary inputs and \(\deg G\le m\). Take
\[
H=Z+A+GZ^2.
\]
On binary inputs \(Z=0\) gives \(A\) and \(Z=1\) gives 1. The derivative is 1. On \(G\ne0\) set \(U=GZ\) and then replace \(U\) by \(U+A\). The resulting equation is \(U^2+U=A\) when \(G=A\), and \(U^2+U=(h+1)A\) when \(G=A+h\). Both right sides have positive odd degree, so the previous pole valuation proves irreducibility. Where \(G=0\) we have \(Z=A\), a locus of dimension at most \(m-1\), so the whole hypersurface is geometrically integral.

For optimality in the derivative-one normalization, write any such equation as \(Z+A_0(X)+\sum_{j\ge1}B_j(X)Z^{2j}\). On binary inputs \(A_0\) is the rejection function, and \(1+A_0+\sum_j B_j=1\) because no allowed lift has \(Z=1\). Thus \(\sum_j B_j\) is the rejection function as well. If the accepted set has odd cardinality, the rejected set has odd cardinality, and the coefficient of \(X_1\cdots X_m\) in the multilinear rejection function is one. Reducing any polynomial modulo \(X_i^2-X_i\) never increases degree. Therefore some \(B_j\) has degree at least \(m\), giving total degree at least \(m+2\). For general \(q\), linear growth in \(m\) is necessary for a worst-case subset even without the derivative-one constraint: a hypersurface with a single rational point has degree at least \(m+1\) by Chevalley–Warning.

## 3. Exact bounded-incidence smooth intersections of quadrics

**Theorem 4.** Fix any global field or finite field \(k\). A list of equations in \(m\ge1\) variables, given by arithmetic circuits, admits a deterministic polynomial-time reduction to a smooth geometrically integral affine complete intersection over \(k\) of dimension \(m\). Its equations have degree at most two, use at most three variables each, and each variable appears in at most three equations. The number of variables and equations is linear in the input circuit size plus \(m\), and projection onto the original coordinates induces a bijection on \(k\)-points. For \(k=\mathbb Q\) one may also require coefficients in \(\{0,1,-1\}\), with a linear bound on variables and equations when rational constants are charged by their bit lengths. Thus the rational-point decision problem, in particular Hilbert's tenth problem over \(\mathbb Q\), is polynomial-time many-one equivalent to the problem on these bounded-incidence smooth complete intersections, in the circuit representation of input systems.

A Boolean circuit is a special case with coefficients from a fixed finite set, and gives the \(O_k(m+s)\) bound for \(s\) Boolean gates. In particular, exact point counting in this class is #P-complete over every fixed finite field under parsimonious reductions.

**Proof.** First compress the input equations by the quadratic norm tree. Call the resulting polynomial \(F\), using \(F=0\) for an empty list. The tree uses a linear number of gates. Over a finite field apply the single-polynomial case of Theorem 2. A syntactic degree bound for a fan-in-two arithmetic circuit with \(c\) gates is \(2^c\), so the degree padding uses only \(O_k(c)\) gates even when this bound is exponential.

For global fields, modify the fixed smoothing polynomial \(Q\) of Section 1 to avoid testing whether a circuit represents a constant. Let \(\beta\in k\) be the unique coordinate of its \(k\)-point over \(T=0\). Its derivative \(Q_Z(0,\beta)\) is nonzero. Set \(Q_1(T,Z)=Q(T,TZ+\beta)/T\). This is a polynomial and \(Q_1(0,Z)\) is affine linear with nonzero slope \(Q_Z(0,\beta)\). Replace \(F\) by \(f(X)=F(X)N(X_1,1)\) using the same quadratic norm. Since the norm factor is nowhere zero on \(k\)-points, the arithmetic zero set is unchanged. Since the factor is a degree-two polynomial, \(f\) is either identically zero or nonconstant. Output \(Q_1(f(X),Z)=0\), with no test for which case occurred. If \(f=0\) identically, this is a hyperplane. Otherwise, on \(f\ne0\) it is isomorphic to the prior smooth integral pullback, through \(Z\mapsto fZ+\beta\), and has no \(k\)-points. Over \(f=0\) its \(Z\)-derivative is a unit, there is one lift of every \(k\)-point, and this locus has dimension at most \(m-1\). Thus no geometric component is lost by localizing at \(f\), so the whole hypersurface is both smooth and geometrically integral. This calculation costs only a fixed number of operations after compression and keeps the degree linear in any supplied degree bound for the compressed polynomial.

Represent each arithmetic gate by an output variable and its defining equation. Addition and multiplication each give an equation with at most three variables and degree at most two. Hardwired constants and negation use fewer variables. Before writing equations, replace every fanout of size greater than two by a directed binary copy tree. A copy uses a new variable defined by one linear equality. The sum of all fanouts was linear in the number of gates, so this adds only linearly many gates. Treat the final condition that the circuit output is zero as one consumer. Each variable now appears in at most its defining equation and two consumer equations. The only coefficients outside a fixed \(k\)-dependent finite set come from constants in the input circuits.

For rational constants in the \(\mathbb Q\) case, synthesize the numerator and positive denominator by binary integer arithmetic using \(0\), \(1\), additions and negations. Write \(B C=A\) as the defining equation for the output \(C=A/B\). The denominator \(B\) becomes a nonzero rational constant once its defining equations are eliminated. Use distinct copy variables for any input read twice in one gate, so combining repeated terms never produces a coefficient 2. The extra gates stay linear in the total bit lengths, and all coefficients are now in \(\{0,1,-1\}\).

All defining equations for gate and copy variables eliminate their own outputs in a topological ordering. They are monic linear up to a unit, which in the rational constant case is the nonzero integer denominator after prior elimination. The quotient ring prior to the final zero condition is a polynomial ring on the original \(m\) inputs and the smoothing coordinate. The final equation is nonzero in that ring and its quotient is exactly the smooth hypersurface ring. These equations therefore form a regular sequence and the constructed scheme is a smooth geometrically integral complete intersection of dimension \(m\). Each auxiliary value is unique on rational points. The reverse decision reduction simply reads the output system as an ordinary input.

For the Boolean application normalize to fan-in two, arithmetize AND by multiplication and NOT by \(1-X\), and include the equations \(1-C(X)=0\) and \(X_i(X_i-1)=0\), where \(C\) denotes the resulting circuit polynomial. This has linear size. Over fixed finite \(k\), guessing a coordinate vector and checking the equations gives a counting-machine verifier with one legal code per tuple, proving #P membership on the promise class. Boolean circuit counting gives parsimonious hardness.

Applied pattern by pattern to the blocked Cook–Levin output, this also gives \(2^{O(t/b)}\) such complete intersections with \(O_k(t\log(b+1))\) variables and equations each, of dimension \(O(t)\), in construction time \(2^{O(t/b)}\operatorname{poly}(t)\). In this tradeoff each epoch is arithmetized directly as a circuit, without expanding its truth table. The disjoint union of point sets again encodes accepting paths.

## Scope and dependencies

The block decomposition, circuit-CSP simulation, and the possibility of path counting appear in [Strassle and Subercaseaux](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11832). They are inputs here. The improvements are the linear-degree and linear-dimension geometric model, the explicit finite-field projection result and its degree bound, and the simultaneous smoothness, geometric integrality and bounded incidence of the quadratic model. The global-field construction uses the exact affine smoothing route, whose underlying singleton existence theorem is [Poonen](https://arxiv.org/abs/0902.3965). Classical [Bertini over finite fields](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2004/160-3/p06) supplies related existence results with local conditions; the additive equations above give explicit degree and circuit bounds. All hypersurfaces and intersections here are affine. Exact checks covered the projection identity over \(\mathbb F_2,\mathbb F_3,\mathbb F_4,\mathbb F_5,\mathbb F_7,\mathbb F_8\), the simultaneous \(\mathbb F_4+\mathbb F_8\) root space over \(\mathbb F_2\), and every Boolean truth table through dimension three. The proofs and priority are subject to independent review.
