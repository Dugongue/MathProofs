# Unordered local coordinates from finite algebras

A Transportensor is a directed graph with thresholds and edge identities, vector-space modes at each vertex, named multilinear operations at the vertices and linear mode maps along the edges. An isomorphism simultaneously preserves the graph, thresholds, tensors and transports. The two inputs can have completely different bases, including at corresponding vertices. An Equitransportensor also has a costed groupoid acting on this structure. In algorithmic claims we use a fixed finite field k of order q, fixed named sorts and bounded total arity, explicit tensors and explicit groupoid tables and actions when present. Let N be the total input length. All proofs in this packet are informal.

**Theorem.** Suppose each positive-dimensional vertex-mode space has named operations making it a finite commutative unital k-algebra. In its product decomposition into local rings impose the following promise on *each* factor A separately: the cardinality of its radical J is at most N^c for a fixed c. Then isomorphism, with the graph identification unknown, reduces to one ordinary Graph Isomorphism instance in deterministic polynomial time. For reduced algebras the promise is automatic. The residue-field degrees, the number of local factors, and the dimension of the full algebra need no constant bound. Additional tensors and singular edge maps are arbitrary. The result extends to explicit groupoid actions with unknown groupoid correspondence. On this class the decision problem is GI-complete under polynomial many-one reductions.

### A general criterion

Construct polynomial-size sets P of actual vectors and D of actual covectors spanning each space and its dual. The sets may be unordered, overcomplete and permuted nontrivially by automorphisms. Require that every isomorphism between the two inputs carry their sets onto one another, acting contragrediently on D. It suffices to construct the sets using shared auxiliary choices for the pair. It does not suffice to choose an arbitrary basis independently on each side.

Include one graph-sort object for every vertex, edge, vector label and covector label, with typing and incidence. Record every pairing d(x). For each operation from an input tensor product to an output tensor product, evaluate it on each ordered tuple of input P labels and pair its output with each ordered tuple of output D labels. Store the resulting scalar with its relation name and argument order. Repeated modes allow independently varying test points. Empty products use the field and its unit. For each edge and mode record the corresponding evaluations on its tail P and head D. Fixed arity bounds the number of records polynomially. In the groupoid case also encode base objects and arrows, domains, codomains, composition, costs, and graph actions. Encode the action maps on modes by the same rectangular evaluation rule. All combinatorial data are explicit, of bounded relational arity and with polynomial-length colors.

To see why this works, let a relational isomorphism induce bijections f on P and g on D. If a linear combination of source labels vanishes, pairing its proposed image with every g(d) gives zero. Since D' spans the target dual, the proposed image vanishes. Thus f extends linearly to a map. Its inverse extends by the same argument. The map on D is now forced by the pairing. All operation and edge equations hold because they have been checked on spanning input tuples and separating output covectors. Conversely every original isomorphism preserves every recorded evaluation. Use one label per distinct vector or covector per sort to get a bijection on witnesses at the relational level. The standard incidence construction encodes this colored relational isomorphism test as GI by tuple and position vertices; the two inputs use common color numbering. Colors can be removed by ordinary polynomial-size rigid tags. No graph correspondence has been guessed.

### Extracting the two spanning sets from the multiplication tensors

Check the algebra axioms by structure constants (a missing distinguished unit can equivalently be found by solving linear equations). A finite-dimensional commutative algebra is a product of local Artin algebras. Here this decomposition is computable without enumerating its elements. Compute the k-linear q-power Frobenius on a basis. Its fixed algebra is a product of copies of k, one per local factor: in a local factor every root of x^q=x reduces to a root in the residue field, and its lift is unique since the difference of two lifts would be a nilpotent fixed by Frobenius. Scalars give all q lifts. On the fixed algebra evaluate a basis one element at a time. Split each current idempotent by its fibers at the q possible values using the indicator polynomial 1−(x−a)^(q−1). Drop empty fibers. A basis distinguishes distinct coordinate projections, so the final nonzero idempotents are the primitive ones. This uses fixed q and polynomially many vector-space operations.

On each resulting A the stable kernel of Frobenius is its nilradical J, and its stable image K maps isomorphically to its residue field: Frobenius is bijective on the finite residue field and eventually kills J. Thus A=K⊕J canonically as k-spaces. Iterate to stable rank, computing powers of the linear map. If |J| exceeds the stated bound, this factor fails the promise. Otherwise enumerate J and its k-dual. Both sets have polynomial cardinality. Add them to the vector and covector labels, supported on this factor alone and extended by zero on complementary summands for covectors.

To handle arbitrarily large fields K without listing their elements, group them by degree e. For each degree use a single reference field F taken from either input, with any fixed k-basis and its dual. For a target factor field K of that degree compute all e maps F→K. One explicit method forms the algebra F⊗_k K of dimension e². It splits into e copies of K by separability and the e Frobenius embeddings. Compute its primitive idempotents by exactly the fixed-algebra procedure above. For each primitive idempotent p solve p(x⊗1)=p(1⊗y) for y in K as x ranges over a basis of F. In this factor 1⊗K is identified with the whole factor, so the solutions exist uniquely. They describe one embedding; the e idempotents give them all. Send the reference basis and dual along all embeddings, the latter by inverse pullback. Support the resulting labels on their local summand and deduplicate. There are at most e² primal and e² dual labels per field summand. Products with very different residue degrees need no common splitting field.

Isomorphisms permute primitive factors, preserve both Frobenius summands, and permute the full lists of reference-field embeddings. They therefore preserve the two unordered sets on every mode. The criterion proves the upper bound. For the lower bound, put k with its usual multiplication at every vertex of an ordinary graph and use identity transports. The only unital k-algebra automorphism of k is the identity, so isomorphism is exactly the original GI test.

### Why unordered coordinates matter

With one vertex and local algebra k^m, permuting the m primitive factors gives the full symmetric group. Every individually invariant vector is constant on the factors, so those vectors span at most one dimension. Yet the unordered set of primitive idempotents is a basis and is recovered by the algorithm. One further arbitrary unary tensor on k^m amounts exactly to a matrix under simultaneous row/column permutations. Independent linear basis changes in the raw input are allowed. More generally, products of many dual-number rings pass the radical bound factorwise even when the radical of their product is exponentially large as a set. The theorem requires a *named* algebra structure; discovering a suitable hidden multiplication on an arbitrary tensor is a different problem.

Pairing-based GI reductions have precedents. [Bardet, Otmani and Saeed-Taha](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00073) give a GI reduction for permutation code equivalence with trivial hull. The present proof uses two spanning sets extracted from supplied algebra tensors. No novelty claim is made for a general pairing trick or for the elementary finite-algebra decomposition.
