# Automorphism maxima of simple evolution algebras over every field

For every field $F$ with at least three elements, the largest order of the automorphism group of an $n$-dimensional simple evolution algebra over $F$ is
$$
\max\bigl(n!,\,n|\mu_{2^n-1}(F)|\bigr).
$$
Here $\mu_t(F)=\{x\in F^\times\mid x^t=1\}$. Over $\mathbb F_2$ the maximum is $n!$ for even $n$, $1$ for $n=1$, $3$ for $n=3$, and $(n-1)!$ for odd $n\ge5$.

We also identify all maximizing types over every field. Over $\mathbb C$ the order formula is Conjecture 4 of Kaygorodov, Martín González and Viruel, already proved and formalized by trureturing. Their resolution explicitly leaves equality cases and optimal attainment over other fields open in that work. The argument uses their joint control idea via the ordinary period of a strongly connected digraph and retains the precise root-of-unity count. [Conjecture 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22248v1#S2.SS3) · [Prior Lean resolution](https://www.trureturing.org/research/kmvc4-simpleaut/)

## 1. The period bounds the two parts of the same group

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ be a natural basis, so $e_i e_j=0$ for $i\ne j$. Write $M_{ji}$ for the coefficient of $e_j$ in $e_i^2$. Simplicity makes the span of the squares the whole algebra, since that span is a nonzero ideal. Thus $M$ is invertible. The support digraph has $i\to j$ for $M_{ji}\ne 0$. The span of the vertices reachable from $i$ is a nonzero ideal, so the support is strongly connected. Conversely, invertibility and strong connectivity imply simplicity. From a nonzero vector of an ideal, multiply by a basis vector of nonzero coordinate to obtain its nonzero square. Repeat along paths to obtain all squares, which form a basis.

An automorphism is monomial in the natural basis. For any two distinct source vectors, their images have zero product. The independence of the squares forces their coordinate supports to be disjoint, and invertibility then gives one nonzero entry per image. Its permutation preserves the support digraph. A diagonal automorphism with scales $t_i$ satisfies $t_j=t_i^2$ along every arc $i\to j$. [Elduque–Labra, evolution algebras and graphs](https://www.math.uci.edu/~brusso/ElduqueAutoGraph.pdf)

Fix a root and let $d$ be the gcd of the positive lengths of all its closed directed walks. The residue modulo $d$ of the length of a path from the root to a vertex is well-defined, since one can append a return path to compare any two lengths. Here paths used in this argument may revisit vertices. Every arc advances the residue by one, and all $d$ residue classes occur along a closed walk. Every graph automorphism rotates the classes by one common shift, as is seen by applying it to the paths from the root. An invertible $M$ has a nonzero determinant term and hence a supported permutation. Such a permutation sends each class into the next class bijectively, so $n=dh$ and the classes all have size $h$. The permutation image of the automorphism group therefore has order at most $d(h!)^d$. It also has order at most $d(n-d+1)!$: for any positive integers $u,v$, $u!v!\le(u+v-1)!$, and iterating gives the displayed coarser bound.

All diagonal scales are determined by the scale of the root. A closed walk of length $L$ forces the root's scale to belong to $\mu_{2^L-1}(F)$. The integer identity $\gcd(2^a-1,2^b-1)=2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1$, obtained by the Euclidean algorithm, gives the same gcd formula over all these lengths. The diagonal group thus injects into $\mu_{2^d-1}(F)$. If $d=1$, the entire group has order at most $n!$. If $d=n$, each class has one vertex and the only possible arcs form the directed $n$-cycle. The upper bound in this case is $n|\mu_{2^n-1}(F)|$.

If $1<d<n$, then the order is at most $n!$. For $d=2$ and $n\ge6$, use $6(n-1)!\le n!$. Equality in this bound is possible numerically only at $n=6$, where the equal-class bound gives $6(3!)^2=216<720$. The other $d=2$ case is $n=4$, where the equal-class bound gives $6(2!)^2=24$. This equality cannot occur. It would require the full order-eight permutation group preserving or exchanging the two classes and a diagonal kernel of order three. In particular the image would contain a transposition on one class fixing the other pointwise. For any lift $g$ of it, $g^2$ is in the kernel, so $g^3$ is an involutive lift of the same permutation. On the fixed class its scales square to one, so it fixes each of the squares of the two basis vectors in that class. Those squares span the other class by invertibility, contradicting the transposition there. For $d\ge3$ and $d<n$, we have $n\ge2d$. Thus $n!/(n-d+1)!$ is at least $(d+2)^{d-1}$. For $d=3$ this is $25>3(2^3-1)$. For $d\ge4$ it is at least $6^{d-1}>d\,2^d$, by induction from $216>64$. This proves the claim and strictness for the proper-period cases with $n\ge6$.

## 2. Attainment and the binary exception

The coefficient-one directed $n$-cycle is always simple and attains $n|\mu_{2^n-1}(F)|$. Each rotation lifts by permuting the basis and every permitted root scale gives a diagonal automorphism.

If $|F|>2$ and $n\ge2$, choose $a\ne 0,-n$ in $F$ and use $M=aI+J$, where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. Its determinant is $a^{n-1}(a+n)$, and every off-diagonal entry is one. Hence it defines a simple algebra and admits all coordinate permutations. The one-dimensional algebra with $e^2=e$ has order one. These witnesses attain the stated general maximum.

Over $\mathbb F_2$ every nonzero scale equals one, so the algebra group is exactly the automorphism group of the binary support matrix. In even dimension $n\ge2$, $I+J$ is invertible and has every off-diagonal entry one, realizing $S_n$. Full $S_n$ invariance forces constant diagonal and constant off-diagonal entries. Simplicity forces the off-diagonal value one and invertibility rules out $J$ for $n>1$. Consequently $I+J$ is the only maximizing matrix up to isomorphism in even dimension.

For odd $n\ge3$, even $A_n$ invariance is impossible when $n\ge5$: $A_n$ is transitive on ordered pairs of distinct points and on points, so a strongly connected invariant matrix must be $I+J$ or $J$, both singular. A subgroup of $S_n$ of index less than $n$ contains $A_n$. Its action on its fewer than $n$ cosets cannot be faithful, and any nontrivial normal subgroup of $S_n$ contains $A_n$ for $n\ge5$. Thus the automorphism group has order at most $(n-1)!$. This is attained by the matrix with all off-diagonal entries one and a loop at exactly one vertex. Its leading block on the other $n-1$ vertices is $I+J_{n-1}$, which is invertible and fixes the all-ones vector. The last Schur complement equals $1$ because $n-1$ is even. Its support is strongly connected, and it admits all permutations of the $n-1$ unmarked vertices.

For equality at odd $n\ge5$, a subgroup of order $(n-1)!$ has an action on $n$ cosets. The kernel must be trivial by the same normal subgroup argument, and this action defines an automorphism of $S_n$. Since $n$ is odd and $n\ge5$, every such automorphism is inner. Thus the subgroup fixes one point in the original action and is its full stabilizer. After moving that point to the last coordinate, the matrix has block form
$$
\begin{pmatrix} aI+bJ_{n-1}&c\mathbf 1\\d\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}&e\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Strong connectivity forces $c=d=1$. Invertibility on the sum-zero subspace forces $a=1$, and the Schur complement then forces $e=1$. Both $b=0$ and $b=1$ attain the maximum. They give two distinct classes, a bidirected star with a loop at every vertex and a complete bidirected graph with only the distinguished vertex looped. Their edge counts differ.

For $n=3$ full $S_3$ invariance is impossible by the same constant-matrix calculation. A directed cycle attains order three. A matrix invariant under that 3-cycle has a common diagonal and two constant cyclic off-diagonals. Exactly one cyclic off-diagonal must be one: if both are one the matrix is singular, and if both are zero it is disconnected. The diagonal must be zero, since adding the identity to the cycle matrix gives row sum zero. Hence the cycle is the unique maximizer. For $n=1$ the single nonzero product gives the unique class.

## 3. All equality types over fields with more than two elements

The strict interior-period bounds show that for $n\ge2$ a maximizer has period one or period $n$.

In the period-$n$ case the maximum can be attained only when $n|\mu_{2^n-1}(F)|$ equals the maximum. The permutation image then contains every rotation. Normalize all cycle weights but one by rescaling consecutive basis vectors and call the remaining weight $w$. Solving the monomial isomorphism equations along the cycle for a step-one rotation gives $w/w^2\in F^{\times(2^n-1)}$. One way to see this is to write the scale recurrence $\lambda_{i+1}=(b_{i+1}/a_i)\lambda_i^2$ with source and target weights $a_i=b_i$ equal to one except for the last edge, which has weight $w$. Going around the cycle gives $\lambda_0^{2^n-1}=w/w^2$. Thus $w$ itself is a $(2^n-1)$-st power, and the remaining weight can also be normalized to one. This leaves exactly the coefficient-one cycle type in this branch.

In the period-one case the diagonal kernel is trivial and equality requires the entire $S_n$ in the permutation image. Each permutation $\sigma$ has a unique lift $g_\sigma$. The stabilizer of the first coordinate line is $S_{n-1}$ and it acts there by a multiplicative character $\chi$. It is trivial or sign because the transpositions generate and are conjugate, or trivial when $n=2$ or when the field has characteristic two. Pick permutations $\sigma_i$ sending $1$ to $i$. In the natural basis $f_i=g_{\sigma_i}e_1$, the action on $f_i$ has multiplier $\chi(\sigma_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}\sigma\sigma_i)$. If $\chi$ is trivial the matrices are permutation matrices. If $\chi$ is sign, multiplying $f_i$ by $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_i)$ makes the action the permutation matrices multiplied by the common sign of the acting permutation.

The sign alternative would give $P_\sigma M P_\sigma^{-1}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)M$ by compatibility with squares. If $n\ge4$, for each matrix entry there is a transposition fixing both its indices, forcing that entry to vanish in the odd-characteristic sign case. If $n=3$, the row-sum vector $M\mathbf1$ would transform by sign. A vector in the natural permutation representation transforming by sign is zero, since for each coordinate there is a transposition fixing it. Thus $M\mathbf1=0$, contradicting invertibility. In every case the lifts can therefore be made ordinary permutations.

It follows that in the period-one case all maximizers admit a natural basis with $M=aI+bJ$, where $b\ne0$ and $a(a+nb)\ne0$. For $n=2$ require also $a+b\ne0$ to have period one. Conversely all of these matrices attain $n!$, so belong to the list of maximizers exactly when $n!$ equals the claimed maximum. In this family the isomorphism types are given by the ratio of diagonal to off-diagonal entry. For $n\ge3$, comparing the off-diagonal equation at every ordered pair forces all basis scales equal, since for any two head indices there is a third index for the source. For $n=2$, the nonzero diagonal in this branch already forces the scales equal. Permutations preserve both constants. Thus two members of the family are isomorphic exactly when their pairs $(a,b)$ differ by a common nonzero scale.

Over $\mathbb C$, the cycle branch is the unique maximizing type for $2\le n\le5$, and the constant diagonal/off-diagonal branch gives the maximizers for $n\ge6$, since $n!>n(2^n-1)$ there. The one-dimensional case has one nonzero product up to a basis change. The structural cases of a full $S_n$ group and a maximal diagonal group were studied by Sriwongsa and Zou, whose classification is consistent with the direct argument above. [Sriwongsa–Zou, Theorems 3.2 and 4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01333)

This is an informal proof. The complex order maximum is a prior Lean result. The result here retains the root-of-unity dependence, resolves the binary exceptions, and decides which group-action cases can give equality.
