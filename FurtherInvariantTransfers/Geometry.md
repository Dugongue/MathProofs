# Everywhere locally equivalent smooth cubics that are inequivalent over the rationals

**Theorem.** There are two integral cubic forms in six variables whose hypersurfaces are geometrically smooth. The forms are equivalent over $\mathbb R$ and over $\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z$ for every prime $p$ and every $k\geq1$, using determinant-one matrices. Over $\mathbb Q$ there is no linear equivalence even up to nonzero scalar, and their cubic fourfolds are nonisomorphic even as abstract varieties. This answers negatively the real-and-congruence equivalence question of Agrawal and Saxena, *On the Complexity of Cubic Forms*, §6, second question on printed page 38. They allow finitely many exceptional primes.

After explicit rational changes of basis, the polarized six-by-six-by-six tensors are equivalent under three standard special orthogonal matrices over every completion of $\mathbb Q$. There is no ordinary three-tensor isomorphism over $\mathbb Q$. More generally for each $m\geq1$ there are $2^m$ pairwise nonisomorphic rational cubic hypersurfaces in $6m$ variables, all geometrically Fermat and all equivalent locally everywhere by such special orthogonal changes of basis. [Agrawal–Saxena, §6](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/%75sers/nitin/papers/cubic-forms.pdf#page=38)

## 1. Trace cubics recover a finite étale algebra

Let $F$ be any field of characteristic zero. For a finite étale $F$-algebra $A$ of dimension $d$, define the quadratic form $q_A(x)=\operatorname{Tr}_{A/F}(x^2)$, cubic $f_A(x)=\operatorname{Tr}_{A/F}(x^3)$, and symmetric trilinear form
$$
 T_A(x,y,z)=\operatorname{Tr}_{A/F}(xyz).
$$
The trace here is the trace of the regular action by multiplication. These objects commute with scalar extension and are defined in any chosen $F$-basis of $A$. An algebra isomorphism preserves all three by conjugating the regular actions.

Over an algebraic closure, $A$ splits as a product of $d$ copies of the field. In the primitive idempotent coordinates $f_A$ is the Fermat cubic, so it is geometrically smooth. The Hessian of $f_A$ at a vector with these coordinates $(x_1,\ldots,x_d)$ is diagonal with entries $6x_i$. Thus the nonzero rank-one Hessian directions are exactly the primitive idempotent lines. The Hessian transforms by congruence under a linear substitution and an overall scalar has no effect on rank. Therefore a projective equivalence over $F$ gives a Galois-equivariant bijection of the primitive idempotent lines. Match the primitive idempotents themselves by this bijection. This defines an algebra isomorphism over the algebraic closure because componentwise multiplication is preserved. Galois equivariance makes it descend to $F$. The converse was noted above. This argument also works after any extension of $F$.

When $d\geq5$, an abstract isomorphism of two such hypersurfaces over $F$ extends to their ambient projective spaces. Over an algebraic closure the Picard group is generated by the hyperplane bundle by Grothendieck–Lefschetz. An abstract isomorphism preserves its ample generator. The complete linear system of that bundle is the given projective embedding, since all its sections are restrictions of ambient linear forms by the hypersurface exact sequence. Thus the projective extension exists and is unique over the algebraic closure. If the original isomorphism is defined over $F$, so is its unique projective extension. A projective map of the hypersurfaces carries the equations into scalar multiples of each other. In particular abstract indistinguishability over $F$ is also exactly algebra isomorphism for $d\geq5$. [Grothendieck, SGA 2, Exposé XII, Corollary 3.7](https://jcreinhold.github.io/sga/ii/12-projective-algebraic-schemes.html)

Even three independent invertible mode maps cannot lose this algebra invariant. For the first two modes, the pair-adjoint space is the space of pairs $(M,N)$ with
$$
 T_A(Mx,y,z)=T_A(x,Ny,z)\quad\text{for all }x,y,z.
$$
By nondegeneracy of the étale trace pairing it follows that $(Mx)y=x(Ny)$. Evaluating at each unit argument gives $M=N=m_a$, the regular multiplication map for some $a\in A$. Conversely every such pair works. The first projection of the pair-adjoint space is thus the regular algebra $m(A)$. An independent-mode tensor isomorphism conjugates this algebra to $m(B)$ by its first mode map. Hence it implies $A\simeq B$. The converse comes from using the same algebra map in all three modes.

## 2. A six-dimensional pair over $\mathbb Q$

Set
$$
 K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{13},\sqrt{17}),\qquad
 A=K\times\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q,\qquad
 B=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{13})\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{17})\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{221}).
$$
The simple-factor degree multisets of $A,B$ are respectively $\{4,1,1\}$ and $\{2,2,2\}$. Thus they are nonisomorphic over $\mathbb Q$. In the basis $1,\sqrt{13},\sqrt{17},\sqrt{221}$ of $K$ followed by the two scalar factors, the cubic for $A$ is
$$
 4a^3+156ab^2+204ac^2+2652ad^2+5304bcd+e^3+f^3 .
$$
In the natural square-root bases of the three factors of $B$ its cubic is
$$
 2\sum_{i=1}^{3}(a_i^3+3D_i a_i b_i^2),
 \qquad (D_1,D_2,D_3)=(13,17,221).
$$
These displays describe the rational forms. The all-prime congruence claim uses the integral bases below.

At every finite prime outside $13,17$, $K$ is unramified because the quadratic discriminants are $13,17$. Its local decomposition subgroup is cyclic. At $13$, the class $17$ is a square since $17\equiv 2^2\bmod 13$, and Hensel's lemma lifts the root. At $17$, the class $13$ is a square since $13\equiv 8^2\bmod 17$. Thus at each ramified prime at most one independent quadratic extension remains. Both square roots are real. It follows that every decomposition subgroup of the biquadratic Galois group has order at most two.

If the decomposition subgroup is trivial at a place $v$, both algebras become $F_v^6$ for $F_v=\mathbb Q_v$. If its order is two, write $E/F_v$ for the quadratic local extension it defines. The degree-four factor of $A$ becomes $E\times E$, so $A_v\simeq E\times E\times F_v\times F_v$. Exactly one of the three degree-two subfields of $K$ splits at $v$, since exactly one nontrivial quadratic character of the biquadratic group is trivial on the order-two subgroup. The other two give copies of $E$. Hence $B_v\simeq E\times E\times F_v\times F_v$ as well. This proves local algebra isomorphism at every place.

Use any integral bases of the products of rings of integers $\mathcal O_A,\mathcal O_B$ to define the integral cubics in the theorem. These bases are explicit if desired. In $K$ use $1,s,t,st$ where $s=(1+\sqrt{13})/2$, $t=(1+\sqrt{17})/2$, and use the two scalar unit coordinates. The product basis in $K$ is integral and the order it spans has discriminant $13^2 17^2$. For example, its trace Gram matrix is the tensor product of the two quadratic Gram matrices, whose determinants are $13$ and $17$. The order is maximal outside $13,17$ since its discriminant is a unit there. At $13$ the $t$-algebra is split étale over $\mathbb Z_{13}$, so the product order is a pair of copies of the maximal quadratic $s$-order. At $17$ exchange the roles. Thus this is an integral basis of $\mathcal O_K$. In each factor of $B$ use $1,(1+\sqrt{D_i})/2$. The discriminant of their product basis is $13\cdot17\cdot221=13^2 17^2$, so the two integral trace Gram matrices have the same determinant.

Every local algebra isomorphism preserves the integral closure of $\mathbb Z_p$, which in these products is the respective product of local rings of integers. Its matrix in the indicated bases is therefore in $\operatorname{GL}_6(\mathbb Z_p)$. It preserves the trace form, so the equality of the two discriminants gives determinant $1$ or $-1$ over $\mathbb Q_p$. In every local algebra of the pair there are at least two scalar factors. Swapping them is an algebra automorphism with determinant $-1$. Compose with this swap if necessary to obtain determinant $1$. Reduction modulo $p^k$ gives an isomorphism of the two integral cubics over $\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z$ for every $k$. The real trace forms have the same discriminant in these bases, and the same determinant correction works over $\mathbb R$. The classification in Section 1 excludes all projective equivalences over $\mathbb Q$. This settles the stated question, with geometric smoothness and no exceptional primes.

## 3. Standard orthogonal gauges in six variables

In the square-root bases, the trace quadratic forms simplify by rational reordering and rescaling to
$$
 q_A\simeq I_2\perp\langle 1,13,17,221\rangle,\qquad
 q_B\simeq 2I_2\perp 2\langle1,13,17,221\rangle .
$$
The four-dimensional diagonal form displayed here is rationally isometric to $I_4$. In three standard coordinates let $u=(2,3,0)$, $v=(3,-2,0)$, $e=(0,0,1)$. Then $u,v+2e,2v-13e$ are pairwise orthogonal with squared norms $13,17,221$. Add a separate standard unit coordinate. Also $2I_2\simeq I_2$ by the two orthogonal vectors $(1,1),(1,-1)$ of squared norm two. Hence both $q_A,q_B$ have rational standard orthonormal bases, obtainable by these displayed invertible rational changes.

In those bases, each local algebra map preserves the standard dot product and the cubic and trilinear forms. Use the same scalar-factor transposition to make it special orthogonal at each place, separately. Thus the same six-dimensional map on all three modes is a local tensor isomorphism in $\operatorname{SO}_6(\mathbb Q_v)^3$, and in particular in $\operatorname{SL}_6(\mathbb Q_v)^3$. The pair-adjoint invariant from Section 1 excludes every ordinary tensor isomorphism over $\mathbb Q$. The orthonormal bases here are rational rather than integral. The congruence claim uses the separate integral gauges in Section 2.

## 4. Exponentially many distinct companions in one dimension

Choose $m$ disjoint positive prime pairs $a_i,b_i$ with $a_i,b_i\equiv1\bmod8$ and $b_i\equiv1\bmod a_i$. Dirichlet's theorem supplies arbitrarily many choices by taking $b_i\equiv1\bmod 8a_i$ and excluding the finitely many primes already used. Quadratic reciprocity makes $a_i$ a square modulo $b_i$, and $b_i$ is a square modulo $a_i$. Repeat the six-dimensional construction with these pairs, making étale algebras $A_i,B_i$. The local decomposition argument is unchanged. At each place, the common local algebra has at least two scalar factors.

For each bit string of length $m$, form the direct product of one of $A_i,B_i$ per index. All $2^m$ resulting algebras have dimension $6m$. They are mutually nonisomorphic over $\mathbb Q$, since the presence of the degree-four field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{a_i},\sqrt{b_i})$ records exactly the choice $A_i$. These fields for distinct indices are different by their ramified primes. At every place every choice gives the same local algebra. Thus all trace cubics are mutually equivalent locally everywhere but pairwise nonisomorphic over $\mathbb Q$, even projectively.

The trace quadratic forms can all be put into standard rational gauges. For $A_i$ it is enough to handle $\langle1,a_i,b_i,a_i b_i\rangle$, since the other two entries are one. At a fixed place, the cyclic decomposition condition means that the square classes of $a_i,b_i$ span a group of order at most two. It follows that the diagonal form is either already standard or is equivalent to $\langle1,1,d,d\rangle$, where $d$ can be taken to be one of $a_i,b_i$. Both primes are sums of two rational squares by the two-squares theorem. The two-dimensional change of basis from such a representation identifies $\langle d,d\rangle$ with $I_2$. Thus the four-dimensional form is everywhere locally standard, and globally standard by Hasse–Minkowski. The trace form of $B_i$ is locally isometric to that of $A_i$ by the local algebra equivalence, so it too is rationally standard by Hasse–Minkowski. Orthogonal direct sums give the gauges in dimension $6m$. As before a local scalar-factor swap makes any needed determinant correction. The local special orthogonal equivalences therefore hold simultaneously throughout the family.

## Scope and antecedents

Mazur's discussion of companions already gives locally isomorphic smooth plane curves in the case of Selmer's cubic. The antecedent concerns abstract isomorphism of curves. The result here gives the linear congruence equivalences asked for by Agrawal and Saxena and also the higher-dimensional orthogonal tensor statements. The arithmetic input uses the classical fact that the two actions of the biquadratic group on six letters, one regular orbit and two singletons versus three orbits of size two, agree after restriction to every cyclic subgroup. The recovered Ley incidence idea moved geometric data into intrinsic configurations. The specific transfer here keeps the individual lines rather than only their rank histogram, just as the preceding smooth-cubic reduction used a localized Hessian rank test to read the hidden coordinates. No novelty is claimed for trace duality or the example of coincident cyclic restrictions. The new proof is informal, and priority for this precise cubic-equivalence answer and the quantitative family remains unverified. [Mazur, §5 on companions](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9307231) · [Linowitz–McReynolds–Miller on locally equivalent arithmetic inputs](https://www.numdam.org/item/AIF_2017__67_2_451_0/)

The specific everywhere-cyclic extension used in Section 2 is the classical Serre–Tate Hasse norm example. Its three quadratic subfields also occur in an example of a polynomial with a root at every completion. The relation between the six-point actions is the biquadratic Brauer relation.
