# Even dihedral moments and finite group recovery

The results below are informal proofs.

## Sharp characteristic-two tensor cutoffs for every even polygon

Let \(K\) be an infinite field of characteristic two, \(n\) even, and \(D_n\) act naturally on the \(n\) vertices of a regular polygon. Use the **unnormalized group-sum tensors** \(T_d(x)=\sum_{g\in D_n}(gx)^{\otimes d}\). Their entries through order four generate the invariant field for every even \(n\ge6\), and order four is sharp for geometric generic separation. For \(n=4\) the exact cutoff is five. For \(n=2\) the natural action has a kernel of order two, so every group-sum tensor vanishes. This completes the characteristic-two question for even polygons. [Edidin and Katz, §5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09599v4#S5.SS2) · [Odd-polygon cutoffs](odd-dihedral-lemma.md#sharp-dihedral-moment-cutoffs-in-characteristic-two)

The entries of a group-sum tensor of order \(d\) span the group traces of degree-\(d\) homogeneous polynomials, by taking all coordinate monomials. In characteristic two, \(T_1=T_2=0\). Each linear or quadratic coordinate monomial is fixed by a reflection, using the reflection interchanging the two positions in the quadratic case. Its group trace cancels in pairs. The order-zero group sum is also zero. Since the all-ones vector \(\mathbf 1\) is fixed, expanding the three slots gives \(T_3(x+a\mathbf 1)=T_3(x)\) for every \(a\in K\). This gives positive-dimensional fibers and rules out any generic cutoff below four.

### The square

Write each diagonal pair in ordered coordinates, with pair sums \(s,t\) and pair products \(p,q\). The dihedral group of the square is generated by the independent swaps inside each pair and a swap of the two pairs. The invariant ring under the internal swaps is \(K[s,t,p,q]\). The group trace under the internal swaps has image exactly \(st\,K[s,t,p,q]\). For each single pair with entries \(a,c\), the trace of any polynomial is symmetric and vanishes at \(c=a\), hence is divisible by the pair sum \(a+c\). Its quotient is again symmetric. Conversely the trace of \(a\) times any symmetric polynomial gives that polynomial times \(a+c\). Apply this separately in the two pairs, which also preserves the homogeneous degree shift by two.

The full group trace thus equals \(st\) times the swap trace in \(K[s,t,p,q]\), where \(s,t\) have degree one and \(p,q\) degree two. Through total degree four the only nonzero generators of the trace images are \(st(s+t)\), \(st(s+t)^2\), and \(st(p+q)\). They generate exactly \(K(s+t,st,p+q)\), of transcendence degree three, so they leave positive-dimensional fibers. Order five adds \(st(sp+tq)\). On the generic locus, \(s+t,st\) recover the unordered pair \(s,t\) by a separable quadratic. Once this order is chosen, \(p+q,sp+tq\) recover \(p,q\) by a linear system. Then each opposite pair is recovered by its sum and product, using separable quadratics. The ambiguity is exactly the dihedral action. These reconstructions prove field generation also, since all extensions used are separable and the resulting degree is at most eight, the degree of the full fixed-field extension. The four values \(s+t,st,p+q,sp+tq\) will also be used for the quotient in the octagon case.

### Doubling a polygon

Let \(n=2m\) with \(m\ge3\). Index the opposite pairs by \(i\in\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\). Write \(t_i=x_i\) for a chosen coordinate of a pair and \(s_i=x_i+x_{i+m}\) for its sum. The central half-turn \(h\) sends \(t_i\) to \(t_i+s_i\) and fixes all \(s_i\). The quotient \(D_{2m}/\langle h\rangle=D_m\) acts naturally on \(s\). Every positive-degree quotient trace of a monomial in \(s\) is available at the same degree in the original tensors. To see this, replace one factor \(s_i\) by \(t_i\) and trace over \(\langle h\rangle\) first. The same statement holds for linear combinations of such monomials.

Suppose the \(s\)-configuration has been recovered up to \(D_m\) and choose one representative of it. Put \(z_{ij}=s_i t_j+s_j t_i\). The half-turn trace of \(t_i t_j+t_i s_j\) is \(z_{ij}\). For a polynomial \(P(s)\) of degree at most two, the original group trace of \((t_i t_j+t_i s_j)P(s)\) is thus the quotient trace of \(z_{ij}P(s)\), an available measurement of degree at most four. A lift of a quotient permutation \(q\) acts on the \(t_i\) by \(t_{q(i)}+\epsilon_{q,i}s_{q(i)}\) with known \(\epsilon_{q,i}\in\{0,1\}\). Consequently these traces, after subtraction of known terms in \(s\), give a linear system in \(t\). Its homogeneous rows are
\[
 \sum_{q\in D_m} (s_{q(i)}t_{q(j)}+s_{q(j)}t_{q(i)})\,(qP)(s).
\]
Every row vanishes on \(t=s\).

Here is a rank-\((m-1)\) specialization, showing that the generic kernel is exactly the line spanned by \(s\). Choose \(s_0=x,\ s_1=y\) with \(x,y\) distinct and nonzero, and put all other \(s_i=0\). For each \(d\ne0\) apply the rows \((i,j)=(0,d)\) with \(P=s_0,s_0^2\). Only permutations taking \(0\) to \(0\) or \(1\) contribute. At either center \(r\), their two images of \(d\) are \(r+d,r-d\), counted with their multiplicities. The two rows have coefficients \(x,y\) and \(x^2,y^2\) on the corresponding centered sums, respectively. Their determinant is \(xy(x+y)\ne0\). Thus a kernel vector satisfies
\[
 s_r(t_{r+d}+t_{r-d})+(s_{r+d}+s_{r-d})t_r=0
 \quad(r=0,1).
\]
The \(d=0\) equations are identically zero as well. Subtract a multiple of \(s\) so \(t_0=0\), and set \(a=t_1/y\). The center-zero equations give \(t_{-i}=t_i\). Combining the two center equations gives
\[
 t_{i+2}=t_i+(s_{i+2}+s_{-i})a
\]
for every index modulo \(m\). If \(m\) is odd, indices \(0,2,\ldots,m-1,1\) show \(t_{m-1}=xa\) and \(t_1=t_{m-1}\). Since \(t_1=ya\), this forces \(a=0\). The shift by two then forces all entries to vanish. If \(m\) is even, the even nonzero indices all have \(t_i=xa\), and the odd indices all have \(t_i=ya\). The additional row with \((i,j)=(0,2)\) and \(P=s_0s_1\) has only two contributing permutations, the ones taking the ordered pair \((0,1)\) to \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\). At the specialized vector this row is \(xy(x^2+y^2)a\), so \(a=0\) here as well.

Solve the full generic linear system after setting one coordinate, such as \(t_0\), to zero by subtracting a multiple of \(s\). On \(s_0\ne0\), write \(t=t'+w s\) with \(t'_0=0\). The vector \(t'\) is known. The half-turn replaces \(w\) by \(w+1\). Substitute \(t=t'+w s\) into the original cubic trace of \(t_0^2t_1\). In each quotient summand, the coefficient of \(w^3\) before the half-turn trace is \(s_{q(0)}^2s_{q(1)}\), regardless of the lift. Since
\[
 (w+1)^3+w^3=w^2+w+1,\qquad
 (w+1)^2+w^2=(w+1)+w=1,
\]
the measurement recovers \(w^2+w\) with coefficient equal to the quotient dihedral trace of \(s_0^2s_1\). That coefficient is nonzero: the adjacent ordered pair with unequal exponents has trivial stabilizer in \(D_m\) for \(m\ge3\). Thus the remaining two possible \(w\)'s differ exactly by the half-turn. This proves that the doubling step requires only order four once the quotient configuration \(s\) is recovered. It also gives a separable lift of field degree at most two after adjoining all the \(s_i\).

### The hexagon and octagon

For \(n=6\) the quotient is the triangle, whose own characteristic-two group-sum data through order four are missing one invariant. Use the product decomposition \(D_6=D_3\times C_2\) and Fourier coordinates on the \(C_3\) frequency of each of the two rows. Retain the notation \(s_i=t_i+t_i'\) for their sums, with \(i\) now a frequency modulo three, so the central half-turn exchanges \(t_i,t_i'\), and a reflection sends \(i\) to \(-i\) in both rows. Put \(b=s_1,\ c=s_2,\ v=t_1,\ u=t_2,\ L=cv+bu\). The quotient trace data already give \(b^3+c^3\) and \(s_0(b^3+c^3)\), hence \(s_0\) generically. The original trace of \((t_0t_1+t_0s_1)s_2\) gives \(s_0L\). The original trace of \(v^2uc\) gives \(L^2+bcL\). To check the latter, first trace over the flip to obtain \(c^2v^2+b^2uc+b^2c^2\), then add its reflection. All these expressions have cyclic weight zero, and the order-three rotation factor is one in \(K\). Since \(L\) is generically nonzero, this recovers \(bc\). Therefore \(s_0,bc,b^3+c^3\) give the whole triangle fixed field. The unordered cubes \(b^3,c^3\) are obtained by a separable quadratic, and \(bc\) ties the third-root choices together up to rotation. The doubling argument then applies, in ordinary vertex coordinates after undoing the Fourier change.

For \(n=8\) the quotient is the square. Set \(s_i=x_i+x_{i+4}\) for \(i=0,1,2,3\), and \(A=s_0+s_2,\ B=s_1+s_3,\ P=s_0s_2,\ Q=s_1s_3\). The square calculations give \(A+B,AB,P+Q\) from quotient orders at most four. The original cubic trace supplies the missing invariant:
\[
 \operatorname{Tr}_{D_8}(x_0^2x_2)=\sum_{i\bmod 8}x_i^2(x_{i+2}+x_{i-2})=AP+BQ.
\]
The two parity classes give the two summands. Thus the whole square fixed field is available and the doubling argument again applies.

For any even \(n\ge6\), either \(n=6,8\), or its half \(m\) is odd with \(m\ge5\), or \(m\) is a smaller even integer with \(m\ge6\). The cited odd-polygon theorem applies in the second case, and induction applies in the third. For field generation, over an algebraic closure the extension obtained by adjoining \(s\) has degree at most \(2m\), and adjoining \(t\) thereafter has degree at most two. Since the original fixed field has degree \(4m\) in the rational function field, the contained moment-data field equals the full fixed field. These conclusions descend to \(K\). In particular the only Fourier splitting needed above is finite separable, and formation of invariant polynomial spaces commutes with scalar extension by the linear kernel description. Passing to fraction fields gives scalar extension for the fixed field because one can make a denominator invariant by multiplying it by its other translates. The geometric generic fibers are exactly the dihedral orbits by the same explicit reconstruction.

## One generic real Gram graph recovers groups whose inverse pairs are distinguished

Consider a faithful finite orthogonal representation in which
\[
 g+g^{-1}=h+h^{-1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h\in\{g,g^{-1}\}.
\]
One generic real Gram graph determines its abstract group, even among arbitrary competing generic real orthogonal representations. In particular this holds for the real regular representation of **every** finite group and for every representation containing it. This establishes a representation-theoretic case of Mixon and Vose's Conjecture 2.7 without a restriction on the abstract group. [Mixon and Vose, Theorem 2.5 and Conjecture 2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S2)

The generic orbit is free. Under the labeling \(g\mapsto g^{-1}x\), its inner-product labels are \(x^{\mathsf T}gh^{-1}x\) on the pair \(g,h\). By the hypothesis, different inverse pairs yield different quadratic forms, hence different values on a nonempty Zariski open set. The Gram graph is therefore the complete Cayley graph colored by inverse pairs of differences. The real regular representation satisfies the hypothesis because applying \(g+g^{-1}\) to the basis vector at the identity reads the unordered pair directly.

Any competing faithful group \(H\) acting on a generic orbit gives a regular subgroup of the graph's color-preserving automorphism group. In any real orthogonal representation an involution's symmetric operator cannot equal the symmetric part of another element. Indeed on the two eigenspaces of the involution, equality in Cauchy–Schwarz forces the other operator's actions to be the same signs. Hence in a generic \(H\)-orbit each involution gives its own one-neighbor color. Every other inverse pair has at least two neighbors, and the observed graph has valencies at most two. Thus its two-neighbor colors are also precisely the non-involutory inverse pairs of \(H\). A candidate element taking a chosen basepoint to a point in a two-neighbor color cannot be an involution.

Use the classification of color-preserving automorphisms of the complete inverse-pair Cayley graph, with right-translation convention. For a Boolean group or a nonabelian group outside generalized dicyclic type, the color-preserving group is just the regular translation group. A regular subgroup is then the entire group. For a non-Boolean abelian group it is the extension of right translations by inversion. For a generalized dicyclic \(G=\langle A,t\rangle\) outside the family \(Q_8\times E\) with Boolean \(E\), it is the extension of right translations by an involution \(\psi\) fixing \(A\) pointwise and inverting every element outside \(A\). In these last two extensions, a regular subgroup has the form
\[
 \{R_g\alpha^{\chi(g)}:g\in G\},
\]
where \(R_g\) is right translation, \(\alpha\) is the indicated automorphism, and \(\chi\) is an \(\alpha\)-invariant homomorphism to \(C_2\). This follows because the regular subgroup has index two in the extension and does not contain the basepoint stabilizer \(\alpha\). The quotient homomorphism to \(C_2\) therefore has value one at \(\alpha\). [Alimirzaei and Morris, Theorem 18](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.09367#S3) · [Sutherland, Theorems 5.3 and 5.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33938#S5)

In the abelian case, any nonzero \(\chi\) has value one at some non-involution \(g\). If a chosen \(a\) with \(\chi(a)=1\) is an involution and every non-involution \(b\) has \(\chi(b)=0\), then \(a+b\) is a non-involution with value one. The permutation \(R_g\alpha\) is an involution taking the basepoint to \(g\), which is impossible for its two-neighbor color. In the dicyclic case any nonzero \(\chi\) has value one at some \(g\notin A\), since products of two elements of the nontrivial coset cover \(A\). All such \(g\) have order four, whereas \(R_g\psi\) has order two, giving the same contradiction. Thus in either case the competing subgroup is the original regular translation group.

Finally suppose \(G=Q_8\times E\) with \(E\cong C_2^r\). The one-neighbor matching permutations generate, on the original labeling, the elementary abelian group of translations by \(C_2\times E\). In the \(H\)-labeling these same matchings are right translations by its involutions. Thus their generated abstract subgroup \(A_H\) in \(H\) is elementary abelian of order \(2^{r+1}\), and its elements exhaust all the involutions together with the identity. In the original inverse-pair graph each two-neighbor color is a disjoint union of four-cycles. Starting at the basepoint, the vertex opposite in any such four-cycle is always the one corresponding to \(z=(-1,e)\). In the \(H\)-action the same two-neighbor color is the union of cycles induced by the corresponding inverse pair, so every \(h\notin A_H\) has \(h^2=z_H\), the element of \(H\) at that same opposite vertex. For \(a\in A_H\) and \(h\notin A_H\), comparing \((ah)^2=h^2\) shows that \(a\) commutes with \(h\). Hence \(A_H\) is central. The quotient \(H/A_H\) is \(C_2^2\). Choose representatives \(u,v\) of two independent quotient elements. Then \(u^2=v^2=(uv)^2=z_H\), giving \(vu=z_Huv\). They generate a copy of \(Q_8\) meeting \(A_H\) in \(\{1,z_H\}\). A complement of \(\langle z_H\rangle\) in \(A_H\) is central and gives \(H\cong Q_8\times C_2^r\). This proves abstract recovery in the remaining case.

## Recovering finite linear groups without an invariant inner product

Let \(G\) be any finite subgroup of \(\operatorname{GL}_d(K)\) over an infinite field \(K\). Given unlabeled point sets for \(d+1\) generic \(G\)-orbits, the group can be recovered concretely. For every nonscalar \(G\), \(d\) generic orbits suffice. Over the real or complex numbers the \(d+1\) worst-case bound is sharp for \(d\ge2\) when a competing subgroup may realize the observed orbits with stabilizers. These statements address the non-isometric linear-action case in Mixon and Vose's discussion. [Mixon and Vose, Problem 1.1 and §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S4)

Pick the first \(d\) generic seeds \(x_i\) so their column matrix \(X\) is invertible. A linear map preserving each of the first \(d\) orbit sets has \(Ax_i=g_i x_i\) for some \(g_i\in G\). It is thus among the finite candidate matrices
\[
 A_{\mathbf g}(X)=(g_1x_1\;\cdots\; g_dx_d)X^{-1}.
\]
Preserving one more orbit with seed \(y\) requires \(A_{\mathbf g}(X)y=u y\) for some \(u\in G\). Each such condition is an equality of rational functions in the seeds. If \(A_{\mathbf g}\ne u\) identically as a matrix-valued function, the equality is excluded on a nonempty open set by clearing denominators and varying \(y\) independently. There are finitely many assignments and \(u\), so on a common nonempty Zariski open set the only maps preserving all the observed sets are precisely \(G\). The algorithm can work directly with the sets by choosing a basis from their union, enumerating the images of its vectors within their respective sets, and checking each candidate on all sets. More generally the same argument gives recovery from \(k+1\) generic orbits if \(k\) generic orbits span \(K^d\), by choosing a basis from the first \(k\) orbits.

For the improved bound, choose a nonscalar \(t\in G\). Preserving the \(i\)-th set requires \(A_{\mathbf g}(X)t x_i=u_i x_i\) for some \(u_i\in G\). For generic seeds the only possibilities are the equalities that hold identically as rational functions on the space of basis matrices, by the same finite polynomial exclusion. A nonscalar linear map has \(x_i,t x_i\) linearly independent on a nonempty open set. Fix \(j\ne i\) and first specialize the \(j\)-th basis column to \(t x_i\), completing a basis with the other columns. The identity gives \(g_j t x_i=u_i x_i\). Instead specialize the \(j\)-th column to \(x_i+t x_i\), again completing a basis, to obtain
\[
 g_j(x_i+t x_i)-g_i x_i=u_i x_i.
\]
Thus \((g_j-g_i)x_i=0\) on a nonempty open set, so \(g_j=g_i\). This holds for all pairs of distinct indices. Every surviving \(A_{\mathbf g}\) is consequently a member of \(G\), proving the bound.

For sharpness with arbitrary competing subgroups, let the true group be the nontrivial scalar sign group. For any at most \(d\) independent seeds, extend them to a basis and let independent coordinate signs act in that basis. They form a strictly larger finite linear group for \(d\ge2\), and each observed two-point orbit is identical to its orbit under the original scalar group. Therefore \(d\) generic orbits do not determine the concrete group in that data model. If the competing group must have the same order and act freely on the observations, the analogous ambiguity still occurs over \(\mathbb C\) by using scalar cube roots and the cyclic order-three subgroup acting by a root on one basis direction and its inverse on another. The proved universal upper bound allows any competitor, and the sharpness argument uses the hypotheses on competitors just specified.
