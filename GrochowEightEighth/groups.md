# Permutation-group conjugacy witnesses and minimum faithful actions of class-two groups

## 1. Orbit-marked conjugacy and conjugacy search for abelian permutation groups

There is a deterministic polynomial-time many-one reduction from conjugacy of permutation groups with an ordered list of distinguished point orbits to ordinary permutation-group conjugacy. For abelian permutation groups, this yields a deterministic polynomial-time Turing reduction from search to ordinary decision, returning a permutation on the original points. The abelian groups may have arbitrary point-orbit sizes, arbitrary exponents and unbounded generator rank. This is a restricted answer to the permutation-group conjugacy search-to-decision question of Allender–Grochow–van Melkebeek–Moore–Morgan. [Search and decision, Sections 2 and 5](https://arxiv.org/html/1710.09806)

### Encoding distinguished point orbits

Let \(G,H\leq S_n\) be given by generators. Distinguish pairwise distinct point orbits \(O_1,\ldots,O_t\) of \(G\) and corresponding pairwise distinct point orbits \(D_1,\ldots,D_t\) of \(H\). Choose distinct primes \(q_i>n\). These can be found in polynomial time by trial division in a polynomial range, using \(p_m=O(m\log(m+2))\) for the \(m\)-th prime and \(t\leq n\). Construct the action of \(\widetilde G=G\times\prod_i C_{q_i}\) on the original domain together with the disjoint sets \(O_i\times C_{q_i}\). On each such set, \(G\) acts as on \(O_i\), the \(i\)-th cyclic factor acts by translation in the second coordinate, and all other added factors act trivially. On the original domain all added factors are trivial. Form \(\widetilde H\) analogously. All generator arrays have polynomial length. If the two expanded degrees differ, return a fixed no instance of ordinary conjugacy.

A conjugator mapping each \(O_i\) onto \(D_i\) extends coordinatewise to a conjugator of the expanded groups. Conversely, in either expanded action the original domain is the union of the orbits of sizes at most \(n\). Among the other orbits, the one whose size is divisible by \(q_i\) is exactly the added orbit for label \(i\). The factor \(C_{q_i}\) is the unique Sylow \(q_i\)-subgroup, since all primes dividing a subgroup of \(S_n\) are at most \(n\). Thus any expanded conjugator induces a bijection from the \(C_{q_i}\)-orbits in \(O_i\times C_{q_i}\) to those in \(D_i\times C_{q_i}\). This is an equivariant bijection \(O_i\to D_i\) for the same group isomorphism \(\phi:G\to H\) induced on the original action. The equivariance on the original domain follows because the quotient by the product of the added cyclic factors is exactly the original group.

The expanded conjugator's restriction already gives some \(\phi\)-equivariant bijection of the original domains. For each isomorphism type of transitive \(G\)-set, consider the \(H\)-orbits as \(G\)-sets via \(\phi\). The total multiplicities of each type agree, and every prescribed pair matches the same type. Assign the prescribed pairs first using their equivariant bijections, then match the remaining orbits of each type arbitrarily. Their union is a conjugator of the original groups with all specified orbit matches. This proves the many-one reduction. By successively trying the target orbits for each source orbit and retaining a choice allowed by this decision test, one can find a complete orbit matching that extends to some conjugator. There are at most \(n^2\) tests.

### Recovering an actual conjugator for abelian groups

For abelian \(G,H\), every transitive constituent is regular in its faithful local image. Indeed, in a transitive abelian group the stabilizers of the points agree, so the point stabilizer in the faithful quotient is trivial. Fix a compatible orbit matching. Write \(A_i,B_i\) for the local image groups on the matched orbits. They have orders bounded by \(n\), so their Cayley tables can be computed by enumerating the local images of the given generators. Choose an origin in every orbit and identify the points with the regular group on that orbit.

The global generator lists identify \(G,H\) with subgroups \(U\leq\prod_i A_i\) and \(V\leq\prod_i B_i\). A conjugator respecting the chosen matching exists precisely when the diagrams of finite abelian groups
\[
  U\longrightarrow A_i\quad(1\leq i\leq t),\qquad V\longrightarrow B_i\quad(1\leq i\leq t)
\]
of coordinate projections are isomorphic. In one direction, conjugation gives the commuting maps. In the other direction, given diagram isomorphisms \(\phi_0:U\to V\) and \(\phi_i:A_i\to B_i\), map the point \(a\) of the regular \(A_i\)-orbit to the point \(\phi_i(a)\) of the regular \(B_i\)-orbit in the chosen origin coordinates. The commuting projections prove equivariance on every orbit, and thus conjugacy on the entire domain.

Construct compact additive presentations, with the coordinate projection maps, by integer Smith or Hermite normal form from the generator matrices in cyclic bases of the local factors. Concretely the relations on the global generator images are the integer vectors whose generator-matrix linear combination lies in the lattice of cyclic-coordinate moduli. A basis of this lattice preimage gives the presentation and the coordinate maps. Its bit encoding is polynomial in the input, even when the global group has exponentially many elements. [Polynomial-time integer normal forms](https://doi.org/10.1137/0208040)

Encode the two diagrams as finite modules for the same star-shaped quiver ring over \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\), with \(m\) the least common multiple of the exponents of all local groups. There is one vertex idempotent per group position and an arrow from the global vertex to each local vertex. Nonidentity arrows have no composable pairs. On the additive direct sum of vertex groups, idempotents are coordinate projectors and arrows are the given projection maps. Ring-module isomorphism is exactly the stated diagram isomorphism. The ring is given by its additive cyclic factors and structure constants, with a basis consisting of these idempotents and arrows, and the modules by their additive cyclic factors and action matrices. These fit the bit-polynomial input specification of Ciocănea–Teodorescu's constructive module isomorphism algorithm for finite rings. Apply that algorithm and use the regular-action construction to return the conjugating permutation. The case \(m=1\) has only trivial local actions. [Ciocănea–Teodorescu, Section 1 and Corollary 1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.08365)

## 2. Minimum faithful permutation representations of class-two groups

Given the Cayley table of a finite group \(G\) of class at most two, suppose its Sylow 2-subgroup has elementary abelian quotient by its center. There is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm computing the minimum faithful permutation degree and a faithful action of exactly that degree. The odd Sylow subgroups may have arbitrary exponent, and the Sylow 2-subgroup may have arbitrary center exponent. This includes all odd-order class-two groups and all class-two groups of exponent dividing four. This is a restricted solution to the minimum faithful permutation degree problem proposed by Kantor and Luks. [Kantor–Luks, Section 13](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~kantor/PAPERS/quotient.pdf) [Das–Thakkar, Cayley-table complexity](https://doi.org/10.1137/24M1685353)

### Reducing the minimum to central hyperplanes

First take \(G\) to be a nontrivial \(p\)-group and put \(S=Z(G)[p]\), the subgroup of central elements killed by \(p\). Every nontrivial normal subgroup meets \(S\) nontrivially, since the class equation for conjugation on that subgroup gives a nontrivial intersection with the center. Thus an action given by orbit stabilizers \(L_i\) is faithful exactly when \(\bigcap_i(L_i\cap S)=1\). If \(L\cap S\) has codimension \(k\geq1\), choose \(k\) hyperplanes of \(S\) containing it with that same intersection, and replace \(L\) by its products with those hyperplanes. Each new stabilizer has its prescribed hyperplane as intersection with \(S\), and index \([G:L]/p^{k-1}\). Their total cost is no more than the original, since \(k\leq p^{k-1}\). A stabilizer containing all of \(S\) can be discarded. The minimum faithful degree is therefore the minimum weight of a basis of \(S^*\), where the weight of a nonzero functional is the least index of a subgroup whose intersection with \(S\) is its kernel. A spanning family reduces to a basis without increasing cost. Enumerate the at most \(|S|\) functionals and take a minimum-weight basis by the greedy algorithm. It remains to compute the weights and attaining stabilizers. The reduction to a weighted independent set in the dual also occurs in the general matroid formulation for nilpotent groups. [Elias–Silberman–Takloo-Bighash, minimal permutation representations](https://doi.org/10.1080/10586458.2010.10129066)

### Odd primes and arbitrary exponents

For odd \(p\), use the class-two Lie-ring correspondence, with underlying additive group \(A\), commutator bracket and product \(xy=x+y+[x,y]/2\). Its additive and bracket tables are constructed from the group table. Subgroups are exactly additive subgroups closed under the bracket. Fix a nonzero functional \(\lambda:S\to\mathbb F_p\), identifying its values with the order-\(p\) subgroup of \(\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\). For any candidate stabilizer \(L\) with \(L\cap S=\ker\lambda\), the character that vanishes on \(L\) and equals \(\lambda\) on \(S\) is well-defined on the additive subgroup \(L+S\). It extends to an additive character of \(A\), since \(\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\) is divisible. In finite abelian groups this also follows by adjoining one generator at a time and solving the required multiple equation in \(\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\).

Enumerate the \(|A|\) additive characters using a cyclic decomposition. For each \(\chi\) whose restriction is nonzero, set \(K=\ker\chi\) and define the alternating pairing \(\beta(x,y)=\chi([x,y])\) on \(K\). Any Lie subalgebra of \(K\) is isotropic for \(\beta\). Conversely the inverse image in \(K\) of a maximal-size isotropic subgroup of \(K/\operatorname{rad}\beta\) is a Lie subalgebra. It contains \(K\cap Z(G)\), and isotropy puts its brackets in that subgroup because the bracket is central. Its intersection with \(S\) is exactly \(\ker(\chi|_S)\).

A finite abelian group with a nondegenerate alternating pairing into \(\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\) splits into orthogonal hyperbolic planes. To construct them, choose \(a\) of maximal order \(p^e\) and \(b\) for which \(\beta(a,b)\) has order \(p^e\). This \(b\) exists by nondegeneracy and the identification with the character dual. The pair generates a nondegenerate copy of \(C_{p^e}\times C_{p^e}\), so its orthogonal complement splits off. Continue on the complement and choose one cyclic axis of each plane. This is isotropic of square-root order, which is optimal by comparing the sizes of an isotropic subgroup and its orthogonal complement. Taking the inverse image gives a stabilizer of size \(\sqrt{|K|\,|\operatorname{rad}\beta|}\). Its group index is the best for this \(\chi\), and maximizing the size over all extensions of \(\lambda\) gives the exact weight and an attaining stabilizer. All subgroups, quotients and pairings here are computed by table or finite abelian linear algebra. [Class-two Lie correspondence](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.16317#S7)

### The 2-primary case with elementary abelian central quotient

Let \(Z=Z(G)\) and \(V=G/Z\), now elementary abelian. Choose a central character \(\chi:Z\to\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\) extending \(\lambda\). It suffices to compute the maximum order of a subgroup of \(G/\ker\chi\) disjoint from the cyclic central image \(C=Z/\ker\chi\). Indeed, any stabilizer for \(\lambda\) admits such a character vanishing on its intersection with \(Z\), by the same character extension in the abelian group \(Z\). Enlarge it by \(\ker\chi\), then pass to the quotient. Conversely a subgroup disjoint from \(C\) lifts to a subgroup intersecting \(Z\) in exactly \(\ker\chi\). Enumerate the \(|Z|\) central characters and take the best weight for every \(\lambda\).

A subgroup disjoint from \(C\) embeds into \(V\). For a subspace of \(V\) to lift this way, it is necessary and sufficient that some lifts of its basis are commuting involutions. Because \(V\) is elementary abelian, all commutators have order dividing two. If \(|C|\geq4\), square parity modulo \(2C\) is a well-defined linear map on \(V\), by the class-two square identity and the fact that the commutator values lie in \(C[2]\subseteq 2C\). Its kernel consists of the vectors admitting involutory lifts, since a change of lift changes its square by an arbitrary element of \(2C\). On the kernel take a maximum isotropic subspace for the alternating commutator pairing into \(C[2]\), and lift a basis to involutions by central adjustments. Nondegenerate alternating elimination on the quotient by the radical computes the maximum.

If \(|C|=2\), squares define a quadratic form \(q:V\to\mathbb F_2\), with the commutator pairing as its polar form. The liftable subspaces are exactly the totally singular ones. To compute a largest one, split the polar radical \(R\) from a nondegenerate complement. If \(q|_R\ne0\), choose \(r\in R\) of value one. Take a symplectic Lagrangian in the complement and add \(r\) to each of its basis vectors of value one, then adjoin \(\ker(q|_R)\). Its dimension is optimal because any singular subspace has intersection with \(R\) of dimension at most \(\dim\ker(q|_R)\), and its projected dimension is at most the Lagrangian dimension. If \(q|_R=0\), include \(R\) and use binary Witt elimination on the complement. A symplectic basis decomposes the complement into quadratic planes, and pairs of anisotropic planes can be converted to pairs of hyperbolic planes. A remaining anisotropic plane lowers the maximal singular dimension by one. For example, the signed sum \(\sum_v(-1)^{q(v)}\) is negative exactly when one anisotropic plane remains, whereas a singular Lagrangian would extend to a hyperbolic basis and give positive signed sum. This computes the maximum and the required subspace constructively.

### Constructing the optimum action

Use the selected stabilizers for the greedy minimum-weight basis and return their coset actions on a disjoint union. For a general nilpotent \(G\), this construction is performed separately on each Sylow subgroup. The minimum degrees add. One proof is that any stabilizer in a finite direct product of Sylow subgroups is the product of its Sylow intersections. Every orbit can be replaced by its nontrivial Sylow coset actions, inflated to the whole group. Its index is a product of the corresponding nontrivial indices, which is at least their sum when there are two or more factors. The replacement preserves the same intersection of cores. Conversely, the disjoint union of optimal Sylow actions is faithful and has the sum of their degrees. The trivial group convention can be handled separately. The resulting witness has degree at most \(|G|\) by the regular-action bound and is explicitly computable in polynomial table time.

Both results are paper proofs.

## 3. Removing the class bound on odd Sylow subgroups

For every odd prime \(p\), the minimum faithful permutation degree and an action attaining it can be computed in deterministic polynomial time from the Cayley table of an arbitrary finite \(p\)-group. Consequently the full Section 2 algorithm applies to any finite nilpotent group whose Sylow 2-subgroup has elementary abelian quotient by its center, with no class bound on the odd Sylow subgroups. This argument uses ordinary character theory as well as the central-hyperplane optimization above. Ford's same-field induction theorem and the equality of odd-prime permutation and quasi-permutation degrees are prior results. The construction below finds inducing characters and then actual stabilizers in polynomial table time. [Ford, Theorem 1](https://doi.org/10.2307/2046653) [Quasi-permutation degree and its relation to permutation degree](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05852#S2)

### Computing the ordinary character table

Let \(m=\exp(G)\), a divisor of \(|G|\), and work over \(K=\mathbb Q(\zeta_m)\). Compute the conjugacy classes \(C_i\) and the integer multiplication matrices for their class sums in the center of the group algebra. On the central component of an irreducible character \(\chi\), the eigenvalue for class \(C_i\) is \(b_i=|C_i|\chi(g_i)/\chi(1)\), for a representative \(g_i\). Over \(\mathbb C\) the center has one such one-dimensional component for each irreducible character, and all eigenvalues are in \(K\), so the simultaneous character decomposition of the center is already split over \(K\). Factor each original class-sum matrix characteristic polynomial over this field to list its possible eigenvalues. Split joint eigenspaces by taking intersections of kernels for each new possible eigenvalue. At each stage there are at most as many nonzero joint eigenspaces as the number of classes. For each nonzero list \((b_i)_i\), recover the positive integer \(\chi(1)\) from character orthogonality using
\[
 \chi(1)^2\sum_i \frac{|b_i|^2}{|C_i|}=|G|.
\]
Then obtain all \(\chi(g_i)\) from the scalar formula. All matrices have dimensions at most \(|G|\), the cyclotomic field has degree at most \(|G|\), and the characteristic polynomials are computed from integer matrices whose entries are bounded in terms of \(|G|\). Polynomial factorization over number fields gives polynomial-size eigenvalue representations. In the refinement, each joint eigenspace is computed from the original matrices and the selected eigenvalues rather than from a succession of basis changes. Exact field linear algebra has polynomial bit cost by working with the rational structure constants of the cyclotomic basis. This gives the entire ordinary character table in deterministic polynomial table time. [Lenstra, polynomial factorization over number fields](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/2541/2541D.pdf) [Class-sum and character algorithms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14790)

### Constructing a monomial inducing pair with the same field

Given an irreducible \(\chi\), first quotient by its kernel, which is read from the equality \(\chi(g)=\chi(1)\). On this quotient the faithful irreducible character has cyclic center, since every central element acts as a scalar. If the quotient is abelian, the character is already linear. Otherwise an odd-prime noncyclic \(p\)-group has a normal elementary abelian subgroup \(E\) of rank two. One can find \(E\) in polynomial table time by trying pairs and checking their order, independence, commutation and normality. It cannot lie in the cyclic center. The field \(\mathbb Q(\chi)\) contains \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_p)\), from the value of \(\chi/\chi(1)\) at a central element of order \(p\). All linear characters of \(E\) therefore take values in \(\mathbb Q(\chi)\). [Bender–Glauberman, Proposition 4.6 and Lemma 4.7](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/53612/1/Helmut%20Bender.pdf)

Choose a linear constituent \(\eta\) of \(\chi|_E\) by taking inner products with the \(|E|\) linear characters. Its inertia group \(T\) is proper. If \(\eta\) were invariant under the full group, Clifford theory would make \(\chi|_E\) a multiple of one linear character, which would make all of \(E\) scalar and hence central in the faithful quotient. For \(g\in T\), the trace of the action on the \(\eta\)-isotypic subspace is
\[
 \theta(g)=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{a\in E}\overline{\eta(a)}\chi(ag).
\]
Indeed the average in this formula is the character projector onto that subspace, which commutes with the action of \(T\). The Clifford correspondence makes \(\theta\) irreducible and gives \(\operatorname{Ind}_T^G\theta=\chi\) on the current quotient. The displayed formula gives \(\mathbb Q(\theta)\subseteq\mathbb Q(\chi)\), and induction gives the reverse inclusion. Recurse with \((T,\theta)\), factoring out its own kernel when needed. Each nontrivial descent uses a proper subgroup, so there are at most \(\log_2|G|\) stages. Lift back through all kernel quotients and use transitivity of induction. The output is a subgroup \(J\leq G\) and a linear character \(\xi\) of \(J\) such that \(\operatorname{Ind}_J^G\xi=\chi\) and \(\mathbb Q(\xi)=\mathbb Q(\chi)\). All stages use table operations and sums and products in the original cyclotomic field. Along the descent the denominators grow by at most one factor of \(|E|\) at a stage, and multiplication by a root of unity uses a fixed cyclotomic basis with polynomial-size coefficients, so the bit lengths remain polynomial. [Ford's same-field induction theorem](https://doi.org/10.2307/2046653)

### Exact costs for central hyperplanes

For each irreducible \(\chi\) nontrivial on the central socle \(S=Z(G)[p]\), let \(\lambda_\chi\) be the functional corresponding to its scalar character on \(S\). The subgroup \(J\) of the inducing pair contains \(S\), since induction from \(J\) would vanish at any central element not in \(J\), whereas an irreducible character is nonzero on central elements. On \(S\), the inducing character is precisely the scalar character \(\chi/\chi(1)\). Thus \(L=\ker\xi\) has \(L\cap S=\ker\lambda_\chi\) and
\[
 [G:L]=[G:J]|\xi(J)|=\frac{p}{p-1}[\mathbb Q(\chi):\mathbb Q]\,\chi(1).
\]
For the last equality, the image of \(\xi\) is cyclic of nontrivial prime-power order, and the same-field condition identifies its cyclotomic degree with the character field degree. The integer index can be computed directly from \(L\).

Conversely, let \(L'\) have \(L'\cap S=W\) a hyperplane. The central group \(S/W\) acts freely on \(G/L'\), so the sector of the permutation representation where it acts nontrivially has dimension \((p-1)[G:L']/p\). That sector contains at least one irreducible \(\chi\) nontrivial on \(S/W\). Since the permutation character is rational-valued, it contains the entire Galois orbit of \(\chi\) with positive multiplicity. Those characters all belong to the same nontrivial sector. Hence
\[
 \frac{p-1}{p}[G:L']\ \geq\ [\mathbb Q(\chi):\mathbb Q]\,\chi(1).
\]
Thus for every \(W\), the least index is exactly the smallest constructed index among the irreducibles with \(\ker\lambda_\chi=W\). Apply the minimum-weight basis step from Section 2 and output the corresponding coset actions. This gives actual permutation actions of minimum degree in polynomial time, including for odd-prime groups of unbounded class. The Sylow product argument completes the stated nilpotent extension. This is a paper proof.
