# One-orbit recovery for odd-order groups and two extensions

The proofs below are informal. They address Mixon and Vose's real one-orbit conjecture and their questions about noisy observations and non-isometric linear actions. [Mixon and Vose, Conjecture 2.7 and §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S2)

## One real Gram graph recovers every finite odd-order group

For every finite group \(G\) of odd order and every faithful real orthogonal representation of \(G\), one generic orbit Gram graph determines the actual multiplication table and regular action on its vertices, after any vertex has been chosen as identity. It therefore determines \(G\) up to isomorphism even against competing faithful real orthogonal representations of arbitrary dimension on their own generic loci. There is a deterministic polynomial-time reconstruction from the exact matrix in the real arithmetic model. This settles the odd-order case of Mixon and Vose's Conjecture 2.7, including representations in which different inverse pairs yield the same Gram color. [Mixon and Vose, Theorems 2.5–2.6 and Conjecture 2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S2)

### Recovering a normal subgroup and the quotient law

Let \(X=Gv\) be the free generic orbit, with vertices labeled temporarily by \(g\mapsto gv\) and with \(v\) chosen as identity. Write \(L_g,R_g\) for left and right translations, so \(R_gR_h=R_{hg}\). We first recall the core argument to specify exactly what the decoder can read from the matrix.

On a common generic locus the quadratic forms of distinct symmetric operators \(h+h^{-1}\) are distinct at \(v\). For a fixed vertex \(gv\), all its neighbors \(ghv\) of any one Gram color have a common value of \(h+h^{-1}\). Each \(ghv\) has the mate \(gh^{-1}v\) in the same color. The sum of each such pair is twice the centroid of the color class. Thus the Gram graph determines the permutation \(J_g:k\mapsto gk^{-1}g\), by reflecting each neighbor about its color centroid. Equality to the specified sum is a test on the squared norm of a linear combination of observed vectors, so this uses only the Gram matrix. Write \(D_g=J_gJ_1=L_gR_g\).

The subgroup \(\mathcal D\) generated by the \(D_g\)'s is the image of
\[
 \{(a,b)\in G\times G:ab\in G'\}
\]
under \((a,b)\mapsto L_aR_{b^{-1}}\). Indeed, the generating pairs are \((g,g^{-1})\), and they generate both commutator factors by taking
\((g,g^{-1})(h,h^{-1})(gh,(gh)^{-1})^{-1}=(1,g^{-1}h^{-1}gh)\)
and exchanging factors. Their images on the abelianized factors give all pairs with inverse abelian classes.

A permutation \(L_aR_{b^{-1}}\) preserves the Gram colors exactly when \(b\) centralizes every symmetric operator \(h+h^{-1}\). The span of these operators is the self-adjoint part of the real image algebra of \(G\). The only real simple matrix-algebra type in which the commutant of the self-adjoint part can exceed the center is \(M_1(\mathbb H)\). In matrix size at least two, diagonal projections force any centralizer element to be diagonal, real symmetric off-diagonal matrices force equal diagonal entries, and the self-adjoint matrices with arbitrary conjugate entries off diagonal force that common entry to be central. The size-one real and complex factors are already commutative. No quaternionic irreducible occurs in odd order, because squaring permutes the group and so the Frobenius–Schur indicator is the ordinary character average, zero for every nontrivial irreducible complex character. Consequently the color-preserving condition is \(b\in Z(G)\). As squaring permutes the odd-order center, the color-preserving permutations within \(\mathcal D\) are exactly \(L_K\), where
\[
 K=G'Z(G).
\]
The enumeration has at most \(|G|^2\) elements and tests their effect on the known colors.

Conjugation by \(J_1\) recovers \(R_K\). Their intersection consists exactly of translations by \(Z(G)\). Indeed \(L_a=R_b\) implies \(a=b\) by evaluation at the identity, and then \(a\) is central. The orbits of \(L_K\) are the cosets of \(K\). The known core descends to these cosets and has operation \(a\triangleright b=2a-b\) in the abelian quotient \(G/K\). Its inversion \(b\mapsto-b\) is \(0\triangleright b\), and doubling \(a\mapsto2a\) is \(a\triangleright0\). Since the quotient is odd, doubling is invertible. Thus \(a+b\) is recovered as \(\operatorname{half}(a)\triangleright(-b)\).

### Recovering all products modulo the center

For two vertices \(g,h\), choose any \(t\) in their computed sum coset in \(G/K\). As permutations of \(X\),
\[
 D_gD_hD_t^{-1}=L_{gh t^{-1}}R_{t^{-1}hg}.
\]
Both displayed translating elements lie in \(K\). Factor the known left-hand side by testing the pairs in \(L_K\times R_K\). If \(L_aR_b=L_{a'}R_{b'}\), then the corresponding two left factors differ by an element of \(L_K\cap R_K\), hence by translation by a central element. It follows that applying any such left factor to \(t\) gives a representative of the correct coset \(ghZ(G)\).

### Resolving the center with directed real labels

Write \(Z=Z(G)\), whose permutations and multiplication are already known. For each \(z\in Z\setminus\{1\}\) of order \(m\), let
\[
 P_z=I-\frac1m\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}L_z^i,\qquad A_z=L_z-L_z^{-1}.
\]
Here the permutations \(L_z^i\) also denote their orthogonal action on the span of the orbit. The map \(P_z\) is the orthogonal projection onto \(W_z=\operatorname{Fix}(L_z)^\perp\), and \(A_z\) is skew-adjoint and commutes with the group. It kills the fixed space and is invertible on \(W_z\): its kernel is the fixed space of \(L_z^2\), which equals that of \(L_z\) since \(m\) is odd. Form the directed two-coordinate label
\[
 B_z(x,y)=\bigl(\langle x,P_z y\rangle,\ \langle x,A_z y\rangle\bigr).
 \tag{1}
\]
Both coordinates are rational linear combinations of entries of the real Gram matrix. They are respectively \(\langle P_z x,P_z y\rangle\) and \(\langle P_z x,A_zP_z y\rangle\), so depend only on the projected points. Merge duplicate projections by comparing the pair label with the diagonal label. Equality of the first coordinate with the common squared norm of the orbit points forces equality of their projections, and if the projections coincide the second coordinate is zero.

For two distinct image operators \(b,c\) of \(G\) on \(W_z\), put \(M=b-c\). If \(\langle u,Mu\rangle=0\) identically in \(u\), then \(M\) is skew-adjoint. Since \(A_z\) commutes with \(M\), the operator \(A_zM\) is then self-adjoint. If \(\langle u,A_zMu\rangle\) also vanishes identically, it follows that \(A_zM=0\). Invertibility of \(A_z\) on \(W_z\) contradicts \(b\ne c\). Thus the two-coordinate quadratic forms distinguish every pair of image operators on a nonempty generic locus of projected seeds. For a fixed representation this genericity can be required simultaneously for every \(z\), since \(P_z\) maps onto \(W_z\).

The projected orbit of the image group is generically free. The argument is the directed-color decoder of Mixon and Vose's complex one-orbit theorem, now applied to the two real coordinates in (1). On the projected orbit, the image group element taking a basepoint \(x_0\) to a vertex \(x\) takes \(y\) to the unique \(y'\) with \(B_z(x,y')=B_z(x_0,y)\). Existence follows from invariance and the group action, and uniqueness follows because the labels from each anchor distinguish all image group elements. This recovers the image action as actual permutations of the projected vertices. [Mixon and Vose, Theorem 2.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S2)

Each original vertex \(g\) specifies its projected action via the image of the projected basepoint. For the product of two vertices, compose these projected actions and test the candidates in the already recovered central coset. If two candidates differ by \(c\in Z\setminus\{1\}\), use the component \(W_c\). It is nonzero because the group acts faithfully on the span of its free orbit, and \(c\) has no nonzero fixed vector there. A generic basepoint has nonzero projection on that component, so the two candidates have different projected images. Exactly one candidate matches all projected products. This reconstructs every product and hence the actual regular action, using only rational arithmetic and comparisons on the given Gram entries. Every enumeration is polynomial in the orbit size, and the argument applies to any competing generic representation since the reconstruction uses only the common Gram graph.

## Stable odd-order group recovery from a noisy Gram matrix

One generic odd-order orbit suffices even when its real Gram matrix is given with a sufficiently small entrywise error. The exact polynomial-time decoder admits an explicit radius determined by the distinct real and projected directed-color gaps and the smallest squared distance between distinct observed vertices. This answers the local stability question in Mixon and Vose's §4 for this class. [Mixon and Vose, §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S4)

Let \(\varepsilon\) be a known upper bound on the absolute error in each real Gram entry. In the true matrix let \(\gamma\) be the least positive distance between distinct real labels, and let \(\delta\) be the least squared distance between distinct orbit vertices. For each \(z\ne1\) in the center, let \(\gamma_z\) be the least distance in the sup norm between distinct two-coordinate labels in (1). Treat a gap for a single-label matrix as infinite. These quantities use the true matrix for the guarantee. The decoder only needs the error bound \(\varepsilon\). For every
\[
 \varepsilon<
 \min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{4},\frac{\delta}{32},\min_{z\ne1}\frac{\gamma_z}{8}\right\}
 \tag{2}
\]
it returns the exact multiplication table and action on the observed vertex indices. The trivial group requires no tests, and an empty minimum over nontrivial central elements is infinite.

First cluster the noisy real entries by joining any pair at distance at most \(2\varepsilon\). Equal true labels lie in the same connected component, and distinct true labels have noisy distance strictly above \(2\varepsilon\), so the true partition is recovered. Within the color class \(C\) of a vertex \(y\) at the anchor \(x\), find the mate by minimizing the estimated squared norm of
\[
 y+y'-\frac2{|C|}\sum_{w\in C}w.
\]
The sum of absolute coefficients is at most four, so the error in this squared norm is at most \(16\varepsilon\). The true mate gives zero, and every other candidate has squared norm at least \(\delta\), since its difference vector is its displacement from the true mate. Thus the minimum identifies the exact mate under (2). The resulting core permutations, \(\mathcal D\), \(L_K,R_K\), the center, and the products modulo the center are now recovered by exact finite computations.

Finally calculate the noisy pairs \(B_z\) using (1) and the recovered center permutations. In each coordinate the absolute values of the coefficients applied to the Gram matrix sum to at most two, so each label error in the sup norm is at most \(2\varepsilon\). Join label pairs with noisy distance at most \(4\varepsilon\). Condition (2) recovers the true directed partitions. The diagonal labels identify duplicate projections, and directed colors recover the exact projected image actions. The central-coset test reconstructs the full product. This applies to matrix-entry errors even if the perturbed matrix is not itself a Gram matrix. The radius is a local guarantee at the specified generic orbit.

## No dimension-free bound for abstract recovery from linear orbits

Without a supplied invariant inner product, there is no fixed number of generic orbit observations that suffices for abstract group recovery in all dimensions. For every \(k\ge1\), the same \(k\) generic point-set orbits in dimension \(4k\) admit both \(C_4\) and \(C_2^2\) as acting groups, with both groups in \(k\) copies of their regular representation. The ambiguity holds on the full open locus of linearly independent orbit frames. More strongly, with absolute real coordinates, no Borel estimator can be correct on a dense open set of seed tuples for every conjugate of both of these group embeddings. This separates the abstract non-isometric case in Mixon and Vose's §4 from the known dimension-free isometric bounds. [Mixon and Vose, Problem 1.1 and §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S4)

In fact let \(G,H\) be any nonisomorphic groups of the same order \(n\). Set \(d=kn\) and use the direct sum of \(k\) regular representations for each. The determinant of the \(d\) orbit vectors of a seed tuple is nonzero on a dense open set: taking the basis vector at the identity in each regular block gives a witness. On this set the observations are a partition of a real basis into \(k\) blocks of \(n\) vectors. Any such partition is realized by a conjugate of either regular-sum representation, by choosing a bijection of each block with the corresponding group and transporting its left translations through the basis identification. The observed orbit tuples in both realizations belong to their open independent-frame locus. This alone proves impossibility if the observations are considered up to common linear coordinate change, or if a competing generic tuple is taken on this specified full open locus.

For the stronger coordinate statement, fix orderings of the basis slots and write \(D(X)\) for the \(k\) sets of columns in an invertible frame \(X\in\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbb R)\). Let \(C_G\) be the linear centralizer of the fixed regular-sum embedding of \(G\) in \(\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbb R)\). For each \(X_0\), the frames \(X_0C_G\) give observations from the fixed embedded group \(X_0GX_0^{-1}\). The map from \(C_G\) to the \(k\) seed vectors, taking the identity column in each block, is a homeomorphism onto the basis-generating locus for the original embedding: all other columns are their regular translates, and conversely those translates determine the unique equivariant frame. After multiplying by \(X_0\) it gives the corresponding homeomorphism for the conjugate embedding.

Suppose a Borel estimator of the abstract group were correct on some dense open set of seed tuples for every conjugate of \(G\) and of \(H\). The set
\[
 E_G=\{X\mid\text{the estimator on }D(X)\text{ returns the isomorphism class of }G\}
\]
is Borel and is comeagre on each coset \(X_0C_G\), meaning its complement in that coset is a countable union of nowhere dense sets. The subgroup \(C_G\) is closed, and the projection \(\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbb R)\to\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbb R)/C_G\) admits local product charts with fiber \(C_G\). The Kuratowski–Ulam theorem in each chart implies that \(E_G\) is comeagre in the whole frame space. The same argument makes \(E_H\) comeagre, using its own centralizer. These sets are disjoint because the two abstract groups are nonisomorphic, contradicting the Baire theorem. Thus the ambiguity prevents every Borel reconstruction method from achieving the stated per-group generic guarantee. In particular it prevents every real arithmetic-and-comparison estimator with a countable decision procedure.
