# Seven object types for comparing tensor, group and graph isomorphism

These are proposed working names. The definitions build on tensor diagrams, length functions, groupoids, filtered quivers, quiver representations, and equivariant diagrams. No priority claim is made for the names, constructions or the elementary transport theorem below.

## What counts as inclusion

A family is native when its original data, together with fixed empty, zero, identity or single-object defaults, gives a fully faithful embedding. This means that *every* ordinary homomorphism extends uniquely and *every* homomorphism between the embedded objects comes from an ordinary one. It implies reflection of isomorphisms. Exclusion refers to the absence of a primitive in the indicated presentation. It does not assert the impossibility of another faithful encoding. Groups have multiplication tables and graphs have adjacency tensors. The profile would depend on the encoding if arbitrary recoding counted.

Use sets and small groupoids in the definitions. For finite algorithmic problems take explicit finite graphs and groupoids, rational weights, and finite-dimensional spaces over a fixed effective field. Fix a finite tensor signature for each comparison. Arbitrary tensor orders are covered by varying this signature. The ordinary tensor maps below are covariant restrictions, the group maps are homomorphisms, and graph maps preserve edges. Strict isomorphisms of groupoids are used here. Categorical equivalence would give a different isomorphism problem.

## The seven definitions

1. **Tensystem** (tensors). Specify named vector spaces and named linear maps from tensor products of some of these spaces into tensor products of others. The empty product is the field. A morphism gives one linear map per space. For each named operation, applying the output maps after the operation must equal applying the input maps before its target operation. An ordinary tensor is a single operation from the field into its ordered product of modes. A multiplication and a bilinear form on the same vector space form a genuinely larger example. The same change of basis must respect both, a constraint lost by classifying the operations separately. A nonlinear update rule is outside this definition.

2. **Reverscale** (groups). Use a groupoid with a nonnegative cost on its arrows. Identities cost zero, inversion preserves cost, and composition costs at most the sum of its factors. A morphism is a cost-nonincreasing functor. A group has one object and zero costs, giving exactly its ordinary homomorphisms. A genuinely larger example is the system of distance-preserving bijections between overlapping measurement patches, with identities, inverses and compositions included. One can assign zero costs or symmetric shortest-word costs from a generating family. The existence and cost of transporting one local frame to another are meaningful. An irreversible transition has no place as a groupoid arrow. Invertibility refers to the arrow itself, including its source and target.

3. **Linkscale** (graphs). Use a directed multigraph with a nonnegative threshold on each edge. A morphism maps vertices and edges, preserving both endpoints and never increasing an edge threshold. At level t the visible graph contains exactly the edges whose thresholds are at most t. A simple undirected graph has two oriented arcs per edge and zero thresholds. With simple graphs on both sides the arc map is uniquely determined by the vertex map. Weighted transition networks and parallel links are larger examples. Every homomorphism sends a path visible at t to a path visible at t, so minimum connection thresholds cannot increase. An irreducible ternary incidence has no single edge of this type.

4. **Actitensor** (groups and tensors). Use a Reverscale B and a Tensystem at each object. Every arrow in B transports the whole system by a linear isomorphism, with identity and composition respected. A morphism consists of a cost-nonincreasing functor and local Tensystem maps commuting with all transports. At a one-object base this includes a group representation preserving any given family of multilinear operations. It includes a pure tensor at the terminal base and a pure group with zero vector spaces and zero operations. It also describes an algebra of observables whose multiplication is preserved as frames change. A general linear dissipative update cannot itself be a base symmetry. There is no primitive graph adjacency.

5. **Transportensor** (tensors and graphs). On a Linkscale put a Tensystem at each vertex and one linear map per space along every edge. Edge maps can be singular. They need not carry the tensor attached to their source to the tensor at their target. This allows comparisons of independently measured tensors. A morphism gives a Linkscale map and Tensystem maps at vertices. Each edge square commutes, including noninvertible edge maps. One isolated vertex gives the tensor inclusion. Zero spaces and zero operations give the graph inclusion. Examples include quiver representations with observed tensors at their vertices and networks of linear sensors. A nonlinear transfer function on arbitrary real vectors is excluded as a primitive edge map. For any path the commuting squares imply a commuting square for the composite. A loop at a vertex therefore yields a similarity invariant per mode under an isomorphism fixing that loop.

6. **Symmetrinet** (groups and graphs). Use a Reverscale B carrying a Linkscale at each object, functorially under its arrows. Arrows of B act by threshold-preserving graph isomorphisms. Morphisms consist of a base functor and graph maps commuting with all those actions. This recovers ordinary graphs at the terminal base and ordinary groups with a one-vertex edgeless graph. Examples include filtered covering graphs with a specified deck-group action and finite group actions on sets, viewed as edgeless graphs. On the same square graph, a half-turn and reflection in an axis through opposite edge midpoints give actions of the same order-two group. The latter fixes two undirected edges setwise, the former none, so the mixed objects are nonisomorphic. A permutation that fails to preserve incidence cannot act as a symmetry. Linear data is absent.

7. **Equitransportensor** (all three). Use a Reverscale carrying a Transportensor at every base object, functorially under its arrows. A morphism consists of a base functor and local Transportensor maps intertwining the actions. Thus the map of a space along an edge followed by a change of frame equals the change of frame at the source followed by the map along the transformed edge. This square couples all three ingredients. Examples include periodic linear networks with local interaction tensors and nonfaithful actions with physically invisible group elements. A chosen graph permutation and independently chosen changes of basis fail to define an action when any of these squares fails.

Identity maps and composition in all seven types are componentwise. Inverses in the relevant category give the definition of isomorphism. For costs and thresholds, invertibility forces exact equality. Pure group embeddings use the one-object groupoid with zero costs. Pure graph embeddings use the terminal groupoid whenever a base is needed. In a type with both graphs and tensor data use one isolated vertex for a tensor and zero vector spaces for a graph. Use one isolated vertex with zero spaces wherever both defaults are required. Between two copies of each default there is exactly one morphism. These facts establish full faithfulness directly. More generally Actitensors embed into Equitransportensors by one isolated vertex per base object, Symmetrinets by zero spaces, and Transportensors by the terminal base. The same default embeddings connect the one-ingredient types to the two-ingredient types. All such claims use a common field and a common tensor signature.

## A first theorem for the mixed theory

Fix an isomorphism of the underlying base groupoids and graphs respecting the action, the costs and the thresholds. Identify source and target combinatorial data along it. Include both ordinary graph edges and arrows taking a vertex to its translate under a base-groupoid arrow. This gives a graph on pairs consisting of a base object and a vertex in its local network. Give its arrows the specified linear transports in each mode. Assume all ordinary edge transports are invertible. Symmetry arrows are already invertible.

**Root transport theorem.** In each connected component of this graph, choose one root and a spanning tree. Isomorphisms above the specified combinatorial identification correspond bijectively to tuples of invertible mode maps at the roots satisfying two tests. First, they intertwine every fundamental closed walk, one per non-tree arrow. Second, they carry every named vertex operation pulled back to the root of its component to the corresponding target operation. The theorem includes cycles coming from vertex stabilizers. Reverse traversal uses inverse transport.

**Proof.** Write P_v and P'_v for the tuples of source and target transports along the tree walk from the root to v. If A is the root tuple, every vertex tuple is forced to be A_v=P'_v A P_v^{-1}, mode by mode. This satisfies the tree equations. For a non-tree arrow e from v to w, the equation that A_w intertwines its transport with A_v becomes precisely the root intertwining equation for the loop with transport P_w^{-1} L_e P_v. Pulling a named multilinear operation back to the root means applying the output tree transports inversely and the input tree transports forward. Its local intertwining equation is exactly the second root test. All naturality and edge conditions have now been imposed, so the reconstructed tuples define an isomorphism. Its restriction to the roots is the initial tuple, proving uniqueness and the converse.

For an explicitly listed finite graph and base groupoid, the tree transports and loop matrices can be constructed using polynomially many field operations for fixed tensor signature. This reduces the number of independent changes of basis to one tuple per component. Deciding which graph or base isomorphism to use, or solving simultaneous equivalence of the resulting root tensors, can still be difficult. A succinctly presented symmetry group requires its own complexity analysis.

## Where this builds on earlier mathematics

- [Jacob Turner, A finite-tame-wild trichotomy theorem for tensor diagrams](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001870820302395) develops the theory of representations of tensor diagrams.
- [Harm Derksen and Jerzy Weyman, Quiver representations](https://www.math.utah.edu/~ptrapa/math-library/knutson/derksen-weyman-quiver-JAMS.pdf) use the commuting-arrow definition of a morphism of quiver representations.
- [Cluster Persistence for Weighted Graphs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10743168/) studies mathematical information recovered from weighted graph filtrations.

The research question these definitions isolate is whether tensor data and loop transport jointly make the underlying graph or base symmetry easier to recover on identifiable families. A formalism and its elementary tree reduction give a starting point for that question.
