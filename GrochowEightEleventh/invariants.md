# Even-order Gram recovery and ordinary dihedral cubic invariants

The results below are informal proofs.

## One-orbit recovery for even groups with an odd residual quotient

Let a finite group $G$ act faithfully on a real inner-product space by orthogonal maps. Suppose this representation has no irreducible of quaternionic type and real dimension four. Put $I=\langle g\in G:g^2=1\rangle$ and $K=G'Z(G)^2I$. If $G/K$ has odd order, a single generic real orbit Gram graph determines the group's multiplication table and regular action on its vertices, even against arbitrary competing faithful real orthogonal representations on their own generic loci. The oddness hypothesis holds if $G/G'$ has odd order, or if a Sylow 2-subgroup is generated by involutions. Generic here means on a dense open set. [Mixon and Vose, Conjecture 2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17434#S2)

### Recognizing the quaternionic exception from the graph

Realize the orbit $X$ in its Euclidean span. The positive covariance eigenspaces and the projected Gram matrices are known from the given graph. Indeed the Gram matrix projected to the covariance eigenspace of positive eigenvalue $\lambda$ is $\lambda$ times the corresponding spectral projector of the original Gram matrix on the vertex-index space.

Each positive covariance eigenspace is generically one real irreducible, even with multiplicities. In one isotype, write the irreducible as $U=\mathbb D^d$ for $\mathbb D=\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ or $\mathbb H$, using an orthonormal model in which the group acts on the left. Write its $m$ seed vectors as columns of $X_0\in M_{d,m}(\mathbb D)$; the commuting algebra acts on the right. The covariance acts by right multiplication by the Hermitian matrix $|G|X_0^\dagger X_0/\dim_{\mathbb R}U$. To see this, pair the covariance with right multiplication by a Hermitian matrix $C$ via the real operator trace. The resulting value is $|G|\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}(X_0^\dagger X_0 C)$. The trace pairing of two such right multipliers is the matrix pairing times $\dim_{\mathbb R}U$, proving the formula. Off-diagonal blocks across isotypes vanish by equivariance. The positive singular values are generically distinct within one isotype by perturbing singular values, and distinct between isotypes by independent rescaling. Each positive eigenspace is therefore one $U$, with projected seed represented by $X_0w$ for a corresponding unit right singular vector $w$.

For a four-dimensional positive eigenspace, normalize the projected Gram values by the common squared norm. If the factor is quaternionic, its image algebra is $M_1(\mathbb H)$. Symmetric parts are scalar and the operators have orders dividing $N=|X|$, so every normalized value lies in the finite palette $\{\cos(2\pi j/N):0\le j<N\}$. Otherwise the algebra is $M_4(\mathbb R)$ or $M_2(\mathbb C)$. Its self-adjoint part is spanned by symmetrized group operators and contains nonscalars. The normalized quadratic value of any such nonscalar avoids the finite palette on a dense open set of directions. On the simple-spectrum locus, replacing $X_0$ by $QX_0$ for nearby unitary $Q$ over $\mathbb D$ leaves $X_0^\dagger X_0$ unchanged and perturbs $X_0w$ over an open set of directions. Thus on a dense open set every nonquaternionic four-dimensional block has some label outside the palette. Each isotype is active for a generic seed. This intrinsic test ensures that a competing generic representation also has no exceptional constituent.

### Recovering the common actions and quotient

A generic orbit is free and the real quadratic forms of distinct symmetric operators $h+h^{-1}$ take distinct values at its seed. In any one neighbor color at $gv$, pairs $ghv,gh^{-1}v$ sum to the common value $g(h+h^{-1})v$. Reflection about the centroid of the color class therefore recovers the core permutations $J_g:k\mapsto gk^{-1}g$. Choose one vertex as identity, and write $D_g=J_gJ_1=L_gR_g$ with $L,R$ the left and right translations.

The group generated by $D_g$ is the image of $\{(a,b)\in G\times G:ab\in G'\}$ under $(a,b)\mapsto L_aR_{b^{-1}}$: the pairs $(g,g^{-1})$ generate both commutator factors and the required image in the abelianized factors. A permutation in this image preserves all colors precisely when $b$ centralizes all $h+h^{-1}$. Those span the self-adjoint part of the real image algebra. In every simple factor except $M_1(\mathbb H)$, a centralizer of the self-adjoint part is central, by testing diagonal projections and self-adjoint off-diagonal units with arbitrary conjugate entries. By faithfulness $b\in Z(G)$. Thus the color-preserving subgroup of the generated permutation group is exactly $L_{G'Z(G)^2}$. [Earlier core calculation](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/GrochowEightNinth/invariants.md#one-generic-real-gram-graph-recovers-core-operations-and-a-canonical-subgroup-action)

A one-neighbor off-diagonal color corresponds precisely to an involution. A non-involution shares a color with its inverse, and an involution's symmetric part cannot equal that of another orthogonal operator by equality in Cauchy–Schwarz on its $1$ and $-1$ eigenspaces. Its one-neighbor matching is right translation by the involution. These matchings generate $R_I$, and conjugation by $J_1$ gives $L_I$. This, together with the computed $L_{G'Z(G)^2}$ and its inversion, gives $L_K,R_K$. Their intersection consists of translations by $Z(G)\cap K$, whose odd-order elements are exactly $Z(G)_{\rm odd}$, because every odd-order central element is a central square.

The $K$-cosets are the orbits of $L_K$. On $A=G/K$ the core is $a\triangleright b=2a-b$. Oddness allows reconstruction of its addition using $\operatorname{half}(a)\triangleright(-b)$. Conjugation by $D_g$ on $R_K$ sends $R_k$ to $R_{g^{-1}kg}$, recovering the conjugation action on $K$. A competing $H$ thus has the same actual $L_K,R_K$, the same law whenever either factor lies in $K$, the same quotient with its law, and the same conjugation actions on $K$.

### Shared data permit only an odd central product correction

In $G\times H$, take the subgroup of pairs with the same quotient label in $A$ and the same conjugation action on $K$. Its diagonal $\Delta K$ is normal. In its quotient $P$ by $\Delta K$, the kernel over $A$ identifies with $Z(K)$ via $(1,c)\Delta K$: any pair in the kernel belongs to $K\times K$ and differs by a central element of $K$. The two copies of a common vertex in coset $a$ define a well-defined section value $s(a)$, independent of representative because the two left actions of $K$ agree. The shared core and inversion give
$$
 s(a)s(b)s(a)=s(2a+b).
$$
In particular $s(0)=1$, and induction in steps of two gives $s(ja)=s(a)^j$. Every section element has odd order.

Let $F$ be the subgroup of $P$ generated by the section. A sandwich map $x\mapsto s(a)x s(a)$ acts on the section by translation by $2a$. The commutator of two sandwich maps fixes the section pointwise. It is a product of a left and a right translation fixing the identity, so it is conjugation by its left factor $[s(a),s(b)]$. This commutator centralizes the generators of $F$, so $F$ has class at most two. The derived subgroup is generated by these central pairwise commutators, whose orders divide the orders of the section elements. Hence both $F'$ and $F/F'$ have odd order. In $F/F'$ the sandwich equation and $s(2b)=s(b)^2$ give $2s(a+b)=2s(a)+2s(b)$ in additive notation. Invertibility of doubling shows that every section product defect is central in $F$ and of odd order.

Given vertices $g,h$, let their products in the two laws be $u,u'$. They have the same quotient and conjugation action on $K$. The corresponding section product defect is represented by $(1,u'u^{-1})$, with the second product taken in $H$. This element of $Z(K)$ has odd order and is invariant under conjugation by every section representative, by centrality of the defect in $F$. It is consequently in $Z(H)_{\rm odd}$, which is the shared recovered group of odd central translations. Thus the two product labels differ only by an odd central translation.

For each known odd central $z\ne1$ of order $m$, let $P_z=I-m^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}L_z^i$ and $A_z=L_z-L_z^{-1}$. The directed pair label $(\langle x,P_z y\rangle,\langle x,A_z y\rangle)$ is computed from the common Gram matrix. It depends only on the projected points in $W_z=\operatorname{Fix}(z)^\perp$. On that space $A_z$ is skew-adjoint, commutes with the group and is invertible, since $z^2$ has the same fixed space as $z$. For two distinct image operators with difference $M$, the quadratic forms of $M,A_zM$ cannot both vanish identically: the first condition makes $M$ skew-adjoint, so $A_zM$ is self-adjoint, and the second would give $M=0$. Thus these labels generically distinguish image elements. The directed-color rule matches outgoing labels at a vertex to those at a basepoint and recovers the actual image action on the projected orbit. This is computed identically for both laws. [Odd-order reconstruction](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/GrochowEightTenth/invariants.md#one-real-gram-graph-recovers-every-finite-odd-order-group)

Any product difference by an odd central $c\ne1$ is detected on $W_c$, where $c$ has no nonzero fixed vector and a generic seed has nonzero projection. This rules out all differences, proving the claimed full recovery. For example $S_3\times H$ for any odd-order group $H$ meets the hypotheses in every faithful real representation. Its Sylow 2-subgroup has order two, and its complex irreducibles are tensor products of real-type irreducibles of $S_3$ with real trivial or complex-type irreducibles of $H$, so none is quaternionic.

## Exact ordinary dihedral invariant degree over every infinite field

For the natural action of the dihedral group $D_n$ on the vertices of the $n$-gon over any infinite field, the minimum degree of ordinary polynomial invariants generating the invariant field and the minimum degree generically separating geometric orbits coincide. For $n\ge3$ both are three, except for $n=5$ in characteristic different from five, when both are four. For $n=1,2$ they are respectively one and two. The new case here is characteristic two and even $n\ge4$. The previous unnormalized group-sum tensor cutoffs are higher in this case, so they do not imply the ordinary cubic claim. [Edidin and Katz, §5.2 and the distinction between tensors and polynomial invariants](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09599v4#S5.SS2)

Work first over an algebraic closure of a field of characteristic two. Write $n=2m$, $t_i=x_i$, $s_i=x_i+x_{i+m}$ for the coordinates of the opposite pairs, and $N_i=t_i(t_i+s_i)$ for their products. The central half-turn sends $t_i\mapsto t_i+s_i$; $s$ is fixed under the half-turn and carries the natural quotient $D_m$-action. Every ordinary invariant on $s$ pulls back to one of the same degree. For the square $n=4$, call the opposite-pair sums $s,t$ and the products $p,q$. The invariants $s+t,st,p+q,sp+tq$ have degree at most three. They recover first the two sums up to exchange, then their corresponding products, then the pairs themselves, by generically separable quadratics and an invertible linear system. The ambiguity is exactly the dihedral action.

### Doubling an odd polygon

Suppose $m\ge3$ is odd. Use $D_{2m}=D_m\times C_2$ and the Fourier transform for the order-$m$ rotation on the two rows. Use $t_i,s_i$ now for their Fourier coordinates, with $i$ a frequency modulo $m$. Here $t_i$ and $s_i$ have weight $i$ for rotation, reflection interchanges $i,-i$, and the half-turn sends $t_i\mapsto t_i+s_i$. In this paragraph define $N_i=t_i(t_i+s_i)$ in these Fourier coordinates, so it has weight $2i$. Write $[f]$ for $f$ plus its reflection.

The ordinary quotient cubics determine $s$ up to $D_m$ except at $m=5$, where there are two geometric quotient orbits distinguished by a quartic. The invariant $N_0$, together with $s_0$, determines $t_0$ up to the central half-turn. Fix its choice. For every nonzero pair $i,-i$, put $p_i=s_i s_{-i}$ and
$$
 B_i=s_i t_{-i}+s_{-i}t_i,\qquad
 F_i=t_0(B_i+p_i)+s_0t_i t_{-i}.
$$
Both are invariant under the full group. The first is reflection-invariant, of weight zero and fixed by the half-turn. The change of $t_i t_{-i}$ under the half-turn is $B_i+p_i$, which cancels the change of the first summand of $F_i$. Thus these are respectively quadratic and cubic ordinary invariants. For known $s_0,t_0,p_i$ they recover $t_i t_{-i}$ and the unordered pair of normalized ratios $v_i=t_i/s_i,v_{-i}=t_{-i}/s_{-i}$, using their product and sum. Generically this quadratic is separable.

The cubic $[N_i s_{-2i}]$ is also an ordinary invariant. Its value is
$$
 s_i^2s_{-2i}(v_i^2+v_i)+s_{-i}^2s_{2i}(v_{-i}^2+v_{-i}).
$$
The two coefficients are generically distinct, since they are distinct reflected monomials of odd degree in nonzero frequencies. Generically $v_i^2+v_i\ne v_{-i}^2+v_{-i}$ as well. Therefore, if $s$ is fixed, the unordered ratio pair is uniquely oriented. This proves recovery for odd $m\ne5$. [Earlier odd-polygon result](dihedral-background.md#sharp-dihedral-moment-cutoffs-in-characteristic-two)

For $m=5$, the quotient quadratic products $p_1,p_2$ and the two cubic sums determine the oriented pairs
$$
 (a_1,b_1)=(s_1^2s_{-2},s_{-1}^2s_2),\qquad
 (a_2,b_2)=(s_2^2s_1,s_{-2}^2s_{-1})
$$
up to independent exchanges. Choosing both orientations determines $s$ up to rotation. If the $i$-th orientation changes then the value $[N_i s_{-2i}]$ forces the corresponding unordered ratio pair to change orientation as well, by the nonzero difference just proved. But another ordinary cubic invariant is
$$
 [ (s_1t_{-2}+s_{-2}t_1)s_1]
 =a_1(v_1+v_{-2})+b_1(v_{-1}+v_2).
$$
If exactly one of the two orientations changes, its value changes by
$(a_1+b_1)(v_2+v_{-2})$, generically nonzero. Thus only simultaneous exchange is possible, and that is the group reflection. This completes the odd-$m$ cases.

### Doubling an even polygon

Return to ordinary vertex coordinates and suppose $m\ge4$ is even. By induction the $s$-configuration is recovered up to $D_m$ from ordinary cubics. Choose a representative. The following additional degree-two and degree-three invariants recover $t$ up to the central half-turn.

Let $z_{ij}=s_i t_j+s_j t_i$, fixed by the half-turn. For a distance $d\ne0,m/2$ modulo $m$, take the ordinary quotient orbit-sum of $z_{0d}$ over unordered edges of that shape. It is an invariant quadratic: the stabilizer of $\{0,d\}$ is generated by its swapping reflection, which lifts to a reflection fixing $z_{0d}$ since it swaps $x_0,x_d$ exactly. Take also the full quotient trace of $z_{0d}s_0$, an invariant cubic. In a quotient lift, $t_i$ goes to $t_{q(i)}+\epsilon s_{q(i)}$ for a known bit $\epsilon$. Subtracting the terms involving $s$ only, these give linear measurements in $t$ with homogeneous parts
$$
 \begin{split}
 B_d&=\sum_r s_r(t_{r+d}+t_{r-d}),\\
 E_d&=\sum_r s_r\bigl(s_r(t_{r+d}+t_{r-d})+(s_{r+d}+s_{r-d})t_r\bigr).
 \end{split}
$$
Indeed the first sum counts each unordered edge once, and the second sums the two orientations at each marked endpoint. Every homogeneous row kills the vector $s$.

To establish enough rank, specialize $s_0=x,s_1=y$ with $x,y\ne0$ and $x\ne y$, and all other $s_i=0$. Subtract a multiple of $s$ from a kernel vector to set $t_0=0$, and put $t_1=a$. For $d\ne\pm1,0,m/2$, the two equations $B_d=E_d=0$ separate the centered relations $t_{-d}=t_d$ and $t_{1-d}=t_{1+d}$ since their coefficients in the two equations are proportional to $x,y$ and $x^2,y^2$. The relations are tautological for $d=m/2$. For $d=1$ the two equations read
$$
 x(a+t_{m-1})+y t_2=0,\qquad
 x^2(a+t_{m-1})+y^2t_2+xy a=0.
$$
Thus $t_2=t_{m-1}=b_0a$ with $b_0=x/(x+y)$. The two centered relations together give $t_{i+2}=t_i$ away from the excluded relations involving $0,1$. Starting at $2$ gives the value $b_0a$ on the remaining even indices; on the odd indices $3,5,\ldots,m-1$ the same relation gives their common value $t_{m-1}=b_0a$. For $m=4$ these statements follow already from the displayed equations. Thus the normalized kernel is exactly the line with $t_1=a$ and all remaining $t_i=b_0a$. This line also satisfies each of the displayed homogeneous rows, so the rank before normalization is exactly $m-2$ at the specialization.

Choose $m-2$ independent rows witnessed by this rank calculation. They and the coordinate constraints at $0,1$ give a generically invertible system, so we may write the unknown configuration as
$$
 t=t'+a v+w s,
$$
where $t'$ and $v$ are known rationally, $t'_0=t'_1=v_0=0,\ v_1=1$, and $s_0\ne0$. At the sparse specialization $v_i=b_0$ for $i\notin\{0,1\}$. Ignore any unused linear measurements.

Now use the three ordinary invariants
$$
 \sum_i N_i,\qquad \sum_i s_i N_i,\qquad
 \sum_i s_{i+m/2}N_i,\qquad N_i=t_i(t_i+s_i).
$$
The quotient permutes the $N_i$'s and preserves the antipodal offset $m/2$, so these are invariant. Their degrees are two, three and three. In characteristic two, for weights $f_i$, the change of $\sum_i f_i N_i$ from its value at $t'$ is
$$
 a^2\sum_i f_i v_i^2+a\sum_i f_i s_i v_i+(w^2+w)\sum_i f_i s_i^2 .
$$
The three measurements form a linear system for $a^2,a,w^2+w$. At the sparse specialization its rows are
$$
 (1,y,x^2+y^2),\quad (y,y^2,x^3+y^3),\quad
 ((x+y)b_0^2,0,0).
$$
For the first row, the number $m-2$ of identical nonzero-index contributions involving $b_0^2$ is even. For the third row the antipodal support is disjoint from $\{0,1\}$. The determinant is nonzero, since the determinant on the last two coordinates of the first two rows is $yx^2(x+y)$. Therefore the system is invertible generically. Recover $a,w^2+w$; the last two choices of $w$ differ by $1$, exactly the central half-turn. This proves the induction for all even $n$.

### Exact degrees and field generation

In characteristic two, the degree-two ordinary invariants are spanned by the linear sum, its square from the one orbit of coordinate squares, and the unordered pair orbit-sums at the $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ distances. Their transcendence degree is at most $1+\lfloor n/2\rfloor<n$ for $n\ge3$. Thus the even cutoffs proved above are sharp. The rational function extension generated by the exhibited invariants is separable: the quotient reconstruction uses separable root equations, and the subsequent lifting uses rational linear solving and separable quadratics, including $w^2+w$ in the even doubling. Its generic fibers are exactly the dihedral orbits, so its degree is the degree of the full fixed-field extension. Consequently the invariant-data field equals the full fixed field. Formation of invariant polynomial spaces commutes with scalar extension by the linear-kernel characterization, and invariant rational functions are fractions of invariant polynomials by clearing denominators with an orbit product. This descends the assertion to the original field.

For the other fields and polygon sizes, the nonmodular cubic result and its five-point exception, the odd-polygon characteristic-two ordinary result, and the odd modular group-sum result give the assertion. At the characteristic-three triangle the elementary symmetric polynomials give ordinary cubic recovery, even though its unnormalized group-sum cutoff is four. At the characteristic-five pentagon the modular cubic result applies. For $n=1,2$ use the elementary symmetric polynomials for the effective action.
