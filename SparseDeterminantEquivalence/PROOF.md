# Sparse determinant equivalence and black-box PIT completeness

Grochow's Open Question VII.2 asks in its second clause whether equivalence to the determinant can be decided in time polynomial in the number of listed nonzero monomials. Over $\mathbb C$, the field of the main theorem in that paper, the answer is yes. The decision algorithm uses ordinary exact field operations and equality tests. It uses neither random evaluations nor a univariate root oracle. It also works for affine equivalence. For rational or Gaussian-rational coefficients, deciding equivalence over $\mathbb C$ takes polynomial bit time. For the black-box clause, a direct reduction via reducible local initial forms proves PIT-hardness, and the same structural algorithm gives a deterministic reduction to PIT without additional root finding. [Grochow, Question VII.2](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~billey/classes/lie/bulletins/grochow.2012.pdf#page=10)

## 1. Geometric test on a sparse homogeneous polynomial

Let $K$ be an input field of characteristic zero, let $\bar K$ be its algebraic closure, and let $P\ne0$ be homogeneous of degree $d$ in $N=d^2$ variables. Write $V=K^N$. The case $d=1$ accepts every nonzero linear form. For $d\ge2$, compute
$$
\mathfrak g=\{B\in\operatorname{End}_K(V):\ (Bx)\cdot\nabla P=0\}.
$$
For every unknown entry of $B$, multiply one sparse partial derivative by one variable, then equate the coefficients of the resulting distinct monomials. This is a polynomial-size linear system in $N^2$ unknowns with no substitutions that expand $P$. The kernel is closed under commutators and its computation commutes with scalar extension. Compute its bracket structure constants by matrix multiplication and linear solves.

The Lie stabilizer of the determinant is exactly the two commuting copies of $\mathfrak{sl}_d$ acting by left and right multiplication. Thus if $P$ is equivalent to the determinant, $\dim\mathfrak g=2(d^2-1)$. Its centroid
$$
C=\{J\in\operatorname{End}_K(\mathfrak g):
J[x,y]=[Jx,y]=[x,Jy]\ \text{for all }x,y\in\mathfrak g\}
$$
is two-dimensional and split over $\bar K$. These assertions follow because each simple summand is perfect and its adjoint module has scalar endomorphisms over $\bar K$. In particular centroid elements preserve the two summands. The equations defining $C$ are linear. Reject if either dimension test fails. Choose $J\in C$ independent of the identity and compute the coefficients in $J^2=aJ+bI$. The centroid contains $I$ and is closed under composition. Reject if the discriminant $a^2+4b$ is zero. The remaining two eigenspaces of $J$ over $\bar K$ are Lie ideals, because $J[x,y]=[x,Jy]$. Denote them by $\mathfrak h_1,\mathfrak h_2$. [Garg–Gupta–Kayal–Saha, Theorem 4 and Observation 2.5](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2019/042/download/)

Use the following test on each $\mathfrak h_i$. Let $A_i$ be the unital associative algebra generated by its matrices and test all of the following:
1. $\dim\mathfrak h_i=d^2-1$ and $\dim A_i=d^2$.
2. $Z(A_i)$ has dimension one and the pairing $(X,Y)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}_V(XY)$ on $A_i$ is nondegenerate.
3. $\operatorname{Tr}_V$ vanishes on $\mathfrak h_i$.

Accept if at least one ideal passes. Every test passes for the two determinant components, since each is conjugate to the traceless part of a copy of the full matrix algebra acting on $d$ copies of its standard module. In characteristic zero the trace pairing on that algebra is a nonzero multiple of the usual full matrix trace pairing.

For soundness, work over $\bar K$ and let $A=A_i$ pass the test. Its Jacobson radical lies in the radical of the tested trace pairing: it is a nilpotent ideal, so $XY$ is nilpotent for every $X$ in the radical and every $Y\in A$. Hence $A$ is semisimple. Its one-dimensional center and its dimension imply $A\simeq M_d(\bar K)$ by the structure theorem for semisimple algebras. The representation on $V$ is conjugate to the action on $d$ copies of its standard module. In these coordinates $\mathfrak h_i$ is exactly the traceless part by dimension, since $\operatorname{Tr}_V(I)=N\ne0$.

In the resulting matrix coordinates $X\in M_d(\bar K)$, $P$ is invariant under each elementary left transvection $X\mapsto (I+tE_{ij})X$. Indeed, the derivative with respect to $t$ is the infinitesimal action of $E_{ij}$ evaluated at $(I+tE_{ij})X$, because $E_{ij}^2=0$. It is zero for every $t$, which implies the required polynomial identity in characteristic zero. The transvections generate $\mathrm{SL}_d$. On invertible matrices the $\mathrm{SL}_d$ orbits are precisely the fibers of determinant, so $P(X)=q(\det X)$ there, with $q(t)=P(\operatorname{diag}(t,1,\ldots,1))$. This equality holds on all matrices by density. The homogeneity of $P$ forces $q(t)=ct$ with $c\ne0$. One fixed row scaling absorbs $c$, proving equivalence.

## 2. Exact computation without choosing a root

Run the two-component computation over $E=K[T]/(T^2-aT-b)$, writing $\tau$ for the class of $T$. The eigenspace $\ker(J-\tau I)$ over $E$ specializes to $\mathfrak h_i$ under the two maps into $\bar K$. A unital associative basis is obtained by starting with $I$, multiplying each accepted basis word by the original generators, and adding independent words until the span is closed under those products. There are at most $N^2$ accepted words. The center and trace conditions are again linear solves, rank tests, and equalities.

Elimination over $E$ requires only exact field arithmetic in $K$. For a candidate pivot represented by a linear polynomial, compute its gcd with $T^2-aT-b$. A unit can be inverted by the extended Euclidean algorithm. A nonzero nonunit gives a linear factor and thus splits $E$ into two explicit copies of $K$. If a split is encountered, resume the computation on the two factors individually. Apply the same zero-divisor check whenever testing a scalar equality, including the traces in condition 3. Without a split, each pivot is valid in both geometric components, or zero in both, and the tested ranks and equality results are identical in the two components. If $E$ is a field, the resulting computation is unchanged by either embedding in $\bar K$, so one branch again suffices. This is a degree-two arithmetic construction without a root oracle. It is also possible to test the simple ideals after explicitly splitting the centroid by a quadratic root computation when that operation is available.

For the bit bound over $\mathbb Q$ or $\mathbb Q(i)$, one can test whether the discriminant is a square by integer square roots after clearing denominators, including the usual real and imaginary part formulas over $\mathbb Q(i)$. If it is a square, split immediately. Otherwise work in a quadratic number field presented with this generator. Clearing denominators and using determinant bounds gives polynomial bit size for the fixed number of layers of linear solves. In the iterative associative span calculation, keep independent original words as basis representatives. Each word length is at most $N^2$ and all products use the original generators, so after fixing common denominators for those generators and the field structure constants, the numerator and denominator bit lengths grow polynomially. Each rank test uses those words rather than recursively incorporating the reduced outputs of earlier rank tests. These bounds and ordinary exact linear algebra give polynomial bit time.

## 3. Reduction of sparse affine equivalence to the same test

For a nonzero sparse polynomial $f$ of total degree $d$, let $P=f_d$ be its top homogeneous part. For equivalence to the $d\times d$ determinant in $N=d^2$ variables, first apply the homogeneous test to $P$. The determinant has $d^2$ linearly independent first derivatives, as can be seen from their disjoint nonzero monomial supports, and linear changes of coordinates preserve this property. Solve for the unique translation $b$ satisfying $D_bP=f_{d-1}$, where $D_bP=b\cdot\nabla P$. Reject if no such $b$ exists. A true identity $f(x)=P(x+b)$ must satisfy this linear equation. Its solution field may be taken to be $K$, since the system has coefficients in $K$ and a unique solution over $\bar K$.

Check $(x+b)\cdot\nabla f=df$ by sparse arithmetic. This is exact: setting $y=x+b$ makes the equation the Euler homogeneity identity for $f(y-b)$. The only allowed homogeneous degree is $d$, so $f(y-b)=P(y)$. Thus acceptance is equivalent to affine determinant equivalence. The case $d=1$ is also covered, with the constant term absorbed by translation. Zero and constant inputs are rejected for the positive-size determinant families.

Every variable must occur in $P$ for a positive instance. This permits a preliminary support check without allocating space indexed by a large binary degree or variable count. If $t$ is the length of the nonzero monomial list, the surviving inputs have $N=d^2\le td$, so $d\le t$ and $N\le t^2$. This proves the polynomial dependence on $t$ in the exact field-operation model and on the encoded list length in the rational bit model. The formulation uses an invertible affine map on the standard $d^2$-dimensional determinant-variable space. If redundant ambient variables are allowed by convention, compute the kernel of the directional derivatives of the input polynomial. In characteristic zero, the polynomial is constant on cosets of this kernel. Choose a complement spanned by a subset of coordinate vectors and set the other coordinates to zero. This restricts to a presentation of the quotient without increasing the monomial count.

## 4. Finite-field extension

If $K$ is a finite field of characteristic $p>d$, the same algorithm decides linear or affine equivalence over $K$ itself. In the determinant case the centroid already splits over $K$, which is required by testing whether its nonzero discriminant has a square root using exponentiation in $K$. After confirming that it splits, the two-component computation from Section 2 applies with the same two geometric components. For soundness, a passing $A_i$ is central simple over $K$, hence is the split matrix algebra by the finite division-ring theorem. To prove invariance as a formal polynomial, one may still extend scalars first: the univariate polynomial in each transvection parameter has degree at most $d<p$, so derivative zero implies constancy. The scalar $c$ in $P(X)=c\det X$ is the nonzero value $P(I)$ in the matrix coordinates over $K$. The Euler check is likewise valid because every degree lies between $0$ and $d<p$. With a standard explicit finite-field representation this uses polynomial bit time in the sparse input length and $\log |K|$.

This approach uses the published infinitesimal determinant stabilizer and its identification of an associative full matrix block. The additional steps give an ordinary deterministic algorithm for sparse input. In particular the general black-box reconstruction of the infinitesimal stabilizer is not needed here. [Grochow, thesis, Corollary 4.6.4 and Remark 4.6.5](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~jgrochow/grochow-thesis.pdf)

## 5. Black-box determinant equivalence is equivalent to PIT

Over a fixed field of characteristic zero, for geometric equivalence the decision problem with a polynomial degree bound is PIT-hard by a deterministic many-one reduction. It reduces back to PIT by a deterministic polynomial-time Turing reduction. In particular, in the complex field-operation model no additional root-finding oracle is needed for decision once PIT is available.

**Hardness.** Given a black box for $h$ in $s$ variables with degree at most $D$, take $D\ge1$ and homogenize it to $H(Y,u)=u^D h(Y/u)$. This black box can be evaluated even at $u=0$: interpolate the polynomial $h(tY)$ at $D+1$ fixed $t$ values, then combine its homogeneous coefficients with the corresponding powers of $u$. Choose $m\ge D+3$ with $m^2\ge s+3$. Use distinct coordinates of the generic $m\times m$ matrix $X$ for $Y,u,w,v$, with $w$ a matrix entry. Define
$$
F(X)=\det X+w^{m-D-1}vH(Y,u).
$$
This is homogeneous of degree $m$. A call to its evaluation oracle takes polynomial time and polynomially many calls to the original oracle. When $h=0$, $F$ is the generic determinant.

Assume $h\ne0$. At the rank-one point $X=E_w$, with $w=1$ and every other entry zero, the lowest nonzero Taylor homogeneous piece of the perturbation is $vH(Y,u)$ of degree $D+1<m-1$. The determinant summand vanishes to order $m-1$, so the lowest nonzero piece of $F$ is exactly this product of two positive-degree forms. It is reducible over the algebraic closure.

At a singular matrix of rank $r<m$, the lowest nonzero Taylor homogeneous piece of $\det_m$ is, after independent invertible row and column changes, the determinant of a generic $(m-r)\times(m-r)$ matrix in the bottom-right block. To see this, reduce the base point to $\operatorname{diag}(I_r,0)$. A determinant term must use perturbation entries in all bottom $m-r$ rows, and a term of just this degree must use all top-row diagonal constants. This initial piece is irreducible, including the linear case. For completeness, the singular-matrix variety for a generic square matrix of size $k$ is irreducible because it is the image of the product of full spaces of $k\times(k-1)$ and $(k-1)\times k$ matrices under multiplication. The determinant has a nonsingular zero at $\operatorname{diag}(I_{k-1},0)$, so it is not a power of the defining irreducible factor. At invertible base points the initial piece is a nonzero constant. These properties of initial pieces are preserved by every invertible affine substitution. The reducible piece at $E_w$ therefore rules out even affine determinant equivalence. This proves the reduction with the exact $m^2$-variable target, including over $\mathbb C$.

**Upper bound with PIT.** Let $P$ be a homogeneous black-box input of polynomially bounded degree. The coefficient-free evaluation of each feature $x_j\partial_iP(x)$ takes polynomially many input evaluations by interpolating $P(x+te_i)$ in $t$. Store evaluation rows of the feature vector at points found so far. Compute a basis of the nullspace of the evaluation rows. For each basis vector $B$, run PIT on its corresponding linear combination of features. If they are all identically zero, the current nullspace is exactly $\mathfrak g$. Otherwise some combination $R$ is nonzero. Specialize one coordinate at a time, using PIT to retain a nonzero specialization among $d+1$ fixed choices, where $d=\deg P$. Such a choice always exists by the degree bound in that coordinate. The resulting point has $R(x)\ne0$, and its evaluation row increases rank. There are at most $N^2$ rank increases. This computes $\mathfrak g$ with polynomially many deterministic PIT calls. Apply Sections 1–2 to finish the homogeneous decision test. In the affine version, compute the top and next homogeneous parts by interpolation on $f(tx)$ and use the same rank-increase procedure to solve the linear identity in Section 3. The final homogeneity check uses one PIT call. Nonzero degree and the actual top degree can likewise be determined with polynomially many PIT calls under the supplied degree bound.

The two reductions distinguish the determinant-equivalence *decision problem* from computing a change of coordinates over the coefficient field. The black-box PIT reduction here tests determinant equivalence itself. Grochow's older PIT equivalence concerns computing the Lie stabilizer of a polynomial. The reducible local initial form supplies the direct reduction to orbit membership. No claim about root-finding hardness for a search version is needed for this decision characterization. [Grochow, Corollary 4.6.4 and Remark 4.6.5](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~jgrochow/grochow-thesis.pdf)


### An unconditional evaluation-query lower bound

For completely unrestricted degree-$m$ black-box input, let $m\ge4$ and consider a deterministic algorithm run on $\det_m$. If it makes $Q$ queries, each query imposes one homogeneous linear vanishing condition on a degree-$(m-3)$ form $H$ in the $m^2-2$ coordinates other than $w,v$. The space of such forms has dimension $\binom{m^2+m-6}{m-3}$. If $Q$ is smaller, there is a nonzero $H$ satisfying all the conditions. The alternative input $\det_m+w^2vH$ gives exactly the same adaptive evaluation transcript. It is a no-instance by the initial-form proof. Hence every deterministic evaluation-only algorithm correct on all such forms needs at least $\binom{m^2+m-6}{m-3}=2^{\Omega(m\log m)}$ queries in the worst case. This dimension argument supplies no small-circuit promise for the adversarial $H$.

The PIT hardness and the sparse algorithm answer both clauses of the arXiv Open Question 6.3. The CCC Open Question VII.2 also names root finding. The decision equivalence above bypasses root finding once a PIT decision oracle is given. It makes no assertion about finding roots with a determinant-equivalence decision oracle.
