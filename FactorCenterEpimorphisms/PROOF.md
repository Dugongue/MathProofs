# Epimorphisms of central-radical groups via factor-center branchwidth

Grochow, Srivastava, and Thakkar ask whether Cayley-table epimorphism testing is in polynomial time when both groups have central elementary abelian radicals and their radical quotients are direct products of nonabelian simple groups. For the entire promised class we give a deterministic $N^{O(\log\log(N+4))}$-time test, where $N=|G|+|H|$. We give polynomial-time tests when either group's arrangement of factor centers has bounded branchwidth, when the target's perfect center has bounded dimension, when the two perfect-center dimensions have bounded difference, or when the common characteristic of the nontrivial perfect centers is at least $(\log N)^\epsilon$ for a fixed $\epsilon>0$. On a family built from copies of a single fixed group, the remaining problem contains binary subcode equivalence with its length retained on the logarithmic group-size scale. The unrestricted polynomial-time question remains open. [Grochow–Srivastava–Thakkar, Open Question 3(5)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07429v1#S7)

## 1. The factor centers and a complete lifting criterion

For an input group $J$, its center is its radical under the promise. Write $J/Z(J)=\prod_i S_i$. The simple factors can be extracted from the quotient table: compute the normal closures of all individual elements and select the distinct inclusion-minimal nonidentity closures. In a product of centerless simple groups, a normal subgroup is a product of coordinate factors. Indeed, if an element of a normal subgroup has a nontrivial coordinate, commutating it with an element supported on that factor isolates a nontrivial element in the factor, and its normal closure contains the factor. Compute the center, quotient, normal closures, and derived groups by ordinary polynomial-time table operations.

Let $U_i$ be the preimage of $S_i$ in $J$ and let $E_i=[U_i,U_i]$. The image of $E_i$ is all of $S_i$, since $S_i$ is perfect, hence $U_i=Z(J)E_i$. It follows that $E_i=[E_i,E_i]$. For $i\ne j$, commutators between $U_i$ and $U_j$ lie in $Z(J)$. For fixed $y\in U_j$ the commutator with $y$ induces a homomorphism from $U_i$ to $Z(J)$, so it vanishes on $E_i$. Thus the $E_i$ commute pairwise. Denote their centers by $D_i=E_i\cap Z(J)$, where equality with the center of $E_i$ follows from the centerless quotient and centrality of $Z(J)$.

Since $J=Z(J)\prod_i E_i$, its derived group is the perfect group $J'=\prod_i E_i$. An element of $J'$ mapped to the identity in the quotient has its $i$th factor in $D_i$ in any expression as a product of elements of the $E_i$. Therefore $A_J=Z(J')=J'\cap Z(J)=\sum_iD_i$. A vector-space complement of $A_J$ in $Z(J)$ is a central direct factor, and $J\cong J'\times C_J$ for that complement. Such a decomposition is computed by extending a basis of $A_J$. When $Z(J)$ is zero, so are $A_J$ and $C_J$.

Every epimorphism $G\to H$ restricts to an epimorphism $G'\to H'$ and induces one on abelianizations. Conversely, an epimorphism of the derived groups and an epimorphism $C_G\to C_H$ combine to give one on the direct products. The latter abelian test is elementary: a trivial target always passes, and a nontrivial target requires the same prime and a source of at least its dimension. Work henceforth on the perfect factors and write $A=A_G$, $B=A_H$. Write $E_i,D_i,S_i$ for the source and $F_j,B_j,T_j$ for the corresponding objects in the target.

An epimorphism of the perfect factors induces one on the centerless quotients. The map to each target simple factor comes from exactly one source simple factor, by the commuting images and centerlessness. That source factor maps isomorphically onto it. Different target factors must come from different source factors, because the image on two target factors of a single source factor mapping isomorphically to both is diagonal, and all other sources are trivial on those coordinates. We write $f$ for this injection of target indices into source indices. For a matched source factor $E_i$, its image is contained in $F_jB$. It is perfect and hence lands in $[F_jB,F_jB]=F_j$. Its image maps onto $T_j$ and generates $F_j$ together with $B_j$. The same commutator argument then shows that the restriction $\theta_{ij}$ is onto $F_j$. Every unused perfect factor has central image and is killed.

Consequently an epimorphism exists precisely when the following choice is possible. Match each target factor $j$ to a distinct source factor $i=f(j)$, choose an epimorphism $\theta_{ij}:E_i\to F_j$ inducing an isomorphism $S_i\to T_j$, and set the maps on omitted factors to zero. The maps on all $D_i$ must descend along $\bigoplus_i D_i\twoheadrightarrow A$ to a single homomorphism $\alpha:A\to B$. Surjectivity of $\alpha$ is automatic: each local epimorphism maps $D_i$ onto $B_j$, because the induced quotient map is injective, and the $B_j$ span $B$.

For sufficiency, send $x_1\cdots x_k$ to the product of the chosen local images. The local images commute, as their target factors are distinct. The product map from the direct product of the $E_i$ has a kernel contained in the product of the $D_i$, since the quotient coordinates are independent. On that kernel the chosen product of local maps is zero by descent along $A$. Therefore the product map descends to a well-defined homomorphism from $G'$. It surjects because its image contains every $F_j$. This proves the criterion. If $B\ne0$, $A=0$ or a different elementary-abelian prime makes the criterion impossible. If $B=0$, only inclusion of the multisets of simple factor isomorphism types remains. In the other cases we can work over the common prime field.

Every nonabelian finite simple group is two-generated. Lifts of a generating pair of $S_i$ generate $E_i$, because they generate together with the central $D_i$ and $E_i$ is perfect. Thus all local homomorphisms can be listed by scanning two target values and propagating words through the source Cayley graph, then checking the table, surjectivity, and quotient map. Two lifts inducing the same quotient isomorphism are equal: their pointwise difference is a homomorphism to the central subgroup $B_j$ from a perfect group. As a result the number of admissible maps per matched pair is at most $|T_j|^2$. Thus any tuple containing at most one choice for each target simple factor has at most $\prod_j |T_j|^2\le |H|^2$ possibilities, even when the $F_j$ have overlapping centers. [Guralnick–Kantor–Kassabov–Lubotzky, two-generation and short profinite presentations](https://arxiv.org/abs/0711.2817)

## 2. Polynomial time from small cut intersections

Let $k$ and $\ell$ be the source and target numbers of simple factors. Both are at most $\log_2 N$. For a family of subspaces $(D_i)$, write $V_I=\sum_{i\in I}D_i$ and $L_I=V_I\cap V_{\bar I}$, where the complement is among all the source indices. Use a full rooted binary tree on the source indices. Its width is the maximum $\dim L_I$ over its subtrees with leaf set $I$. The empty family is immediate. A minimum-width tree can be found in $3^k\operatorname{poly}(N)$ time by computing $\dim L_I$ for every subset and trying all disjoint splits $I=I_1\sqcup I_2$ in order of cardinality. The optimal width at $I$ is the maximum of $\dim L_I$ and the least possible maximum of the optimal child widths. Singletons have width $\dim L_I$. Store choices to reconstruct the tree. This is polynomial in the raw table size, with any prime. Apply the same procedure to the target spaces $B_j$ when desired. This rooted-tree width is the usual branchwidth of a subspace arrangement with the edge-intersection convention. [Jeong–Kim–Oum, branch-decompositions of subspace arrangements](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.01381)

**Source bounded width.** For a subtree at $I$, a state records a set $Y$ of target factors used so far, and a linear map $L_I\to B$. It is feasible when the chosen assignments, using exactly $Y$, induce a linear map $V_I\to B$ giving that restriction. At a leaf, test the value zero for an omitted source and each candidate local epimorphism for a matched source. At an internal node combine states from $I_1,I_2$ only when their target sets are disjoint and the two recorded maps agree on $V_{I_1}\cap V_{I_2}$. That intersection lies in both child boundaries. Agreement is precisely what is needed to paste arbitrary witnessing linear maps on the two child spans. Parent boundary values are determined by the child boundaries: if $z\in L_I$ and $z=x+y$ with $x\in V_{I_1}$ and $y\in V_{I_2}$, then $x=z-y$ belongs to $V_{\overline{I_1}}$ and $y=z-x$ belongs to $V_{\overline{I_2}}$. Gaussian elimination gives these decompositions on the boundary basis. At the root require all targets, and the boundary is zero. There are at most $2^\ell|B|^w$ states per node for width $w$, and pairs of states can be tested in polynomial time. This gives $N^{O(w+1)}$ time.

**Target bounded width.** Let $R=\ker(\bigoplus_i D_i\to A)$ be the source relation space. Use the target tree, writing $W_Y=\sum_{j\in Y}B_j$ and $K_Y=W_Y\cap W_{\bar Y}$. A partial assignment for target set $Y$ determines a residual map $R\to W_Y$: on a relation $(d_i)_i$, sum the values of those $d_i$ assigned to the targets in $Y$. For a globally feasible assignment this map has image in $K_Y$, since its negative is the sum assigned to the complementary targets. Thus store a set of already used source factors and the residual map $R\to K_Y$. At a leaf choose a local epimorphism and compute its residual. At a parent combine disjoint source sets, add their residual maps as maps into $B$, and verify that the sum lands in the parent boundary. At the root the boundary is zero, so the residual vanishes exactly when all source-center relations are respected. Unused source coordinates have zero map. Every state is realized by choices satisfying these local tests. Any global solution passes all the boundary tests; conversely any root state gives a global solution by the lifting criterion.

For the target algorithm, $|R|\le N^{18}$. To see this, use the two-generator profinite presentation of $S_i$ with at most 18 relators. Map the profinite free generators to lifts in $E_i$ of the presented generators of $S_i$. These lifts generate $E_i$. The image of the kernel of the map to $S_i$ is $D_i$, and the image of the normal closure of the relators is generated by their images because they land centrally in $E_i$. Thus $\dim D_i\le18$. If $D_i\ne0$, then $p$ divides $|S_i|$. For an elementary proof, a section of $E_i\to S_i$ gives a cocycle $c(x,y)\in D_i$. Put $b(x)=\sum_z c(x,z)$. Summing the cocycle identity in the third argument yields $|S_i|c(x,y)=b(x)+b(y)-b(xy)$. If $p$ does not divide $|S_i|$, the cocycle is a coboundary, the extension splits as a direct product, and perfectness forces $D_i=0$. Hence at most $\log_p|G|$ source factors have nonzero center, and $\dim R\le 18\log_p|G|$. The target tree has at most $2^k|R|^w$ possible states per node. Trying pairs of states gives $N^{O(w+1)}$ time here as well. Both algorithms can recover a witness by retaining the choices in their accepting states. [Guralnick–Kantor–Kassabov–Lubotzky, at most 18 profinite relators](https://arxiv.org/abs/0711.2817)

## 3. Direct polynomial-time regimes and an unrestricted upper bound

If the target perfect center $B$ has dimension $b$, enumerate the at most $|A|^b$ linear maps $A\to B$ using the dual matrix entries. For each one, build a bipartite matching problem. Place the source factors on one side and the target factors with enough dummy vertices on the other to make equal sizes. A genuine edge is allowed when a local epimorphism agrees with the proposed map on the source factor center, and a dummy edge is allowed when the map kills that source center. A perfect matching supplies the complete criterion. The bound is $|A|^b\operatorname{poly}(N)$.

There is also a quotient algorithm when $a-b$ is bounded for $a=\dim A$. Enumerate the at most $2^k$ possible retained source sets, and keep those whose simple factor types match the target multiset. Quotient $G'$ by the product of all omitted $E_i$, a normal subgroup. The quotient has the required simple-factor multiset; its center is the image of $A$, since its quotient by that image is the product of retained centerless simple factors. Every epimorphism from this quotient to $H'$ induces an isomorphism on its centerless quotient, and therefore has kernel contained in that center. If the center dimension is $a'$, enumerate every subspace of dimension $a'-b\ge0$ by enumerating tuples of that length, and compare each corresponding quotient to $H'$ by the polynomial-time central-radical isomorphism test. All genuine kernels are tested, and every successful isomorphism yields an epimorphism. Because $a'\le a$, this takes $N^{O(a-b+1)}$ time for $a\ge b$. [Grochow–Qiao, Theorem C](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.1776)

For the unrestricted bound, first assign just those target factors with nonzero center to distinct source factors with nonzero center. If there are $k_+$ and $\ell_+$ such factors, respectively, this gives at most $(1+k_+)^{\ell_+}$ assignments. Enumerate their local map tuples, of which there are at most $|H|^2$ for each assignment. Every other source center must map to zero. Check consistency of all resulting prescriptions by linear algebra. Match the remaining target factors with zero center by multiset inclusion using the unused sources, since a centerless factor of given simple type accepts any corresponding source lift by first taking its central quotient. This applies the lifting criterion and gives $(1+k_+)^{\ell_+}\operatorname{poly}(N)$ time. It is at most $N^{O(\log\log(N+4))}$ in general. The divisibility argument gives $k_+,\ell_+\le\log_p N$, so it is polynomial when $p\ge(\log N)^\epsilon$ for fixed $\epsilon>0$.

## 4. Exact binary subcode equivalence on a single family

Let $U=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$. It is perfect with center $\{\pm I\}$ and quotient $A_5$. These facts can also be checked from its fixed 120-element multiplication table: elementary transvections generate $U$, and every transvection is a commutator using conjugation by $\operatorname{diag}(2,2^{-1})$, which multiplies its parameter by $4$ in the corresponding upper or lower subgroup. The quotient $\mathrm{PSL}_2(5)$ is the nonabelian simple group of order 60. For a binary code $C\le\mathbb F_2^k$, identify $\mathbb F_2^k$ with $Z(U)^k$ and put $G(C)=U^k/C$. Its center is $\mathbb F_2^k/C$, its radical is exactly its center, its simple quotient is $A_5^k$, and it is perfect.

Consider two length-$k$ codes $C,D$. A coordinate permutation $\sigma$ satisfying $\sigma(C)\subseteq D$ induces an epimorphism $G(C)\to G(D)$ by permuting the coordinates of $U^k$ before taking the target quotient. Conversely, for an epimorphism of these groups the bijection of simple factors is a permutation $\sigma$. A source copy of $U$, mapped to the matching target factor image in $G(D)$, surjects by the lifting criterion. If the target factor image is $U$, the map is an isomorphism and sends the unique nonidentity center element to the unique nonidentity center element. If the target factor image is $A_5$, it kills the center element. Consequently the composite $U^k\to G(C)\to G(D)$ restricts on $Z(U)^k$ to the map $v\mapsto\sigma(v)+D$. It vanishes on $C$, proving $\sigma(C)\subseteq D$.

The construction takes $2^{O(k)}$ time and produces tables of size $2^{\Theta(k)}$. Conversely the code can be recovered, up to coordinate permutation, from any table isomorphic to $G(C)$. Compute its factor images $E_i$ and center. The $i$th column of a map $\mathbb F_2^k\to Z(G(C))$ is the unique nontrivial center element of $E_i$ if it exists, and zero otherwise. The kernel of this map is $C$ up to the ordering of the factors. These operations are polynomial in the raw table size. Thus on this fixed family a deterministic polynomial-time epimorphism test is equivalent to a deterministic $2^{O(k)}$-time binary subcode-equivalence test. This is containment, which permits distinct code dimensions. The existing polynomial-time central-radical group-isomorphism result concerns equality after a coordinate transport. [Santini–Baldi–Chiaraluce, definition and algorithms for subcode equivalence](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2023.3323068)
