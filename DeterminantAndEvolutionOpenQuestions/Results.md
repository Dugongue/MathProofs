# Four published questions on determinant equivalence and evolution algebras

Informal proofs. Section 1 establishes the PIT-hardness alternative in the arXiv version of Grochow's question. Sections 2–4 answer Questions 38, 44 and 45 from the evolution algebra survey. A final section addresses the survey's abstract-group realization question using a previously formalized derivation theorem. These use orbit invariants, strict adjoint recovery and arithmetic of the monomial equations. No priority claim is made. [Grochow, arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1112.2012#page=10) · [Kaygorodov, Martín González and Viruel, survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22248v1)

## 1. Polynomial identity testing reduces to black-box determinant equivalence

**Source and scope.** Grochow's arXiv Open Question 6.3 asks for PIT-hardness of determinant equivalence or a deterministic black-box algorithm. There is a deterministic black-box reduction from bounded-degree PIT to the decision problem for equivalence to the determinant, even with the output promised homogeneous of degree n on exactly n² variables. The proof also works against affine equivalence. The proceedings version adds root-finding to the hardness alternative. Only PIT-hardness is established here. [ArXiv Question 6.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1112.2012#page=10) · [Proceedings Question VII.2](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~billey/classes/lie/bulletins/grochow.2012.pdf#page=10)

Given a black box for P in r variables with degree at most d, take n≥max(3,r+1,d+1). For a symbolic n-by-n matrix write its first row x and its remaining rows Y. In one row of Y select r+1 distinct entries y₁,…,y_r,z. Set H(Y)=z^n P(y/z) and G(x,Y)=det(x,Y)+H(Y). The expression H is a homogeneous polynomial, zero on z=0 because n>d. Hence evaluating G uses ordinary polynomial-time determinant evaluation and at most one black-box call. If P=0, G is the determinant.

Suppose P≠0 and pass to an algebraic closure to compare singular-locus dimensions. Let c(Y) be the vector of signed maximal minors, so det(x,Y)=x·c(Y). Any singular point of G=0 has c(Y)=0. On the stratum S of rank n−2 rectangular matrices Y, the differential of c has rank two. This is checked after invertible row and column operations at a rectangular matrix with an identity block of size n−2. The two remaining entries in the last row give two independent first-order maximal minors, and all other derivatives lie in their span. Thus at each such Y, the Y-gradient equations (Dc(Y))^T x=−dH(Y) allow at most an affine fiber of dimension n−2. In addition c(Y)=0 makes G=0 require H(Y)=0.

The rank-n−2 stratum is irreducible of dimension (n−2)(n+1). Its projection to the chosen row contains every row vector, since the other rows can be chosen to complete the required rank. Hence the nonzero polynomial H does not vanish identically there. Singular points above S have total dimension at most (n−2)(n+1)−1+(n−2)=n²−5. Above matrices of rank at most n−3, the dimension is at most (n−3)(n+2)+n=n²−6, allowing the entire x-fiber. These rectangular rank dimensions follow by taking a basis for the row space and then its row coordinates, or using an invertible rank-size minor chart.

The singular locus of the determinant hypersurface consists of the square matrices of rank at most n−2, of dimension n²−4. Singular-locus dimension is invariant under invertible affine coordinate changes. Thus G cannot be equivalent to the determinant when P≠0. The dimension argument uses the hypersurface equation as well as the partials and so works in every characteristic. The black-box bound is polynomial in r and the supplied numerical degree bound d over C, as in the original question, or in evaluation models that can access sufficiently many points to distinguish polynomials from polynomial functions.

## 2. Faithful associative representations do not determine automorphism Hopf algebras

**Source and scope.** Both questions in the evolution survey's Question 38 have negative answers in arbitrary dimension. The counterexamples are over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic zero. We use the source's definition of a p-representation, with a commutative associative target algebra carrying an involution, and a prescribed bilinear product p obtained as a k-linear combination of ab, ab*, a*b and a*b*. Its tight algebra is generated by the images of the basis and their stars, without formally adjoining an extra unit. [Survey Question 38](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22248v1#S2.SS2.SSS5) · [Original representation definitions, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.15769v2#S3)

For A=k^n with coordinatewise multiplication and n≥3 use p(a,b)=ab*. The universal relations x_i x_i*=x_i and their stars give x_i=x_i*. The remaining relations make the x_i orthogonal idempotents. Their tight algebra is k^n and the map from A is faithful, as is also witnessed by the coordinatewise representation with the identity involution. The automorphism group scheme of k^n is the constant S_n, by permutation of primitive idempotents locally on any coefficient ring, so its coordinate algebra is k^(n!). These dimensions differ.

Even an interpretation that asks whether *some* choice of p for the given A can give that Hopf algebra fails at n=6. Each k-valued character of a tight p-algebra gives pairs v_i=(x_i,x_i*) of evaluated coordinates in k² with its swap involution. In k² with the deformed product p, the v_i are pairwise orthogonal idempotents. Conversely any such assignment yields a p-representation and therefore a character, as long as at least one pair is nonzero. Nonzero orthogonal idempotents are linearly independent: multiply a linear dependence by a selected idempotent. Thus at most two of the v_i are nonzero. If the set of idempotents of this two-dimensional deformed algebra were infinite, putting them at one label would give infinitely many distinct characters, inconsistent with k^(6!). Otherwise two quadratic equations cut out the idempotents in k², with at most four points by the affine Bézout bound. There are then at most 6·4+binom(6,2)·16=264 nonzero assignments, fewer than 6!=720. This rules out an isomorphism to k^(6!) for any p.

For the second question, every finite-dimensional **simple perfect** algebra with a faithful p-representation over algebraically closed k has dimension at most two. To prove this, take the finitely generated commutative associative subalgebra generated by the image of a basis and its stars, adjoining a unit if needed. If every character kills the image, the Nullstellensatz puts the image in the nilradical J. This ideal is star-stable and nilpotent by Noetherianity. Since the input is perfect, its image is spanned by products under p of two image elements, so is contained in J², then in J⁴, and eventually in zero. Thus some character ε detects a nonzero image. The map a↦(ερ(a),ε(ρ(a)*)) is a nonzero homomorphism into the two-dimensional algebra for the same deformed product and the swap involution. Its kernel is an ideal, so simplicity makes it injective.

Now take the three-cycle evolution algebra with natural basis e₀,e₁,e₂, e_i²=e_(i+1 mod 3). It is perfect. Every nonzero ideal contains a basis vector by multiplying a nonzero element by a suitable e_i, and by squaring it contains the entire cycle. Thus it is simple and, by the preceding bound, has no faithful p-representation. Its cyclic rotation is a nontrivial automorphism. This refutes the proposed equivalence even over an algebraically closed field.

## 3. Twisted forms are classified by a graph action and explicit multiplicative equations

**Source and scope.** Question 44 asks to compute the first cohomology of the automorphism scheme of a perfect evolution algebra and to determine which forms have a natural basis. The following gives all fibers as explicit products of field-unit groups with monomial relations and a finite permutation action. It applies over arbitrary k. The cohomology is fppf when inseparable forms are allowed, and an additional criterion below identifies the separably split forms. An arbitrary field in this statement is a mathematical parameter and need not be given by an algorithmic presentation. [Survey Question 44](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22248v1#S2.SS2.SSS7) · [Elduque–Labra, automorphism group schemes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.02191)

Fix a natural basis in the original perfect algebra A and denote its graph by Γ, its vertex and edge sets by V,E, and its nonzero weights by w∈(k×)^E. Write i→j when the coefficient of e_j in e_i² is nonzero. Define the integer edge-row matrix D with row e_j−2e_i for i→j. A change by diagonal scalars on the inputs of an isomorphism changes edge weights by the monomial map

```text
δ(t)_(i→j) = t_j t_i^(−2).
```

This convention comes from t_j w_(i→j)=t_i² c_(i→j) for a map from weights w to weights c. A perfect algebra's structure matrix is invertible, so there is a directed cycle cover in its support. An integer or rational vector with D a=0 satisfies a_j=2a_i along every edge. On the cycles it vanishes, so D has full column rank. On each undirected connected component the diagonal torsion group is cyclic of odd order. Indeed every coordinate of an element of ker δ has order dividing an odd 2^ℓ−1 from a cycle. Squaring can therefore be inverted by a power on each coordinate, and along an undirected path one scalar determines all the others. Equivalently this kernel is the cyclic diagonalizable group computed by Smith reduction. This argument works for group schemes by using the relations in the character group, which is generated by one character per weak component of Γ and has odd finite order there.

For any edge tuple c, let its free invariants be c^u for u in the integer kernel of D^T. A basis of this kernel suffices. Over an algebraic closure the invariant equations c^u=w^u are exactly the condition that c differs from w by δ(t). This follows by Smith normal form, since all nonzero integral power maps are surjective over the algebraic closure. Let P₀ be the subgroup of graph automorphisms preserving these free invariants of w, with the action on exponents induced by edge permutation.

Here is the full parameter set. Run over conjugacy classes of continuous homomorphisms α from the absolute Galois group of k to P₀ and use the finite Galois extension K fixed by its kernel. Form the following sets.

* Edge weights c in (K×)^E, with g(c_e)=c_(α(g)e) for every g∈Gal(K/k) and edge e, and with c^u=w^u for every free invariant basis vector u.
* Vertex scalars t in (K×)^V with g(t_i)=t_(α(g)i) for every g and vertex i.

Quotient the first set by δ of the second and by the permutations in the centralizer of α(Gal(K/k)) in P₀. Empty sets contribute no forms. The disjoint union is H¹_fppf(k,Aut(A)), with the original algebra as the point for trivial α and c=w. Each equivariance condition is explicit: one coordinate per orbit belongs to the fixed field of its stabilizer, and all other coordinates are its specified Galois conjugates. Thus a fiber is given by multiplicative equations and a quotient in products of units of finite separable extensions. Its nonemptiness is exactly those displayed monomial equations in the orbit fields. For trivial α, Smith normal form makes the fiber before the P₀-quotient exactly the product, over the undirected components, of k×/(k×)^N for the corresponding odd kernel orders N. The P₀-action on those coordinates is the one computed by permuting the edges and applying the same Smith transformations. It may be affine when the origin is taken to be w.

To prove completeness, recover for a form B the intrinsic algebra of operators S satisfying (Sx)y=x(Sy). In a natural basis of a perfect algebra, distinct square vectors are independent, so this algebra consists exactly of the diagonal operators. The defining equations are linear and commute with base change. For B, this gives a finite étale algebra of rank |V|, split over an algebraic closure, whose action on B has rank one on each split line. Hence B is a free rank-one module over it, since modules over the product of fields are determined by their component dimensions. Choose such a module generator. Over a separable Galois splitting field the primitive idempotents applied to that generator give natural basis vectors. They form a basis with zero mixed products by the defining adjoint equality. The Galois group permutes them with no scalars.

Label those vectors so that the graph and free invariants match the original A. Such a labeling exists because a geometric isomorphism is monomial: in natural bases the independence of target squares forces each row to have at most one nonzero entry. It follows that the resulting permutation action lies in P₀, and the coefficients are exactly the equivariant tuples above. Conversely any such tuple, with the prescribed semilinear permutation action, gives an algebra by Galois descent. After extension to an algebraic closure, Smith normal form supplies a diagonal isomorphism to A. In particular its square matrix is invertible, so perfection imposes no hidden additional condition. An isomorphism between two descended algebras is monomial over the splitting field and must commute with the two Galois actions. Its permutation conjugates the two α's and, after aligning them, centralizes their image. Its diagonal entries satisfy precisely the vertex equivariance conditions. Its permutation belongs to P₀ by comparison of the free invariants. This proves both directions of the quotient formula. The use of fppf cohomology is the standard identification of forms with their isomorphism torsors. [Stacks Project, torsors and forms](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/03AG)

The descended algebra has a natural k-basis exactly when α is trivial, because its strict adjoint algebra is split exactly then. If only forms isomorphic over a separable closure are desired, take the union over σ∈P₀ of the tests c/σ(w)∈δ((k_sep×)^V). Each is checked by the same Smith matrix using separable power tests. The union allows for the possibility that geometric lifts of different permitted permutations have different inseparability behavior. It gives the subset corresponding to H¹ for the étale topology. If all N are prime to the characteristic the power tests automatically pass, and over a perfect field the two form sets coincide.

## 4. Exact primes of good reduction with the same graph

**Source and scope.** Question 45 asks for the primes at which a perfect rational evolution algebra has a model over Z_(p) with perfect reduction and the same graph, and for comparison of automorphisms and idempotents. The following specifies the primes and all specialization data, allowing arbitrary finite free models with the given rational generic fiber. [Survey Question 45](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22248v1#S2.SS2.SSS7)

Write its square matrix in a fixed rational natural basis as C, with C_ij the coefficient of e_i in e_j². Write v_p for the normalized valuation. A prime p admits a same-graph perfect model precisely when the system in integers a₁,…,a_n

```text
2a_j − a_i + v_p(C_ij) = 0   for every nonzero C_ij,
v_p(det C) + Σ_i a_i = 0
```

is consistent. This is one Smith normal form test. Only primes occurring in a nonzero entry's numerator or denominator or in det C need testing, since all other valuations are zero. These conditions are sufficient by using the basis p^(a_i)e_i. The edge equations make every nonzero entry a unit, so the graph is unchanged, and the determinant equation makes the reduction perfect.

For necessity, let L be any lattice giving such a model. The linear equations defining its strict adjoint algebra have kernel rank n both over Q and over F_p. Smith normal form over Z_(p) then shows that their integral kernel commutes with reduction. It is a free subalgebra of End(L) reducing to the diagonal algebra F_p^n in a natural residue basis. Complete at p and lift the primitive idempotents by Hensel's lemma in this finite commutative algebra. Each lift is one of the primitive diagonal idempotents of Q_p^n, so is the scalar extension of a rational coordinate projection. It already belongs to the integral strict adjoint algebra over Z_(p), because its matrix in a rational lattice basis is rational and p-adically integral. These idempotents decompose L into its intersections with the individual rational natural lines. A lattice on such a line is p^(a_i)Z_(p)e_i. Relabeling matches the residue graph, and now the displayed edge and determinant conditions are necessary. The lifts are unique as an unordered set, and the valuations a_i are unique in the fixed labeled rational lines whenever the edge equations are soluble. For the last statement the difference of two solutions vanishes on a directed cycle cover, since it doubles along edges.

### Automorphisms at every good prime

Use the normalized lattice coefficients w. For each graph automorphism σ, monomial maps with permutation σ are precisely the solutions of δ(t)=σ(w)/w with the matching permutation convention. These equations over Q, Q_p or F_p give the corresponding automorphism groups, by retaining the permutations for which the equations pass and all solutions for the diagonals. Smith reduction specifies the tests explicitly. The rows off the rank of D demand a product equal to one. Every nonzero Smith diagonal entry d demands a specified d-th power, and each accepted fiber is a torsor under the corresponding diagonal kernel. Over F_p the size of a soluble power fiber is gcd(d,p−1). Thus permutations newly available mod p are detected exactly, including those whose product equalities appear only after reduction. Kernel orders in geometric fibers likewise follow by replacing d with its prime-to-p part in characteristic p, with subgroup schemes retaining the full orders.

Every rational or p-adic automorphism of the normalized generic fiber preserves the lattice. Its scalar valuations satisfy the homogeneous edge equations and hence are all zero. Over Q a diagonal automorphism is trivial, since every coordinate is a root of unity of odd order. Reduction on rational automorphisms is injective: a map reducing to the identity must have identity permutation and therefore is diagonal. The displayed power tests, with the unit restriction automatic, determine the complete reductions of rational and p-adic maps. In particular the comparison does not assume constancy of the abstract group at all good primes.

### Idempotents and their specialization

For normalized C over Z_(p), the idempotent algebra is the quotient by x_i²−(C⁻¹x)_i for all i. It is finite free of rank 2^n with squarefree basis. The equations are monic in pairwise coprime leading monomials x_i² in a degree order, and their pairwise S-polynomials reduce to zero by substituting the linear right-hand sides. Thus this description also computes the scheme in characteristic p, including its multiplicities. Every rational or p-adic idempotent is integral by monicity, or by comparing the maximum coordinate absolute value on the two sides of the equations.

More explicitly, for each residual point, pass to the completed finite algebra and lift the idempotent of the corresponding special-fiber local summand. The rank of that free summand is the special multiplicity, counting dimensions over the residue field after splitting the point. Its generic fiber gives precisely the generic points specializing to that residual point and their multiplicities. Factoring the reduced generic finite algebra into field factors distinguishes the ground-field points, as these are precisely the factors equal to the ground field. Thus one can obtain all p-adic lifts and collisions from the summand's fraction-field algebra and, for rational idempotents, by evaluating the rational points and reducing their integral coordinates. To compare geometric points above a nonrational residue point, make the unramified base extension splitting the residue field first. Finite flatness gives the equality of the total specializing multiplicities and the special multiplicity. At a simple residual point Hensel gives exactly one lift over its unramified coefficient ring. At any good p=2 all points are simple because the Jacobian is −C⁻¹, so the entire geometric specialization is finite étale. These tests apply also at good primes with singular idempotent fibers.

Outside finitely many primes, the automorphism group and idempotent set over F_p are the Frobenius fixed points on their respective generic geometric objects. For the automorphism schemes, discard the primes where incompatible Smith equalities can become true and those dividing the finite diagonal torsion. All remaining strata are finite étale, with multiplication of automorphisms compatible with reduction. For idempotents, spread out the nilradical and the reduced finite algebra by clearing denominators. Its reduced quotient over Q is finite étale, and can be made finite étale over an open of Spec Z, with the spread-out ideal still nilpotent. This retains the point set in every remaining fiber, whether or not the generic scheme was reduced. A common finite Galois splitting field then gives the densities of the joint pair consisting of the automorphism order and the idempotent count by Chebotarev.

## 5. The abstract group of complex points need not be realizable

The survey's Question 31 asks for realization of linear algebraic groups as automorphism groups of evolution algebras. The algebraic-group and group-scheme versions have previously reported negative answers. For the abstract-group version, SL₃(C) already cannot occur. [Survey Question 31](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22248v1#S2.SS2.SSS5) · [Prior algebraic-group refutation and its scope](https://the-omega-institute.github.io/trureturing-mdbook/Blueprint/D5/S3/QuadraticForms/EvolutionAlgebras/AlgebraicGroupRealizationRefutation.html)

Indeed suppose the complex points of an algebraic automorphism group H were abstractly isomorphic to SL₃(C). The group SL₃(C) has no nontrivial finite quotient: it is generated by elementary additive subgroups isomorphic to (C,+), which are divisible and have trivial image in every finite group. Hence the finite-index algebraic identity component of H is the whole group. A connected solvable normal subgroup maps, via the proposed abstract isomorphism, to a solvable normal subgroup of SL₃(C). Its image in the simple nonabelian group PSL₃(C) is trivial. It thus lies in the order-three center, and connectedness makes it trivial. Therefore H is connected semisimple.

Over C its Lie algebra is the derivation algebra of the evolution algebra. By the semisimple derivation classification, it is a direct sum of Lie algebras so_m with m≥3. In that case the simply connected cover of H is a product of spin groups, with each center a 2-group: the spin double cover maps its center into the center of SO_m, which has order at most two. The center of a connected semisimple central quotient is the image of the center of its simply connected cover, so the center of H is also a 2-group. This contradicts the order-three center of SL₃(C), an abstract group invariant. The argument uses the published Lean-checked derivation theorem as its input. [Semisimple derivation classification](https://the-omega-institute.github.io/trureturing-mdbook/Blueprint/D5/S3/QuadraticForms/EvolutionAlgebras/SemisimpleDerivationClassification.html)
