# Exact determinant-one orbit counts for pure-power complete intersections

Let $k$ be any field, $d_1,\ldots,d_n\ge 2$, and
$$
A=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/(x_1^{d_1},\ldots,x_n^{d_n}),\qquad
N=\prod_i d_i .
$$
Let $\mu(x,y)=xy$, viewed as a multiplication tensor with three independent changes of basis. Put $E_i=N(d_i-1)/2$, an integer. For an exponent $d$ occurring at least twice put $S_d=(N/d^2)\binom d2$.

**Theorem.** The determinant image of $\operatorname{Aut}_k(A)$ is the subgroup
$$
D=\left\langle (k^\times)^{E_i}\ (1\le i\le n),\;(-1)^{S_d}\ (d\text{ repeated})\right\rangle .
$$
The determinant-one orbits inside the ordinary orbit of $\mu$ are classified by three cosets
$$
k^\times/D\;\times\;k^\times/(k^\times)^N\;\times\;k^\times/(k^\times)^N .
$$
In particular, over $\mathbb F_q$ there are
$$
\gcd(q-1,N)^2\,\gcd\bigl(q-1,E_1,\ldots,E_n,\text{ optional }(q-1)/2\bigr)
$$
such orbits. The optional entry is present if $q$ is odd and some $S_d$ is odd.

Equivalently, start with $\gcd(q-1,N)^2\gcd(q-1,E_1,\ldots,E_n)$. Divide this baseline by two exactly when $q\equiv 3\pmod 4$ and the only even exponents are two copies of the same $d\equiv2\pmod4$. For example, $k[x,y]/(x^2,y^2)$ gives 32 classes for $q\equiv1\pmod4$, four for $q\equiv3\pmod4$, and one for even $q$.

## Determinants of algebra automorphisms

Write $\mathfrak m=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$. It is the unique maximal ideal, since every element with nonzero constant coefficient is a unit. Its powers give the total-degree filtration, because a monomial of degree at least $r$ is a product of $r$ generators times another monomial. Every automorphism preserves this filtration and has the same determinant as its action on the associated graded vector space. The associated graded algebra is canonically $A$ again. Therefore it suffices to understand graded substitutions $x_i\mapsto \sum_j a_{ji}x_j$.

The pure term $a_{ji}^{d_i}x_j^{d_i}$ survives if $a_{ji}\ne0$ and $d_j>d_i$. It cannot cancel with any other term in the same expansion. Hence $a_{ji}=0$ when $d_j>d_i$, so the substitution is block triangular by truncation degree and each diagonal block is invertible.

In a block of common exponent $d$, consider two nonzero coefficients in a column. In characteristic zero, the term with powers $d-1$ and 1 on those two coordinates has a nonzero coefficient and survives. In characteristic $p>0$ set $e=p^{v_p(d)}$. If $e<d$, the coefficient of the term with powers $d-e$ and $e$ is nonzero. Indeed, expanding the $d/e$-th power of the $e$-th power gives the coefficient $d/e$ times the two nonzero coefficient powers, and $p$ does not divide $d/e$. Both monomial exponents are less than $d$, so again this term survives. Thus the diagonal block is monomial in either case. If $d=e=p^s$, every invertible block is permitted, since the $p^s$-th power of a linear combination is the sum of the $p^s$-th powers. Every such diagonal block, acting on its coordinates alone, is realized by an algebra automorphism.

Take the block-diagonal substitution of a given graded automorphism and compose its inverse with that automorphism. The result is $x_i\mapsto x_i+\sum_{d_j<d_i}b_{ji}x_j$. Assign each monomial $x^\alpha$ the secondary weight $\sum_i d_i\alpha_i$. Each nonidentity term of a substituted monomial replaces some $x_i$ by a variable of smaller weight and therefore lowers the weight. The matrix has diagonal ones and is triangular in any ordering by this weight. Its determinant is one.

The admissible blocks are generated by scalings and coordinate swaps in the monomial case, together with elementary transvections in the general-linear case. A transvection $x_i\mapsto x_i+c x_j$ is triangular with diagonal ones by the exponent of $x_i$. A scaling $x_i\mapsto a x_i$ contributes $a^{E_i}$, because each exponent from 0 to $d_i-1$ occurs on exactly $N/d_i$ monomials. A swap of two coordinates truncated at the same $d$ fixes the monomials with equal exponents on those coordinates. The unequal exponent pairs form $\binom d2$ transpositions for each choice of the other exponents. This gives sign $(-1)^{S_d}$, the final determinant generator. All listed scalings and swaps exist over the field itself. The construction therefore computes the actual rational-point determinant image.

## The full tangent test and the pure-power profile

The same expansion gives an exact test for an arbitrary invertible linear part. In characteristic zero, the column for $x_i$ is allowed exactly when $\sum_{j:a_{ji}\ne0}(d_j-1)<d_i$. Every coefficient of a supported multinomial is nonzero, and every total up to the summed capacity can be allocated. In characteristic $p$, write $d_i=\sum_s c_s p^s$. Factor the $d_i$-th power into the product of the $c_s$-th powers of $\sum_j a_{ji}^{p^s}x_j^{p^s}$. Consequently the column is allowed exactly when there is **no** tuple $(u_j)$ satisfying
$$
0\le u_j<d_j,\quad u_j=0\text{ if }a_{ji}=0,\quad
\sum_j u_j=d_i
$$
with digit sums $\sum_j (u_j)_s=c_s$ at every $s$. Each digit exponent in the expansion is at most $c_s<p$, so the base-$p$ digits specify a unique choice of multinomial terms. The multinomial coefficients with total $c_s$ are nonzero. There is therefore an uncancelled surviving term precisely when such an allocation exists. An invertible linear part passing the column tests is itself a valid algebra automorphism. Its images of degree-one generators span the degree-one piece, so it is surjective and then invertible by finite dimension.

The Hilbert series of the maximal-ideal filtration also recovers the multiset $(d_i)$ over every field. The coefficient of $t$ in the Hilbert series gives $n$, and multiplication by $(1-t)^n$ gives $\prod_i (1-t^{d_i})$. Two such products can agree only for identical multisets. Cancel common factors and compare the first nonzero degree of the remaining products. Thus the multiset determines the ordinary multiplication-tensor orbit within this family, and the three determinant cosets give its exact refinement.

## From the determinant image to the orbit count

Left and right multiplication by a unit have determinant equal to the $N$-th power of its constant coefficient. With the basis ordered by total degree, multiplying by the remaining terms is strictly triangular. Every nonzero constant coefficient occurs. The unital isotopy normal form sends a stabilizing triple to units $a,b$ and an algebra automorphism $\sigma$. Its determinant triple is $(\ell\delta,r\delta,\ell r\delta)$ where $\ell$ and $r$ independently range over $(k^\times)^N$ and $\delta$ independently ranges over $D$. The determinant map on the three linear groups is surjective and its kernel is the determinant-one group. Therefore the orbit refinement is the quotient by these stabilizer determinant triples. In coordinates
$$
(d_X,d_Y,d_Z)\longmapsto
(d_Xd_Y/d_Z,\ d_Z/d_Y,\ d_Z/d_X),
$$
this subgroup becomes $D\times(k^\times)^N\times(k^\times)^N$, giving the claimed cosets and count. [Grochow–Qiao on tensor isomorphism](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00309)

To simplify the optional sign, if $d$ is odd then $E_i=d S_d$ for each variable in that block. An odd $S_d$ then makes some $E_i$ odd, so $-1$ is already an $E_i$-th power. If $d$ is even, $S_d$ is odd exactly when $d\equiv2\pmod4$ and $N/d^2$ is odd. This requires exactly two even exponents, both $d$, and then the gcd of the $E_i$ has 2-adic valuation one. Over an odd finite field, adding the sign changes the cyclic-group index precisely when $q-1$ has 2-adic valuation one.

For concrete comparison over a finite field, let (r=[k^	imes:D]) and (s=gcd(q-1,N)). After choosing any ordinary change of basis from the reference tensor, raise its ratio coordinate to $(q-1)/r$ and the other two coordinates to $(q-1)/s$. These values are independent of the ordinary change of basis because the corresponding kernels are precisely the three subgroups in the theorem. Two tensors in the ordinary orbit are determinant-one equivalent exactly when the three resulting values agree. This uses field exponentiation and requires no discrete logarithms.
