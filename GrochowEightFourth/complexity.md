# Exact placeholder degree of symmetric IPS in positive characteristic

Let `G` be a finite group permuting variables and axiom labels in a compatible finite system `f_i(X)`, over a field `K` of characteristic `p>0`. The degree is the total degree in all placeholder variables, measured after expansion as a polynomial. Coefficients may have arbitrary degree in the original variables. For `d>=1`, a symmetric IPS refutation of degree at most `d` in the placeholders exists exactly when every `p`-subgroup `P` and every point `a` of its fixed locus over the algebraic closure satisfy the following condition. There is a violated axiom label `i` at `a` whose `P`-orbit has size at most `d`.

Necessity follows by viewing the polynomial as a sum of coefficient-weighted monomials in the placeholders, all of positive degree at most `d`. On the fixed point `a`, every non-singleton orbit of such monomials gives zero upon axiom substitution because all its evaluated summands agree and its orbit size is divisible by `p`. A `P`-fixed monomial of positive degree has support equal to a nonempty union of whole label orbits. For a nonzero value, all labels in the support are violated, which gives a violated orbit of size at most its degree.

For sufficiency, let `H=G_a` and choose its Sylow `p`-subgroup `P`. By assumption an orbit `O=P i` with size at most `d` consists of violated labels. Let `M=prod_{j∈O} y_j` and let `G_M` be its stabilizer. This monomial is fixed by `P`, has positive degree at most `d`, and gives a nonzero value at the evaluated axioms. Choose an interpolating polynomial with value one on `G_M a` and zero on the rest of the finite orbit `G a`, and take the product of its translates over `G_M`. The result `u` is `G_M`-invariant with those same values. Then `sum_{gG_M} g(u M)` is invariant, has the required placeholder-degree bound, and evaluates at `a` to `[H:H∩G_M] M(f(a))≠0` by the orbit calculation. The index is prime to `p` since `H∩G_M` contains `P`.

The outputs of such invariant placeholder polynomials form an ideal in the invariant coefficient ring. The construction gives no common zero for this ideal in the whole polynomial ring over the algebraic closure, so the Nullstellensatz yields a finite expression `1=sum_i b_i J_i` with invariant `J_i` in the ideal. Expanding the product of all conjugates of the expression, using formal variables in the positions of `J_i`, gives invariant coefficients and no constant term in those formal variables. Substitution of `J_i` shows the invariant output ideal contains one. Combining the certificates with coefficients in the invariant coefficient ring keeps placeholder degree at most `d`. A base-field-linear functional taking one to one descends the certificate coefficientwise. An invariant polynomial admits a syntactically symmetric circuit by monomial expansion, as the group permutes variable labels. This exact existence characterization makes no size claim.

## Exact threshold for pigeonhole equations

For the fully `S_N×S_m`-symmetric matrix pigeonhole system with `N>m`, the minimum possible placeholder degree of a symmetric IPS refutation in characteristic `p` is the largest power `q` of `p` such that `N mod q <= m`. At least `q=1` is valid. No power exceeding `N` is valid since `N>m`. For the original `N=m+1` family this is the largest power of `p` at most `N`.

For the lower bound, let an order-`q` permutation of the rows consist of as many disjoint `q`-cycles as possible and `N mod q` fixed rows. Fix all holes, injectively assign the fixed rows to holes, and set the other rows to zero. At this point the only violated labels are the row constraints of those other rows. Their orbits under the generated cyclic `p`-subgroup all have size exactly `q`. For `q=1` the argument is simply that a refutation must have positive degree.

For the upper bound fix any `p`-subgroup `P` and a fixed point `a`. Suppose that there is no violated label of orbit size at most `q`. Consider only rows in `P`-orbits of size at most `q`. The number of these rows is at least `N mod (p q)>m`, since every larger `P`-orbit has size divisible by `p q` and maximality rules out the next power `p q`.

In each small row orbit choose a representative `i`. Its row-sum axiom holds. Let `P_i` be its stabilizer in `P`. The entries at this row are constant on `P_i`-orbits of columns. Non-singleton orbits cancel in the row sum, so some `P_i`-fixed column `j` has a nonzero entry in row `i`. The orbit of the cell `(i,j)` has size exactly that of the row orbit, because `j` is fixed by `P_i`. Its Boolean axiom therefore holds and its nonzero entry is one. Choosing one such cell per small row orbit defines a `P`-equivariant map from the small row set to the columns by extending the choice equivariantly. This map picks cells of value one throughout.

It remains to find two rows mapped to the same column whose collision label has `P`-orbit size at most `q`. If the map within some individual row orbit is noninjective, choose a fiber over `j` of size greater than one. The column stabilizer `P_j` acts transitively on this fiber, as an element moving one row to another over `j` fixes `j` by equivariance. The induced permutation group on the fiber is a nontrivial `p`-group, with a nonidentity central element `z`. Such an element has no fixed points in a faithful transitive action, since fixing one point and commuting with the group would fix all points. Choose any `i` in the fiber and `i'=z i` using a representative of the induced permutation. The two stabilizers in `P_j` agree by centrality in the induced group. Both row stabilizers in `P` are contained in `P_j`, so their stabilizers in `P` also agree. This common stabilizer fixes the collision label and has index equal to the size of the small row orbit, at most `q`.

Otherwise every small row orbit maps injectively onto its image, a whole column orbit. The greater number of small rows than columns forces two distinct row orbits to have the same column-orbit image. Choose rows `i,i'` over a common column `j`. Both row stabilizers are `P_j` since their restrictions are equivariant bijections onto that column orbit. Again the collision label has orbit size at most `q`. In either case it is violated, as both entries are one, a contradiction. This applies to either ordered or unordered collision labels. The general criterion now gives the upper bound.

The result concerns degree solely in the placeholders. The construction is existential and its circuit size can be large. The proof is informal. Definition 19 supplies the original pigeonhole equations. Its remarks give examples where finite characteristic defeats placeholder linearity. The exact degree formula covers the nonlinear hierarchy as well. The orbit calculation uses a weighted transfer, as in modular invariant theory. [Dawar–Grädel–Kullmann–Pago, Section 6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16820#S6.SS3) [Symonds, Section 3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00029-024-00968-w)

# Square-symmetric permanent lower bounds in all odd characteristics

Over every field of odd characteristic, any family of square-symmetric arithmetic circuits for the permanent has orbit size `2^Ω(n)`. Here square symmetry is simultaneous permutation of the row and column labels. The assertion applies already on `0/1` input matrices. This answers the question in Section 8, extending the characteristic-zero square-symmetric lower bound without requiring the independent row and column symmetry of Theorem 7.11. [Dawar–Wilsenach, Symmetric Arithmetic Circuits, Sections 7–8](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v021a014/v021a014.pdf)

## Two directed graphs

Let `Γ` be a simple connected 3-regular bipartite expander with `v` vertices and uniform edge-expansion constant `h>0`. We can take sizes `v=6·2^j` from successive degree-three bipartite Ramanujan two-lifts. A regular bipartite graph has a perfect matching by Hall's theorem. Removing such a matching leaves a spanning 2-factor, so orient its cycles. A connected 3-regular bipartite graph is 2-connected: successive perfect matchings decompose its edges into three matchings, each edge lies in a cycle given by the union of two of these matchings, and a cut vertex of degree three in a connected graph without bridges is impossible. [Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava, Interlacing Families I, Theorem 5.5](https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/spielman/PAPERS/Marcus_Spielman_SrivastavaIFI.pdf)

Use the two Dawar–Wilsenach graphs `X_0,X_1` from Section 7.2, with even parity at every base vertex for `X_0` and odd parity at exactly one vertex for `X_1`. Recall their fibers. A base edge `e` has two bit vertices `e_0,e_1`. A base vertex `w` has one balance vertex `b_w` and four inner vertices labeled by incident bit triples of the required parity. Each inner vertex is adjacent to `b_w` and to the corresponding bit vertex for each of the three incident edges. The bipartition consists of all inner vertices on one side and all balance and bit vertices on the other. Both parts have `4v` vertices.

For `b=0,1` let `M_b` be the biadjacency matrix from the inner side to the other side. Form `N_b` by deleting every incidence between an oriented 2-factor edge's bit vertices and the inner fiber at its tail. All other incidences remain. Consider the directed adjacency matrix

```text
A_b = [   0     M_b ]
      [ N_b^T    0  ].
```

Its permanent is `perm(M_b) perm(N_b)`, because a permutation contributing a nonzero monomial maps each of the two sides bijectively onto the other. All entries are zero or one.

## The permanent gap

In a perfect matching of `N_b`, both bit vertices of each factor edge go to its head. At each vertex one factor edge enters and one leaves, so two of its four inner vertices must match to the entering edge's bits. A third matches to the balance vertex in that fiber. The last matches to one of the two bit vertices of the incident complementary perfect-matching edge. Consequently each complementary edge divides its two bits between its endpoints, and there are independently two choices on each such edge.

Fix all those choices. Locally, projection of the four allowed bit triples onto the doubled edge and the singleton edge is a bijection onto the four possible bit pairs. The doubled edge's two bit vertices are each adjacent to one of the two disjoint pairs of inner vertices. The singleton bit vertex has two neighbors, one in each pair. There are two choices for its matched neighbor. After choosing it, the matching of the doubled bit vertices is forced in the occupied pair and has two choices in the other pair. The balance vertex takes the last inner vertex. Thus there are four local matchings, independently of the parity and singleton bit. It follows that `perm(N_0)=perm(N_1)=2^(v/2) 4^v`.

Dawar–Wilsenach's Section 7.2 shows that `perm(M_0)−perm(M_1)` is, up to sign, `2^(3v/2+1)`. Multiplication gives the integer identity `perm(A_0)−perm(A_1)=±2^(4v+1)`. This difference survives in every odd characteristic. The construction uses one full matching count and one restricted matching count on the two directions, so their product preserves the gap.

## Counting indistinguishability with the directed incidences

Put `k=floor(c v)` for a fixed sufficiently small positive `c`, for example `min(1/16,h/100)`. Deleting any set of at most `2k` base vertices leaves a unique component with more than `v/2` vertices. Otherwise a union of remaining components could be chosen with size between `v/4` and `v/2`. To see this, use a component already in that range if one exists, or accumulate smaller components until the size first reaches `v/4`. Expansion gives at least `h v/4` edges from that union to the deleted set, which can be incident with at most `3(2k)` edges. The same component is nested when more vertices are deleted, as two sets of size greater than `v/2` intersect.

Play the bijective `k`-pebble game. Duplicator always maps each fiber to the corresponding fiber, where inner, balance, and bit fibers are distinguished. A pebble on an inner or balance fiber blocks its base vertex. A pebble on an edge bit blocks both endpoints of its base edge. Keep an edge-bit vector `σ` that translates the pebbled labels by addition, with local incident parity agreeing with the required parity changes at every base vertex except a single defect. The defect lies in the component of size greater than `v/2` of the unblocked base. Initially take `σ=0` and the one twisted vertex as the defect. At any stage no pebble meets a defect fiber. At nondefect fibers the translation is a bijection of the required parity labels, and it preserves compatibility along every observed incidence. Balance adjacency is automatic. Thus the pebbled matching is a partial isomorphism of the directed graphs, since the rule deleting the tail incidences depends only on the fixed base labels.

To make a bijection move after removing one pebble, temporarily keep the previous `σ`. Its defect still lies in the large component of the remaining blockers. For each possible destination fiber `F` independently, choose a vertex `r_F` in the large component after additionally blocking that fiber, and take a path in the current large component from the old defect to `r_F`. Such a path exists by nesting. Adding one to the bits of its edges moves the defect without changing the images of any remaining pebbles, since the path avoids their blocked vertices and endpoints. Use this modified vector to define the translation on the fiber `F`. That translation is well-defined even for inner fibers because the new defect lies outside their blocker. Balance fibers use the unique bijection. The translations specified independently on all fibers combine into a single bijection of the universes. Whatever point Spoiler chooses, the vector chosen for its fiber establishes the next invariant, in either direction of play. Duplicator therefore wins with `k` pebbles. This direct argument also verifies that the standard CFI indistinguishability survives keeping the two directed incidence relations different.

## Orbit lower bound

Apply Dawar–Wilsenach's Theorem 5.1 with a singleton set accepting just the permanent value of the first matrix. It yields a square-symmetric Boolean threshold circuit of the same orbit size. Rigidification does not increase orbit size, as explained after their Theorem 6.4. A rigid threshold circuit with support sizes at most `s` cannot distinguish input binary relations equivalent in the bijective `2s`-pebble game. The underlying support argument applies to directed binary relations as in Anderson–Dawar's relational vocabulary model. Here is the argument for this case.

The value of a gate on a labeled structure is determined once the image of its support is fixed, since all extensions of that labeling differ by a permutation fixing the gate. Fix a parent gate and images of its support that form a winning pebble position. Break its children into orbits under the pointwise stabilizer of the parent support. For one child representative, extend the parent-support assignment to the child support in every injective way. Successive choices of Duplicator bijections give a bijection between these extensions on the two structures while using at most `2s` pebbles. By induction on circuit height, the corresponding child values agree, since each extended assignment gives a winning position on the child support. These extensions sample all child gates in the stabilizer orbit with equal multiplicity. Indeed, they correspond to images of the representative support under permutations fixing the parent support, and the number of supported labelings mapping to any particular child gate is constant by transitivity. The number of children evaluating to one therefore agrees in this orbit. Repeating for each child orbit gives equality at the parent, whose threshold value depends solely on those counts. Input gates agree by the partial-isomorphism condition on their relational labels. The output is fixed by all permutations and has empty support, proving the claim. [Anderson–Dawar, On Symmetric Circuits and Fixed-Point Logics, Sections 2 and 4.4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00224-016-9692-2)

For any sufficiently large target dimension `n`, choose the largest `v` in the doubling expander sequence with `8v≤n`, so `n<16v`. Pad both matrices with the same identity block to size `n`. The permanent gap is unchanged. The bijective game is unchanged too, since Duplicator matches the new vertices by a fixed bijection and plays the preceding strategy on the original vertices. For `r=floor(k/2)`, Dawar–Wilsenach's Theorem 6.2 implies that a circuit of orbit size at most `binom(n,r)` would have supports smaller than `r` after Boolean translation and rigidification. This contradicts the permanent gap by the support argument above. Here `r≤n/4` and `r=Ω(n)`, so `binom(n,r)≥(n/r)^r=2^Ω(n)`. This proves the stated lower bound for all sufficiently large `n`. The proof is informal.
