# Sharp symmetric circuit bounds for symbolic paths

Fix \(0<\varepsilon<1\). Over every field, the optimal gate orbit for vertex-symmetric arithmetic circuits computing the unoriented \(m\)-edge simple-path polynomial on \(N\) vertices is
\[
2^{\Theta_\varepsilon(m\log_2(1+N/m))}
\quad\text{for }1\le m\le(1-\varepsilon)N,\quad m\le N-1.
\]
The upper bound also holds for total circuit size and for every \(m\le N-1\). The lower bound concerns symbolic circuits. It combines the signed path reduction with a counting-game witness that works across characteristics. In particular, growing paths alone give a quantitative negative answer to the symmetric form of Grochow–Urisman Question 1.11 in every characteristic. [Question 1.11](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26244v2#S1.SS3)

## A sparse constraint gap in every characteristic

Use the connected cubic bipartite expanders on \(v=6\cdot2^j\) vertices from the earlier permanent construction. Orient their edges arbitrarily. Let \(\ell=3\) if the computation field has characteristic two and \(\ell=2\) otherwise. At vertex \(a\) of the base, candidates are the assignments of elements of \(\mathbb F_\ell\) to its three incident edge slots for which the sum with incidence signs is a prescribed \(\beta_a\). An edge between distinct colors allows a candidate pair precisely when the two assignments to its common edge agree. Take all \(\beta_a=0\) in the first instance and set just one to \(1\) in the second.

The oriented incidence matrix has rank \(v-1\) over \(\mathbb F_\ell\). A spanning tree already gives this rank, by deleting a leaf successively, and the row sum is zero. Consequently the first instance has \(\ell^{|E|-v+1}\) assignments and the second has none. Their counts differ in the computation field by choice of \(\ell\). Each vertex still has the same number \(\ell^2\) of candidates on both sides.

Choose a perfect matching of the cubic bipartite base and duplicate each of its edges with unit weight. Remove one of those duplicates, attach a new leaf at each of its incident endpoints, and give the leaves singleton domains and unit weights. The graph now has the degree condition for the signed path reduction, while all original constraints remain. Its Euler trail has \(O(v)\) edges. [Earlier cubic expander construction](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/GrochowEightFourth/complexity.md#square-symmetric-permanent-lower-bounds-in-all-odd-characteristics) · [Signed path reduction](ordinary-paths.md)

Apply that reduction, including the input/output split and two terminal leaf markers. Use the weight \(y\) formally, working over \(K(y)\). The signed sum over the four masks on terminal leaf edges, after taking the specified \(y\)-coefficients and summing over lengths, detects the constraint gap. Thus for at least one query length \(d=O(v)\) and one of the four masks, the corresponding \(d\)-path polynomials differ on the weighted host pair. There are \(O(v)\) vertices because each color has constant domain and occurs at most twice, and the split arcs need no subdivision. There is no need to choose a field value for \(y\).

## Why the weighted hosts are counting-indistinguishable

Every active host vertex is either attached to a definite base vertex and a candidate there, with a layer and a split-role tag, or belongs to fixed terminal gadgets and padding. Each nonconstant relation is determined by the tags and by equality of entire candidates within one base color, or equality of the appropriate edge slots at adjacent base colors. The negative weights, links of weight \(y\), and masks of leaf edges are determined by the tags. Thus a translation of all local candidate slots by an edge vector preserves every observed weight when it maps the corresponding affine local candidate sets.

Maintain a translation with one residual defect at a base vertex in the largest unblocked component. A pebble on any copy of a candidate blocks that base vertex. Exactly as in the earlier cubic expander argument, after blocking at most \(c v\) vertices the base has a unique component of size greater than \(v/2\), for a fixed small \(c>0\). The sum of signed incidences across an oriented path from one vertex to another is a vector supported at its two endpoints with opposite values. Adding a suitable signed translation along such a path moves the defect to a new unblocked vertex. Paths in the large unblocked component avoid all currently held pebbles. All copies of any candidate color held at the same move use the same translation.

More explicitly, after one pebble is removed, choose separately for each possible destination base color a translation that moves the defect into the large component remaining when this extra base vertex is blocked. Choose the paths in the old large component, using nesting of the large components. Destination colors that are already blocked need no change. Assemble those local translations into a single bijection of the host sets. For all copies with a given outer tag it uses the indicated candidate bijection for their base color. It preserves the existing pebble assignments. Whichever new vertex is selected, the translation chosen for its color proves the next invariant on the pebbles. Fixed leaf, tail and padding vertices are matched by identity. This is a winning bijective strategy for \(\lfloor c v\rfloor\) pebbles. Add \(v+2\) identical isolated padding vertices. If an assembled bijection has odd sign, compose it with a swap of two currently unpebbled padding vertices in the target. This keeps it valid for all possible next moves and makes the strategy use only even bijections.

## Prescribing the length and bounding gate orbits

If the target length is \(m\ge d\), replace one of the terminal pendant edges by a chain of length \(m-d+1\), masking its leaf edge in the same manner. A path using both terminal leaf edges must traverse that entire chain. Between them it is one of the previous paths of length \(d\), so the four-mask coefficient identity remains valid at length \(m\). This reasoning can be applied to each query length before selecting a distinguishing mask. Add isolated vertices to reach \(N\). Before this last padding the order is at most \(m+C v\) for an absolute \(C\) that includes the extra isolated vertices for parity correction. Choose \(v\) in the doubling sequence with \(v\) a small constant times \(\min(m,N-m)\). This leaves room for the host and ensures \(d\le m\) for every query. For the claimed density range \(v=\Omega_\varepsilon(m)\).

Use the alternating-group small-index theorem of Dawar–Wilsenach, which gives supports smaller than \(r\) if every gate orbit has size below \(\binom Nr\), where \(r\le N/4\). First make the circuit action unique, if necessary, by identifying gates with identical recursive syntactic descriptions and retaining wire multiplicities. This preserves the polynomial and cannot increase orbit size. Restrict the action to the alternating group. For \(r\) a small constant times \(v\), the game uses at most \(2r\) pebbles to compare a parent and a child support. To see the evaluation argument, split the parent's children into orbits under the pointwise stabilizer of the parent support. For a representative child, list every injective extension of the parent assignment to the union of the two supports. Since at least two vertices remain free, each extension extends to an even permutation. It samples the child orbit with equal multiplicity. Successive bijections of Duplicator pair these extensions on the two weighted hosts. Induction on gate height matches child values and thus evaluates each parent equally, since addition and multiplication use the corresponding multisets. At the output the support is empty. This proof works directly for entries in \(K(y)\) and uses no characteristic restriction. Therefore the differing pair requires a gate orbit at least \(\binom Nr=2^{\Omega_\varepsilon(m\log(1+N/m))}\). For bounded \(m\), dependence on an off-diagonal variable forces an orbit of size \(\binom N2\), which gives the same uniform statement with adjusted constants. [Alternating support and evaluation theorems, Dawar–Wilsenach §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.10986#S4)

## Symmetric upper bound without division by two

Compute the oriented path sums on subsets with one prescribed endpoint by the usual last-edge subset recurrence. For even \(m=2t\), each unoriented path has one middle vertex. Sum over a choice of this vertex and unordered pairs of disjoint \(t\)-sets for its two branches, using the product of their oriented branch sums. For odd \(m=2t+1\), sum over a middle edge and an unordered pair of complementary \(t\)-sets anchored at its two endpoints, using its edge weight and the two branch sums. Each unoriented path occurs exactly once, including \(m=1\) with empty branches. Every subset, middle and branch indexing choice relabels naturally under vertex permutations. The recurrence and final sums have size \(2^{O(m\log(1+N/m))}\), by bounding the subset counts and at most \(2^{O(m)}\) branch choices per vertex set. No normalization uses the coefficient field.

For the ordered-pair convention, the same bounds hold for consistently directed paths. On the lower side, retain the directed host of the signed reduction and append directed terminal leaves, avoiding the split and the auxiliary \(y\). After the four endpoint masks and summing the allowed lengths, some query differs. The same padding and game arguments apply. On the upper side, simply sum the last-edge recurrence over all terminal vertices of all subsets of size \(m+1\), since the orientations define different monomials. Thus the quantitative negative answer also applies in the ordered-variable convention.
