# Central quasigroup isomorphism is in deterministic logspace

This informal proof answers Question 7.5 of Johnson, Levet, Vojtěchovský and Widholm. Given Cayley tables of two finite quasigroups, at least one of which is central, isomorphism testing belongs to L. The same algorithm recognizes centrality within L. [Original question](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06478v3#S7)

## Recovering and compressing the affine data

Use Proposition 6.9 of the source to recognize a central quasigroup and recover its data $Q(A,\phi,\psi,c)$, with $A$ Abelian and $x*y=\phi(x)+\psi(y)+c$. Their construction uses only constant-depth table manipulations, so every bit is available in logspace. If one input is central and the other is not, they cannot be isomorphic. For central inputs the underlying Abelian groups are recovered intrinsically up to isomorphism, as the displacement groups.

We first show that, from the explicit table of $A$, a product of prime-power cyclic factors and coordinates relative to it are computable bit by bit in logspace. The identity and powers are available by scanning the table. Trial division of $|A|$ enumerates its prime divisors with logarithmically many work bits. For a prime $p$ use the $p$-primary subgroup $A_p$, which one recognizes by testing annihilation by the largest power of $p$ dividing $|A|$. For $k\ge 1$ let
$$
W_k=A_p[p]\cap p^{k-1}A_p.
$$
Membership is an order test and a scan for a preimage under multiplication by $p^{k-1}$. Choose the lexicographically first vector-space basis of $W_k/W_{k+1}$ by keeping an element exactly when it is outside the subgroup generated by $W_{k+1}$ and all earlier elements of $W_k$. This subgroup test uses undirected reachability in the corresponding generator graph on $A$, admitting a logspace adjacency test, and hence Reingold's theorem. The selected vectors $w$ across the grades form a basis of $A_p[p]$. Choose the first $a$ with $p^{k-1}a=w$ for each vector of grade $k$. It has order $p^k$.

The map from the product of the cyclic groups on the chosen $a$'s to $A_p$ is injective. A nontrivial kernel would contain a nonzero element of order $p$, contradicting independence of the corresponding bottom vectors $w$. The number of vectors chosen at grade $k$ is the count of order-$p^k$ factors in the Abelian-group classification, since $\dim W_k$ counts factors of order at least $p^k$. Therefore the product has order $|A_p|$ and the map is onto. Taking all $p$ gives the desired basis. To obtain the coordinate of any element in one chosen factor, enumerate that factor's possible exponents and test whether subtracting the corresponding multiple leaves the subgroup generated by the other factors. Those factor-selection tests are recomputable in logarithmic space, and the exponent is unique. Thus orders of factors and the coordinates of $c,\phi(a),\psi(a)$ are virtual strings of polynomial length in $\log |A|$, with a logspace bit oracle. Compare the lists of orders, and identify the additive groups by aligning the lists when they match. [Reingold, Undirected connectivity in log-space](https://doi.org/10.1145/1391289.1391291)

## Turning the marked orbit problem into diagram isomorphism

Put $m=\exp A$ and $D_i=1-\phi_i-\psi_i$. For each input $i$, use a diagram with vertices $A$, $B=A\oplus\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$, and $C=\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$. Its arrows are the two loops $\phi_i,\psi_i$ on $A$, the inclusion $A\to B$, the projection $B\to C$, and the map
$$
d_i:B\to A,\qquad d_i(a,k)=D_i a+k c_i.
$$
An isomorphism of diagrams consists of $\gamma$ on $A$, a unit scalar $u$ on $C$, and $(a,k)\mapsto(\gamma a+kt,uk)$ on $B$ for some $t\in A$. The loop arrows require $\gamma\phi_1=\phi_2\gamma$ and $\gamma\psi_1=\psi_2\gamma$. The last arrow requires $\gamma c_1=D_2 t+u c_2$. Multiplying all three vertex maps by $u^{-1}$ gives an equivalent witness with $u=1$, since every vertex group has exponent dividing $m$. Thus the diagram condition is exactly Theorem 6.3 of the source, equivalently $c_2-\gamma c_1\in\operatorname{im}D_2$ and the same two intertwiner equations. Every such witness is also a diagram isomorphism.

The source's Theorem 6.1 proof appeals to transporting the list of images of two bases. That tuple condition alone does not impose intertwining. For example, on $A=\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z$, take $c_1=c_2=0$, $\phi_1=\psi_1=1$ and $\phi_2=\psi_2=-1$. Negation transports the displayed tuples for the same basis, although the identity and negation cannot be conjugate. The diagram retains the intertwiner equations explicitly. [Theorem 6.3 and the tuple in §6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06478v3#S6)

## Compact linear congruences in small space

For an integer matrix $D$ of full row rank $h$ and $s$ columns, let $v(D)$ be the minimum $p$-adic valuation of its maximal minors. It is the valuation of the index of the column lattice in $\mathbb Z_p^h$. We can compute it by a division-free determinant with one polynomial variable:
$$
P(T)=\det\bigl(D\,\operatorname{diag}(T,T^2,\ldots,T^s)D^{\mathsf T}\bigr)=
\sum_{|J|=h}\det(D_J)^2T^{\sum_{j\in J}j}.
$$
Indeed, choose a basis of the column lattice over $\mathbb Z_p$ and express the columns in it. Their reductions modulo $p$ span $\mathbb F_p^h$. The minimizing sets $J$ are exactly the bases of this vector matroid, since the determinant of the lattice-basis matrix contributes the same valuation for all $J$. Distinct weights $1,\ldots,s$ give a unique minimum-weight basis by the matroid greedy theorem. At its exponent in $P$, the unique minimizing term has valuation $2v(D)$ and all other terms have strictly greater valuation. Every coefficient has valuation at least $2v(D)$, proving that the minimum coefficient valuation equals $2v(D)$.

To test the system $Lx\equiv b$ with row moduli $p^{e_i}$, form $D=[L\mid\operatorname{diag}(p^{e_i})]$. The system is solvable exactly when adding $b$ as a column leaves $v(D)$ unchanged. Indeed, the column lattice then has unchanged index and is the same lattice. Integral $p$-adic solutions are equivalent to integer solutions modulo those powers by reduction. The diagonal minor gives $v(D)\le\sum_i e_i$, also after adding $b$. Thus we may compute the polynomial modulo $p^{2\sum_i e_i+1}$, at polynomially bounded degree in the matrix dimensions. Berkowitz's division-free determinant and ordinary binary arithmetic give uniform circuits of polynomial size and polylogarithmic depth in the compact input length. The operations to test whether a coefficient is divisible by $p^a$, count its valuation and minimize are also such circuits. All bit lengths here are polynomial in $\log |A|$. [Berkowitz, On computing the determinant in small parallel time using a small number of processors](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0190(84)90018-8) · [Hesse–Allender–Barrington, Uniform constant-depth threshold circuits for division and iterated multiplication](https://www.cs.rutgers.edu/~allender/papers/division.pdf)

The modulo-$p$ image of the solution group of a homogeneous system is an $\mathbb F_p$-subspace when the unknowns are residues in a product of cyclic $p$-groups. To compute a basis, flag coordinate $j$ exactly when the system has a solution whose earlier coordinates are zero and whose $j$-th coordinate is 1 modulo $p$. Each flag is one feasibility test with additional linear congruences. Projection of the image onto the flagged coordinates is an isomorphism: those coordinates record the increases in dimension of the successive prefix projections, and a nonzero vector with all flagged coordinates zero would have a first nonzero coordinate that must be flagged. Use one basis vector for each unit tuple on the flagged coordinates. To get its value at any coordinate, impose that tuple and test candidate values modulo $p$. Exactly one value is feasible. A serial scan over candidates uses $O(\log p)$ work bits together with the circuit evaluation space. This produces a virtual basis in logspace without storing the compact system or its output.

## A compact endomorphism-algebra test

The diagram isomorphism problem separates into primary parts, since homomorphisms of Abelian groups preserve those parts. Work at one prime. Form the direct sum of the two diagrams and call its endomorphism ring $E$. For each vertex the unknown endomorphism has entries from its prime-power cyclic coordinates. An entry from a factor of order $p^a$ to a factor of order $p^b$ is an integer $x$ with $p^a x\equiv 0\pmod{p^b}$. Commutativity at every arrow is a homogeneous linear congruence in these entries, with row modulus that of the target cyclic coordinate. Thus computing the image $E_0$ of $E$ on all vertex groups modulo $p$ is the task in the preceding section. Identify $E_0$ with its block-diagonal faithful action on the direct sum $V$ of the vertex quotients. A basis for this matrix algebra is virtual and each bit can be recomputed in logspace.

The kernel of $E\to E_0$ is a nilpotent ideal: each map in it carries each vertex into $p$ times itself, and a product of sufficiently many such maps is zero. Therefore $E/J(E)\cong E_0/J(E_0)$, where $J$ denotes the Jacobson radical. We need the radical $J(E_0)$ and a few ranks, without decomposing any semisimple factors.

Here is a division-free form of the radical computation in positive characteristic. For any finite-dimensional algebra $U\subseteq \operatorname{End}_{\mathbb F_p}(V)$ with faithful action, write
$$
U/J(U)\cong\prod_a \operatorname{Mat}_{n_a}(\mathbb F_{p^{f_a}}).
$$
Let $m_a>0$ be the multiplicity of the simple module of component $a$ among the composition factors of $V$. Positivity follows because the regular left module $U$ embeds in a product of $\dim V$ copies of $V$ by evaluating its action on a basis. In particular $m_a\le \dim V$. Set $I_{-1}=U$. Inductively $I_{j-1}$ is the inverse image of the sum of components with $p^j\mid m_a$. For $x\in I_{j-1}$, require that the coefficient at $t^{\dim V-p^j}$ in the characteristic polynomial of $xy$ vanish for every $y$ in a fixed spanning set of $U$.

To verify this instruction, use that the characteristic polynomial is the product of the polynomials on composition factors. Divide each factor by its leading power of $t$ and use $t^{-1}$ as a formal variable. The $a$-th factor has initial terms $1-\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb F_{p^{f_a}}/\mathbb F_p}(\operatorname{tr}(x_a y_a))t^{-1}+\cdots$ before raising to the multiplicity $m_a$. Components where $x_a=0$ contribute 1. For the remaining components all $m_a$ are divisible by $p^j$, and the Frobenius identity shows that the coefficient at order $p^j$ in the whole product is
$$
-\sum_{a: \,v_p(m_a)=j}
\frac{m_a}{p^j}\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb F_{p^{f_a}}/\mathbb F_p}(\operatorname{tr}(x_a y_a)).
$$
This is bilinear on $I_{j-1}\times U$. The trace pairings are nondegenerate, since the field extensions are separable and the matrix trace pairing is nondegenerate. Since $U$ maps onto the full product, vanishing for every basis $y$ is equivalent to $x_a=0$ for the components with $v_p(m_a)=j$. The next $I_j$ is exactly the claimed inverse image. After $j=\lfloor\log_p\dim V\rfloor$, all components have been removed and the result is $J(U)$. This proves a parallel implementation using a logarithmic number of stages, each giving a homogeneous linear system whose coefficients are characteristic polynomial coefficients of products of known basis elements.

For clarity, deterministic field rank and nullspace bases here need no modular inversion. For a matrix $A$ over $\mathbb F_p$ the polynomial
$$
\det(I+z\,\operatorname{diag}(T^i)_i A\,\operatorname{diag}(T^j)_j A^{\mathsf T})
$$
has $z$-degree equal to $\operatorname{rank}(A)$. The principal-minor expansion and Cauchy–Binet express the degree-$r$ coefficient as a sum of squares of $r$-minors weighted by $T$ to the sum of their row and column indices. At the maximum rank $r$, write a rank factorization $A=XY$ for the proof. A nonzero $r$-minor uses a row basis of $X$ and a column basis of $Y$, independently. Distinct weights isolate one minimum-weight basis on each side, so the least-weight nonzero minor contributes a unique nonzero term to that coefficient. Polynomial degrees are bounded by a polynomial in the matrix dimensions, and determinant evaluation over the polynomial ring uses division-free parallel arithmetic. Pivot rows and columns are identified by rank increments on ordered prefixes. For an invertible pivot square $P$, each free coordinate can be set to $\det P$ with all other free coordinates zero, and the pivot coordinates obtained from the adjugate multiplied by the negative corresponding column. This gives a scaled nullspace basis. Keep every intermediate basis array padded to the ambient dimension using zero columns in nonpivot positions. Rank, pivot selection, and coefficient extraction therefore use fixed-size arrays at every stage. Compose the stages as uniform polynomial-size, polylog-depth Boolean circuits on the compact matrix data. [Chistov–Ivanyos–Karpinski, polynomial-time algorithms for modules over finite-dimensional algebras](https://doi.org/10.1145/258726.258751) · [Friedl–Rónyai, Polynomial time solutions of some problems in computational algebra](https://doi.org/10.1145/22145.22162)

Let $e,f\in E$ be the complementary projections onto the two diagrams. They are conjugate by a unit exactly when the two diagrams are isomorphic. Put $S=E_0/J(E_0)$, with the same letters for the projected idempotents. They are conjugate in $S$ if and only if they have the same rank in each matrix-ring component. This is equivalent to the single rank-dimension test
$$
\dim_{\mathbb F_p}eSe+\dim_{\mathbb F_p}fSf
-2\dim_{\mathbb F_p}eSf=0.
$$
In a component over $\mathbb F_{p^{f_a}}$ the contribution is $f_a(r_{e,a}-r_{f,a})^2$, where $r_{e,a},r_{f,a}$ are the two ranks. The dimensions can be computed as the ranks of the spans of projected products $eE_0e+J(E_0)$, $fE_0f+J(E_0)$, and $eE_0f+J(E_0)$, less the radical dimension each time. A conjugating unit in $S$ lifts to a unit in $E$. After that conjugation the resulting $e'$ is congruent to $f$ modulo $J(E)$. The element $f e'+(1-f)(1-e')$ is a unit since it is congruent to 1 modulo $J(E)$, and it intertwines $e'$ and $f$. Thus the quotient test is exact.

## Space bound

Let $N$ be the length of the original table input. The diagram uses $O(\log N)$ cyclic factors and every coordinate modulus has $O(\log N)$ bits. All compact matrices, including the linear congruence systems and those used in the radical test, have bit length $(\log N)^{O(1)}$. The deterministic circuit constructions above have polynomial size and polylogarithmic depth in this compact length, with uniform gate indexing. Evaluating one output of such a bounded-fan-in circuit by depth-first recursion uses $(\log\log N)^{O(1)}$ bits. A requested virtual input bit can be generated in $O(\log N)$ space while holding that recursion stack. Compact circuit evaluation may query a basis entry of $E_0$; this uses one residue scan and then the congruence circuit, whose inputs are provided by the original affine-data and Abelian-coordinate routines. There is a fixed number of such bit-oracle interfaces. The logarithmically many radical stages are composed inside the compact circuit, so they do not add logarithmically many copies of the raw-table workspace. The outer loop over prime factors retains only $O(\log N)$ bits. This gives the claimed deterministic logspace algorithm.

Informal proof. The targeted literature search found the affine classification, module-isomorphism and radical algorithms, but no previous logspace answer to Question 7.5 of the cited paper.


## Compact parallel decision without residue enumeration

There is also a deterministic uniform NC algorithm for the diagram-isomorphism problem when the cyclic primary factors and the arrows in cyclic coordinates are supplied directly. Here each modulus $p^e$ is given in its ordinary binary representation along with $p$. The bound is in the total length of this expanded input. In particular, the circuit does not scan the possible residues in $\mathbb F_p$.

It suffices to replace the residue scan in the homogeneous congruence computation. Let $D=[L\mid\operatorname{diag}(p^{e_i})]$ have $h$ rows and column lattice $\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb Z_p^h$. For $j\ge0$, form $D'_j=[D_{\le j}\mid pD]$, where $D_{\le j}$ consists of the first $j$ columns of $D$. These generate intermediate lattices $\Lambda_j$ containing $p\Lambda$. The dimension of $\Lambda_j/p\Lambda$ over $\mathbb F_p$ is
$$
r_j=h+v(D)-v(D'_j).
$$
This follows by multiplying indices along $p\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda_j\subseteq\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb Z_p^h$. Keep column $j$ of $D$ exactly when $r_j>r_{j-1}$. The selected columns reduce to a basis of $\Lambda/p\Lambda$, so their square matrix $B$ generates $\Lambda$ over $\mathbb Z_p$. Indeed, in any $\mathbb Z_p$-basis of $\Lambda$ the coordinate matrix of the selected columns has determinant a $p$-adic unit.

Compute the integer matrix
$$
C=p^{-v(D)}\operatorname{adj}(B)D.
$$
Every entry of $\operatorname{adj}(B)D$ is divisible by $p^{v(D)}$, because $B^{-1}D$ has entries in $\mathbb Z_p$ and $\det B$ has valuation $v(D)$. After reduction modulo $p$, $C$ is a nonzero scalar multiple of the reduction of $B^{-1}D$. The columns of $B^{-1}D$ selected by the prefix ranks are columns of an identity matrix. Therefore every vector in $\ker(C\bmod p)$ lifts to an exact kernel vector over $\mathbb Z_p$: choose any lift, compute its image under $B^{-1}D$, and subtract that image in the coordinates of the identity columns. The image is divisible by $p$, so this leaves the residues of the lift unchanged. Projecting $\ker(C\bmod p)$ onto the original unknown coordinates thus gives precisely the modulo-$p$ image of the solutions of $Lx\equiv0$ with row moduli $p^{e_i}$. The $p$-adic solutions give integer congruence solutions with the same residues by choosing integer representatives modulo $p^{\max(1,\max_i e_i)}$.

Use the determinant valuation routine on all $D'_j$ in parallel. The scaled diagonal columns give $v(D'_j)\le h+\sum_i e_i$; all determinant-polynomial calculations therefore need at most $p^{2(h+\sum_i e_i)+1}$ as modulus. Its bit length is polynomial in the expanded input length, as are the dimensions, all polynomial degrees and all integer adjugate entries by the determinant bound on the minors of $B$. The division by $p^{v(D)}$ is ordinary binary exact division. Select a basis of the projected kernel by the same prefix-rank procedure with fixed-size padded arrays. The radical and idempotent comparison circuits then give the compact NC verdict, taking the conjunction over the supplied primary components. This route also gives the raw-table logspace algorithm, by evaluating the compact circuit on the logspace bit oracle for the affine coordinates.
