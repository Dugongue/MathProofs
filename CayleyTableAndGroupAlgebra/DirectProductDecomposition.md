# Fully refined direct product decomposition of Cayley-table groups in logspace

We give a deterministic logspace transducer that outputs a fully refined internal direct product decomposition of an arbitrary finite group given by its multiplication table. Factors may be written as lists of original group elements, or by their characteristic vectors. This answers Question 7.3 of Johnson, Levet, Vojtěchovský and Widholm. Their parallel algorithm and the earlier algorithm of Kayal and Nezhmetdinov use the conjugacy-class graph. We retain that graph, replace their abelian stripping and group division with fixed compositions of logspace procedures, and use disjoint masks to select the nonabelian factors without recursion over successive group complements. [Question 7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06478v3#S7) · [Kayal–Nezhmetdinov](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/eccc-reports/2008/TR08-074/)

## 1. Logspace interfaces and graded abelian bases

Write $n=|G|$. An element label, a counter up to $n^{O(1)}$, and a mask of at most $\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor$ bits each fit in $O(\log n)$ space. Elements of any logspace-definable subgroup can be scanned in label order. For such a subgroup and any logspace-definable generating subset, subgroup membership is decidable in logspace by undirected connectivity in the Cayley graph on the available group elements. Its adjacency is computable by a scan of the generator predicate, allowing a generator or its inverse. Undirected connectivity is in deterministic logspace. An abelianization or other quotient by a logspace-definable normal subgroup is accessed through least-labeled coset representatives. For example the derived subgroup by itself is defined by a subgroup-membership test with the easily scanned set of commutators. A fixed number of such implicit transducer compositions still uses $O(\log n)$ work space. No recursion of unbounded depth is implicit in these observations. [Reingold, Undirected connectivity in log-space](https://doi.org/10.1145/1391289.1391291)

For a finite abelian $p$-group $B$, use additive notation and put
$$
W_k(B)=B[p]\cap p^{k-1}B,\qquad U_k(B)=W_k(B)/W_{k+1}(B),\qquad k\ge1.
$$
The groups $W_k(B)$ are descending subspaces of the $p$-torsion. In a cyclic decomposition of $B$, the bottom element of a cyclic summand of order $p^\ell$ contributes one dimension to $U_k(B)$ precisely when $\ell=k$. In particular $\prod_k p^{k\dim U_k(B)}=|B|$.

Suppose we have bottom vectors $w_{k,i}$ whose classes form a basis of $U_k(B)$ for every $k$. Choose any lift $a_{k,i}$ with $p^{k-1}a_{k,i}=w_{k,i}$. It has order $p^k$. The bottom vectors, over all $k$, are a basis of $B[p]$ by the descending filtration. Thus the map from the direct sum of abstract cyclic groups of orders $p^k$ to $B$ sending their generators to the $a_{k,i}$ is injective: any nonzero kernel in a finite abelian $p$-group would contain an element of order $p$. Its order is $|B|$, so it is an isomorphism. The same argument proves that prescribed cyclic generators whose bottoms are independent within their own grades extend to a cyclic basis.

The basis selection is logspace. Membership in $W_k(B)$ uses a scan for a preimage under $p^{k-1}$ and a check of $p$-torsion. Choose the bottom vectors greedily by label order. A bottom vector is selected exactly when it is outside the subgroup generated by $W_{k+1}(B)$ together with all earlier candidate bottoms. This uses the entire earlier prefix as a generator predicate and never stores the earlier selected vectors. For a prescribed independent family include all of its bottoms in the appropriate generator predicate before the scan. A chosen bottom has a least lift obtained by scanning $B$. For general abelian $B$, scan the primes and process the $p$-primary parts separately. Testing primality by trial division, doing group powers and enumerating prime powers at most $n$ all fit the space bound. Membership in the subgroup generated by any selected cyclic basis subfamily is again an implicit Cayley-graph query.

## 2. Abelian stripping and group division in FL

We first split an arbitrary finite group $X$ as $X=A\times H$, where $A$ is abelian and $H$ has no nontrivial abelian direct factor. Let $Z=Z(X)$, $Q=X/[X,X]$, and $j:Z\to Q$ be the natural map. This map preserves each prime and its filtrations $W_k$, and induces linear maps on the $U_k$.

In each grade of each prime, choose a basis for the image of the induced map by scanning the candidate bottoms of $Z$ in label order. The selection test asks whether $j(w)$ belongs to the subgroup of $Q$ generated by its next grade $W_{k+1}(Q)$ and the images of all preceding candidate bottoms in $W_k(Z)$. Retain a least lift $a$ for each selected $w$ in $Z$. Its image $j(a)$ has order $p^k$ and bottom $j(w)$. The selected bottoms are independent in the grades of both $Z$ and $Q$. Consequently these lifts generate an abelian subgroup $A$ that maps isomorphically to a direct summand $j(A)$ of $Q$. Use the extension procedure in Section 1 to find a complement $Q_0$ of $j(A)$, and set $H$ to the preimage of $Q_0$. Since $A$ is central and maps isomorphically onto $Q/Q_0$, we have $X=A\times H$.

If $H$ had a nontrivial abelian direct factor it would have a cyclic prime-power direct factor of some order $p^k$. Such a factor would be central in $X$. Its bottom, together with the selected length-$k$ bottoms of $A$, would have independent classes in $U_k(Q)$, since abelianization preserves an abelian direct factor as a direct factor. Yet this bottom is in $j(W_k(Z))$, whose image in that grade was already spanned by the selected bottoms. This is impossible. All operations above are a fixed number of logspace subroutine layers. In particular the derived subgroup, quotient representatives, image-grade membership, and cyclic complement construction have no depth depending on the number of grades. The grades are scanned, with each selection predicate built from unfiltered preceding candidates.

Next, given a subgroup $P$ of $X$ by a membership predicate, decide whether it is an internal direct factor of $X$ and find a complement when one exists. Let $C=C_X(P)$. Check $X=PC$ by scans; this check is necessary. Put $D=P\cap C=Z(P)$. The subgroup $D$ is central in $C$. If $X=PC$, a complement to $P$ in $X$ exists exactly when $D$ has a complement in $C$. One direction follows by intersecting a direct-product presentation with the centralizer. In the other direction, $C=D\times B$ gives $X=PB$, $P\cap B=1$, and commutation.

For any central $D\le C$, splitting $D$ off is equivalent to its mapping isomorphically onto a direct summand of $C/[C,C]$. Necessity follows by abelianizing a product. For sufficiency take the preimage of an abelian complement, as above. Compute a cyclic basis of $D$ by Section 1. Test independence of its mapped bottoms in every corresponding grade of $C/[C,C]$ by scanning the chosen generators in order, using subgroup membership with the preceding chosen generators' bottoms and $W_{k+1}$. The basis lemma proves that this test is exact, including the injectivity of the map on $D$. Its cyclic basis extension constructs the abelian complement when the test succeeds. This proves the unrestricted group-division procedure is in FL.

## 3. The conjugacy-class masks

We use the graph of Kayal and Nezhmetdinov. Call a noncentral conjugacy class reducible if it is the product of two commuting noncentral conjugacy classes and its size is the product of their sizes. Its other noncentral classes are the vertices of the graph. Join two when they fail to commute elementwise. The graph, its connected components, and the least label used for each component are accessible in FL. Conjugacy, commuting, class products and class counts can be checked by scanning the table, and connectedness is undirected connectivity. These are also the ingredients of Johnson et al.'s Lemma 4.12.

Let the graph have $t$ components. The center together with the elements of the irreducible classes generates $X$: repeatedly break reducible classes into smaller noncentral classes, with sizes decreasing because both factors have size at least two. Class members obtained by this procedure lie in the subgroup generated by the irreducible classes and the center. For a set $S$ of components, let $X_S$ be the subgroup generated by the center of $X$ and the class members in $S$. Then $X=X_S X_{S^c}$, and these two subgroups commute. Their intersection is central, and contains the center. It follows that $X_S=C_X(X_{S^c})$: in a product $xy\in X_S X_{S^c}$ that commutes with $X_{S^c}$, the element $y$ is central in $X_{S^c}$, and also commutes with $X_S$, so is central in $X$. In particular $Z(X_S)=Z(X)$. Applying the intersection argument to each component separately gives that $X/Z(X)$ is the direct product of the nontrivial component quotients $X_{\{j\}}/Z(X)$. Thus $t\le\log_2 n$ and distinct masks give distinct subgroups $X_S/Z(X)$.

Fix for the proof a fully refined direct product $X=A\times P_1\times\cdots\times P_b$, with the abelian factor $A$ from Section 2 and nonabelian indecomposable factors $P_i$. This reference decomposition need not be computed. Every irreducible class has noncentral projection to exactly one $P_i$. Indeed conjugacy classes decompose as the product of their coordinate classes, and two or more noncentral coordinates would give a reducible class by splitting off one such coordinate. Classes corresponding to different $P_i$ commute. Thus every graph component belongs to exactly one $P_i$. Since the irreducible classes generate modulo the center, the components assigned to $P_i$ together generate its exact quotient modulo the center. Write $S_i$ for the nonempty block of components assigned to $P_i$.

For each mask $S$, apply Section 2 to $X_S$, obtaining a central abelian factor $A_S$ and a complement $H_S$ with no abelian direct factor. Run group division for $H_S$ in $X$. The test succeeds exactly when $S$ is a union of blocks $S_i$. To show this, use the central-automorphism form of Krull–Remak–Schmidt, recalled in Johnson et al. Theorem 3.4. A central automorphism takes the indecomposable factors of one fully refined direct decomposition to those of any other, up to reordering, and induces the identity modulo the center.

If $S$ is a union of blocks, then $X_S$ is the product of the selected $P_i$ and a central abelian group. The central-automorphism theorem on $X_S$ takes the product of its reference nonabelian factors to the computed $H_S$. This automorphism extends to $X$ as follows. Write $X_S=P\times C_0$, with $P$ the product of the selected factors and $C_0$ the product of $A$ and the centers of the unselected factors. The restriction of this central automorphism to $P$ projects to a central endomorphism of $P$ inducing the identity modulo $Z(P)$. This projection is invertible by the lemma below, because $P$ has no abelian direct factor. Consequently $H_S$ is the graph of a homomorphism $P\to C_0$. Such a graph splits off $X$: leave every unselected factor and $A$ unchanged and shear each $p\in P$ by the central value of that homomorphism. The shear is invertible by subtracting the same value in the complementary coordinate. Conversely, if $H_S$ splits off $X$, refine that split to a fully refined product and apply the central-automorphism theorem on $X$. Its image modulo $Z(X)$ is the product associated to a subset of the reference $P_i$, because $H_S$ has no abelian factor. Since $A_S\subseteq Z(X_S)=Z(X)$, this image is exactly $X_S/Z(X)$. Distinct masks have distinct images, so $S$ must be the corresponding union of blocks.

The elementary fact used here is that a finite group $P$ with no abelian direct factor has no noninvertible endomorphism inducing the identity modulo its center. Choose a power of any such endomorphism for which the kernels and images stabilize. The kernel is central because the induced map modulo the center is the identity. The stable kernel and stable image have trivial intersection and orders multiplying to $|P|$. They commute, so give a direct product. Any nontrivial kernel would be an abelian direct factor. The absence of abelian direct factors for a product of nonabelian indecomposables follows from Krull–Remak–Schmidt.

## 4. Produce every factor in one pass

The inclusion-minimal nonempty successful masks are therefore exactly $S_1,\ldots,S_b$. They can be enumerated by two nested mask scans. Every mask fits in logarithmic space and there are at most $n$ masks. Testing minimality needs only rerunning the success test on proper submasks. This procedure is uniform and uses a fixed number of logspace compositions.

It remains to show that the independently selected $H_{S_i}$ actually form a single direct product together with the already chosen $A$. For $S_i$, the graph description above shows $H_{S_i}$ is the graph of a homomorphism from $P_i$ to the product of $A$ and the other factors' centers. Sending every $P_i$ to its graph, and fixing $A$, defines an endomorphism $\Phi:X\to X$, because these graphs commute for distinct $i$. It induces an endomorphism on $X/A$ which is the identity modulo the center of $X/A$. The previous elementary fact makes this induced map invertible, since $X/A$ has no abelian direct factors. As $\Phi$ is also the identity on $A$, it is invertible on $X$. Hence the selected subgroups give an internal direct product, with each $H_{S_i}\cong P_i$ indecomposable.

Output the factors as lists of original labels by scanning membership for each minimal successful mask. If multiplication tables are desired, scan pairs of labels in each factor and look up their product in the input. Append the prime-power cyclic subgroups of the computed $A$, which are produced by Section 1. The identity group gives the empty list, and an abelian group has an empty conjugacy graph. The output uses only logarithmic work space, including the storage of indices and all current masks.

The graph method and the central-automorphism refinement theorem are prior work. The two replacements here are the use of graded abelian images to compute both splitting operations without storing successive generators, and the simultaneous choice of factors via minimal component masks. This is an informal proof of the full Cayley-table question.

## 5. Canonization when the factors have bounded generator number

For every fixed $d$, the same result puts canonization in FL on the class of groups whose directly indecomposable factors are each at most $d$-generated. Apply the decomposition above and then Johnson et al.'s logspace canonization for $d$-generated groups to each factor. Rank the canonical factor tables lexicographically, using scans and rereading their entries on demand. Break ties using their order of occurrence. Since equal tables describe identical labelled operations, the tie rule does not change the resulting table. The ordered product has a canonical mixed-radix table. A product entry is computed one factor at a time, and its digits are assembled with a logarithmic-space running integer bounded by the order of the whole group. Krull–Remak–Schmidt identifies the multiset of factor types independently of the chosen decomposition. Thus the product table is canonical. For a canonical relabelling of the input elements, find each element's corresponding factor coordinate by testing whether its product with the inverse of a proposed coordinate belongs to the subgroup generated by the other factors. This uses the same implicit subgroup membership and fixed composition as before. [Johnson et al., Lemma 5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06478v3#S5)
