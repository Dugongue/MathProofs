# Every additive denominator spectrum in restricted assignment

For a restricted-assignment instance, a denominator \(d\ge 1\) is feasible at a given configuration-LP threshold if some feasible configuration weights all belong to \(d^{-1}\mathbb Z\). The feasible denominators are closed under addition, by weighted averaging of two feasible points. The following theorem realizes every possibility, even with bounded processing times and at most three eligible machines per job.

**Theorem.** Given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_s\) in binary, in deterministic time polynomial in their total bit length one can construct a restricted-assignment instance with processing times in \(\{1,3\}\), each job eligible on at most three machines, and optimal configuration-LP threshold \(3\), whose feasible denominators at threshold \(3\) are exactly

\[
\left\{\sum_{i=1}^s a_i n_i : n_i\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\right\}\setminus\{0\}.
\]

Its integer optimum is \(3\) if some \(a_i=1\) and \(4\) otherwise. Thus unbounded required denominators occur on the single exact-gap stratum \(4/3\), and there is no universal finite set of denominators to test. Every nonzero additive submonoid of \(\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) is covered by the theorem. Indeed, relative to its least positive element, choose the smallest member in each residue class it meets, and include the least positive element itself. Together they generate it.

Wu’s counterexample to Conjecture 10 of Encz, Mastrolilli and Vercesi gets its obstruction from a saturated load-three face. The construction here uses that face for a bounded-occurrence addition circuit and uses a path of filler jobs to preserve every denominator. The prescribed additive spectra are the same kind of data as the MathProofs construction for fair binary observations, here without the factor of two. [Wu’s configuration-LP counterexample](https://kenwu-lab.wooqianghao.workers.dev/papers/half-integral-configuration-lp/) · [Encz–Mastrolilli–Vercesi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19967v1) · [Fair binary observation spectra](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/SimultaneousPartiallyObservedLaws/Empirical.md).

**Polytope theorem.** Given a nonempty rational polytope \(P\subseteq[0,1]^n\) by rational inequalities, one can construct in polynomial binary time a restricted-assignment optimal configuration LP with an affine surjection onto \(P\). Its processing times are in \(\{1,3\}\), each job is eligible on at most three machines, the LP threshold is \(3\), and the integer optimum is \(4\). There is an explicit integer \(D\ge2\) such that every feasible denominator is a multiple of \(D\), and for each \(d\ge1\) the image of the \((Dd)^{-1}\mathbb Z\)-grid is exactly \(P\cap d^{-1}\mathbb Z^n\). Each fiber is affinely isomorphic to a transportation polytope. There is one continuous piecewise rational-affine section respecting all these grids simultaneously. This gives a configuration-LP counterpart to affine universality with controlled fibers. [MathProofs rank-two universality](https://github.com/Dugongue/MathProofs/blob/main/RankTwoGeometricUniversality/PROOF.md).

**Exponential denominator theorem.** For every integer \(a\ge2\), an instance with \(O(\log a)\) jobs and machines already realizes feasible denominators exactly \(a\mathbb Z_{>0}\), with the same job degree, processing times, and exact optimum gap \(4/3\). For every feasible configuration LP at threshold \(3\) on \(r\) jobs and machines combined with processing times in \(\{1,3\}\), some denominator is at most \(2^r\). Thus the exponential scale in the number of jobs and machines is sharp.

Classical transportation universality realizes general linear and integer programs as slim three-way tables. A more recent result gives universality and arbitrary vertex denominators for tables with sparse lines and small line sums. Here the targets are whole optimal configuration LPs of restricted-assignment scheduling. The grid condition constrains every feasible point, and the integer optimum stays four independently of the represented polytope or minimum denominator. [De Loera and Onn](https://doi.org/10.1137/040610623) · [Yu](https://optimization-online.org/2026/10/a-sharp-local-threshold-for-integer-feasibility-in-three-way-transportation-polytopes/).

## Bounded-occurrence unit sums

Begin with nonnegative coordinates \(x_i\) satisfying \(\sum_i a_i x_i=1\). Introduce registers for addition and doubling, using binary addition chains and a final sum, and force the final output to equal one. Each register evaluates to a nonnegative number at most one, since its intermediate sum is bounded by the final sum. A gate \(u+v=w\) is represented by \(u+v+c=1\) and \(w+c=1\), with a fresh complement \(c\). This description includes the case \(u=v\). Every auxiliary value is an integral affine function of the inputs. Thus solutions over the real numbers correspond exactly, and the correspondence preserves \(d^{-1}\mathbb Z\) in both directions for every \(d\). The total number of registers and equations is polynomial in the binary input length.

Replace the \(k\) occurrences of each variable by distinct variables \(x_1',\ldots,x_k'\), and impose the equalities between consecutive copies by adding \(x_i'+c_i=1\) and \(x_{i+1}'+c_i=1\), with fresh \(c_i\). Now each equation has at most three distinct variables and each variable occurs at most three times. This step also preserves the solution set and all the grids. Make three identical copies of the entire system. For each variable \(x\) with \(r<3\) occurrences in the system before copying, add \(3-r\) fresh variables \(c\), each with the three equations \(x^{(j)}+c=1\) for \(j=1,2,3\). Every variable now occurs in exactly three distinct equations, and rows still have at most three variables. Projection to the first copy preserves each grid. Every solution of the original system lifts on the same grid by duplicating it in all three copies and assigning the new complements \(1-x\). The final number \(q\) of equations is a multiple of three.

## From unit sums to configuration weights

Create a machine for each of the \(m\) variables in the final system. Create a size-one job for each row, eligible precisely on its variable machines. Every variable machine has exactly three eligible size-one jobs. Set \(f=m-q/3\). This is a nonnegative integer: the row sums imply \(3\sum_j x_j=q\), and the system is nonempty with all \(x_j\le 1\). In the first instance, add \(f\) size-three jobs each eligible on all variable machines. The total processing time is \(q+3f=3m\).

At threshold three, every feasible point has each job covered with weight exactly one, machine weight exactly one, and positive weight only on configurations of load three. In fact the total processing-time-weighted coverage is at least \(3m\), while each machine contributes at most three. Equality forces all stated conditions since job sizes are positive. A variable machine can only use its unique three-unit-job configuration or one size-three filler. The weights \(x_j\) of the former configurations satisfy the original unit sums. The filler variables form a transportation problem: each size-three job supplies one, machine \(j\) demands \(1-x_j\), and the totals agree. Complete eligibility gives a solution by successive allocation of the smaller residual supply and demand. If the \(x_j\) lie on the \(d^{-1}\mathbb Z\)-grid, that algorithm stays on the grid. Conversely reading the point-configuration weights preserves the grid.

## Bounded eligibility and exact integer gap

Replace each original filler by a chain. Number the \(m\) original machines in any order. Introduce size-three jobs \(F_1,\ldots,F_m\) and \(m-1\) new dummy machines \(D_1,\ldots,D_{m-1}\); each \(F_j\) is eligible on original machine \(j\), on \(D_{j-1}\) if it exists, and on \(D_j\) if it exists. Use a separate chain for each filler. The new instance stays saturated because this adds \(m-1\) size-three jobs and \(m-1\) machines per filler. At threshold three each dummy machine is fully used by its two adjacent jobs and supports no other jobs.

Write \(b_j\) for the weight contributed by \(F_j\) to original machine \(j\). The row and machine sums in the chain imply \(\sum_j b_j=1\). Conversely, any such nonnegative vector lifts uniquely in the chain. The \(F_j\)-weight on \(D_j\) is \(1-\sum_{i\le j} b_i\), and the \(F_{j+1}\)-weight on \(D_j\) is \(\sum_{i\le j} b_i\). These are nonnegative and lie on the same grid as the \(b_j\). The chain therefore leaves the spectrum unchanged and bounds filler eligibility by three.

To guarantee a schedule at threshold four, give the first filler \(F_1\) in each chain one more eligible machine \(R\). At most two machines were previously eligible for \(F_1\). For this chain add three private machines \(B_1,B_2,B_3\), each with a private exclusive size-three job. Add three size-one jobs, one per index \(i\), eligible precisely on \(R,B_i\). This adds total size twelve and four machines, so saturation persists. At threshold three each \(B_i\) is occupied exclusively by its private filler. The three new unit jobs must therefore all be on \(R\), which cannot take positive weight from \(F_1\). Thus the spectrum is unchanged. At threshold four put each new unit job on its \(B_i\) alongside the private filler, put \(F_1\) on \(R\), and put \(F_j\) on \(D_{j-1}\) for \(2\le j\le m\). The original variable machines are free of fillers, and each may receive all its at most three incident original unit jobs assigned to it. Choose any eligible original machine for each original unit job. This gives the claimed schedule.

The total processing size is still three times the machine count, so no threshold less than three is feasible even fractionally. A grid point of denominator one at threshold three is exactly a feasible integral schedule there, because all job and machine sums on the saturated face are exactly one. Finally \(x_i=n_i/d\) in the starting relation if and only if \(\sum_i a_i n_i=d\). This identifies the spectrum and the integer optimum. The number of fillers is at most the number of original machines, so the chains and reserve gadgets preserve polynomial size.

## Proof of the polytope theorem

Clear denominators separately in each input inequality with a positive integer multiplier. Write the resulting inequality rows as \(\sum_j a_j u_j\le b\), with integer coefficients. Choose one integer \(D\ge2\) at least \(\sum_j |a_j|+|b|\) for all rows, and use inputs \(x_j=u_j/D\) and a variable \(\delta\) with \(D\delta=1\). Build \(D\delta\) by binary doubling and addition and pin the output to one. Use nonnegative slack \(v_j\) in \(x_j+v_j=\delta\) to impose \(0\le x_j\le\delta\). For each inequality use another nonnegative slack \(s\) and impose

\[
\sum_{a_j>0}a_j x_j+\max\{-b,0\}\delta+s
=\sum_{a_j<0}(-a_j)x_j+\max\{b,0\}\delta.
\]

Both displayed expressions, counting \(s\) wherever the equality holds, have values at most one. All fixed nonnegative multiplications and sums can therefore be compiled by the same unit-sum gate; use a pinned zero for an empty sum, implemented by \(z+w=1,w=1\). Equality of two evaluated registers \(r,t\) uses \(r+c=t+c=1\) with fresh complement \(c\). Each input and auxiliary variable is bounded, and every auxiliary output and slack is an affine function of \(x\) with integer coefficients or an integral affine function of \(x,\delta\). The unit-sum system is therefore affinely isomorphic to \(P\) by reading \(u=D x\). Its \((Dd)^{-1}\mathbb Z\)-grid points correspond exactly to the \(d^{-1}\mathbb Z\)-grid points of \(P\). Any feasible denominator must be a multiple of \(D\), because \(\delta=1/D\) is a coordinate.

The bounded-occurrence and triplication steps above are an affine isomorphism on variable assignments as well. To see the only extra issue, a variable of degree three before triplication must be an interior occurrence in one of the equality chains. Its associated consecutive-equality complement has degree two, so the padding equations identify that complement across all three copies. Its equation with the interior occurrence then identifies the latter across the copies too. Variables of degree at most two are directly identified by padding. Thus every point-configuration weight in the schedule is determined by \(u\), and depends on it affinely.

For a fixed \(u\), the only free variables are the original complete-eligibility filler weights. They form the transportation polytope with row supplies one and column demands \(1-x'_j\), where \(x'_j\) is the point-configuration weight at variable machine \(j\). The path filler variables lift uniquely by the displayed partial sums. The reserve coordinates are forced, and all configurations of load less than three have zero weight. This gives the fiber assertion. The successive allocation procedure chooses minima of residual supplies and demands in a fixed order, which is a continuous piecewise integral-affine function of the point-configuration weights. For example, its weight from filler \(i\) to machine \(j\) is the length of intersection of \([i-1,i]\) with the interval cut out by the preceding and current cumulative machine demands. This gives the stated section and respects every grid. The general construction provides an integral schedule at threshold four. The required denominator \(D\ge2\) forbids an integral schedule at threshold three. All gate and transport constructions are polynomial in the input length.

## Proof of the exponential denominator theorem

Use the one-variable system \(a x=1\) and its bounded-occurrence unit-sum compilation. There are \(O(\log a)\) point jobs and machines. Their point-configuration weights are unique, are explicit in \(a^{-1}\mathbb Z\), and include \(x=1/a\). Since the weights are known, a different filler construction uses only linearly many jobs and machines. Let the positive residual demands at the original variable machines be \(d_i\in(0,1]\), summing to the integer \(f\). For each such original machine add a pair of new dummy machines with no point jobs. Regard the demand list as a partition of an interval into adjacent blocks of lengths \(1,d_i,1\) for successive \(i\). The unit-length blocks belong to the private dummy machines. Partition the same interval into unit intervals in order, with one size-three filler for each unit interval. Its eligible machines are the blocks it meets in positive length. The intersection lengths give a feasible filler allocation, everywhere in \(a^{-1}\mathbb Z\), and the new instance is saturated since there are \(f\) more filler jobs than dummy machines. An interval meets at most one original demand block, since adjacent original demands are separated by two unit blocks. It meets at most two dummy blocks, since meeting three in positive length requires spanning a full unit block and more. Thus each filler has at most three eligible machines. A filler with three choices meets the two private dummy blocks next to its one original demand. Each original block participates in at most one such filler, and the corresponding private pairs are disjoint.

The positive-length allocation gives a fractional matching saturating the dummy machines in the bipartite graph between dummy machines and fillers, with filler load at most one. Hall's inequalities follow by summing that fractional matching, so an ordinary matching saturating all dummy machines exists. Moreover, there is one that also uses every degree-three filler. Start by matching each such filler to its private left dummy machine. These choices are disjoint. Extend to a maximum matching by augmenting paths, which never leave a formerly matched vertex unmatched. Hence each unmatched filler has at most two choices. Give each unmatched filler one additional reserve machine and the three private buffers used in the earlier threshold-four construction. The reserve alternative is dormant at threshold three. At threshold four send each matched filler to its assigned dummy machine, each unmatched one to its reserve, and each reserve point job to its private buffer. The original point jobs can again be assigned arbitrarily among their eligible variable machines, using at most three per machine. The complete construction is linear in the number of unit-sum variables and equations. The known fractional allocation works on the \(a^{-1}\mathbb Z\)-grid, and every denominator is divisible by \(a\) because \(x=1/a\) is forced. These facts prove the construction and its exact gap.

For the general upper bound, add machine and job slack variables to write the configuration LP as a system of \(r\) linear equalities in nonnegative variables with integral right-hand side. A configuration column has a machine entry and at most three job entries of absolute value one. Each slack column has just one nonzero entry of absolute value one. Choose a feasible point of minimum positive support, whose positive columns are linearly independent. Indeed a linear dependence permits moving along the dependence to make at least one supported coordinate zero while preserving all other coordinates nonnegative. An invertible square row minor on the positive columns determines the nonzero coordinates. By Cramer's rule they have a common denominator at most the absolute value of that minor's determinant. Every minor column has Euclidean norm at most two, and there are at most \(r\) columns. Hadamard's inequality bounds the determinant by \(2^r\), as asserted.
