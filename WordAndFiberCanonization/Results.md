# Canonical words, perfect matchings and local invariants

Four informal proof routes extend the distinction between computing a complete invariant and choosing an actual representative.

1. [Intrinsic word normal forms in a fixed integral linear group](LinearGroups.md). A fixed finitely generated subgroup of the direct product of two free groups embeds in SL₄(ℤ). Its word equality has a logspace complete invariant. Canonization over its own generators is in FL exactly when L = NP, and in FP exactly when P = NP. The same construction gives a quantitative gap for intrinsic word length. This characterizes the existence question in Fortnow–Grochow §6.1 and the strict-inclusion question in Elder–Elston–Ostheimer §9. Whether L equals NP remains open. [Fortnow–Grochow](https://arxiv.org/html/0907.4775v2#S6.SS1) · [Elder–Elston–Ostheimer](https://cs.hofstra.edu/~cscgzo/Research/logspaceNormalForms.pdf#page=18)
2. [Supplied perfect matchings in bounded-degree hypergraphs](Matching.md). Given a labelled host and one perfect matching, return the same perfect matching for every choice of supplied matching. On ordinary graphs this is polynomial-time. On simple 3-partite, 3-uniform hypergraphs with each vertex in at most three triples, it is complete for canonizing equal-length polynomial-time kernels. Grohe's earlier SAT-witness canonization theorem supplies the nonuniform lower bound. [Grohe, §3.4](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0028017)
3. [Average-case selection and group products](Parallel.md). On an explicit two-member matching, an inverse-polynomial advantage over returning the input on uniform records recovers worst-case group products. With the alternating group on five letters this gives a characterization of the nonuniform ACC⁰ versus NC¹ containment. [Barrington](https://people.cs.umass.edu/~barring/publications/bwbp.pdf) · [Moitra–Mossel–Sandon](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v125/moitra20a/moitra20a.pdf)
4. [Sparse measurements of rank-one matrices](RankOne.md) and [Boolean locality](Locality.md). One-entry measurements admit polynomial-time canonical completion. Two entries in the same row or column per measurement suffice to encode every Boolean circuit fiber, even when a feasible symmetric Boolean rank-one matrix is supplied. For the underlying Boolean invariants, universal logspace canonization with two input bits per output bit is equivalent to L = NL. At three bits the criterion becomes L = NP. The Boolean basis theorem explains when the polynomial-time case is tractable.

## Measurements confined to rows

There is a further field-size distinction if every measurement in the list is supported within a row, and the domain is unrestricted rank-one matrices. Over the binary field there is a polynomial-time canonical choice even with arbitrarily many entries in each such measurement. Write the matrix as u vᵀ. Every row with a nonzero reading must have its u coordinate equal to 1. Solve the resulting linear equations on v across all those rows, including their zero readings, choosing a fixed solution by row reduction in index order. Set all other row factors to zero. If there was a nonzero reading, v is nonzero and this gives rank exactly one. If all readings vanish, find the first row whose homogeneous system permits a nonzero v. Set only that row factor to 1 and choose a canonical nonzero null vector there. Feasibility of an exact-rank-one matrix guarantees such a row.

Over each fixed field with q at least three elements, even feasibility for one difference per row is NP-complete. From q-colorability of a graph with at least one edge, use one column per vertex and one row per edge. For each oriented edge e = ij require the reading M_(e,i) − M_(e,j) = 1. This is possible precisely when the column factors v_i form a proper coloring by field elements. Given the coloring, take u_e to be the reciprocal of the difference, and conversely the nonzero reading forces that difference to be nonzero. The problem is in NP by checking a candidate matrix. The hardness statement here concerns deciding feasibility without a supplied matrix. The binary polynomial-time result uses the unrestricted domain; imposing symmetry couples row and column factors.

The one-entry tractability statement concerns selecting a representative. Counting can already be hard there. Over the binary field, prescribe zero on the edges of a labelled bipartite graph with a rows and b columns. A nonzero rank-one matrix is uniquely determined by two nonempty sets of rows and columns, and is feasible exactly when their union is an independent set. Thus adding 2^a + 2^b − 1 to the exact number of feasible matrices gives the exact independent-set count. Adding one isolated vertex on each side supplies a known feasible singleton matrix and multiplies the total independent-set count by four, so this counting reduction also works with a feasible matrix supplied. [Provan and Ball, exact counting on bipartite graphs](https://doi.org/10.1137/0212053)

## A further quantitative consequence of the group construction

Here is a way to interpret the word-length approximation gap without assuming a conjecture about SAT. Suppose a polynomial-time algorithm for the group in the first proof always gives a feasible upper estimate for the shortest word length, within a factor m / exp(sqrt(log m)) on sufficiently long inputs of length m. Then 3SAT has a deterministic quasipolynomial-time algorithm.

Indeed, write n for the encoded formula length. The group reduction supplies a word of length m = O(nN + k²) for N = 2^k, with optimum at most (n+k)^c in the satisfiable case for a fixed c, and at least N in the unsatisfiable case. The explicit fallback can be padded to a chosen length Θ(nN+k²) with inverse pairs without changing the element (adjust parity if necessary). Take k = ceil(K(log(n+2))²) for a sufficiently large fixed K. Then exp(sqrt(log m)) exceeds n^(c+3) for large n, after accounting for the fixed log bases. A feasible upper estimate falls below N on satisfiable instances and is at least N otherwise. Constructing and processing the word costs exp(O((log n)²)) time. This implication strengthens the interpretation of the polynomial-reduction result when one allows a larger target instance. It does not assert a quasipolynomial SAT algorithm.

These are proofs on paper. Earlier results on group distortion, bounded membership, SAT witnesses, parity and group products, and Boolean constraint satisfaction supply essential ingredients. Priority for the exact combinations here has not been established.
