# Reconstructing rationally weighted complete graphs from unique-weight edges

## GP-0874

Let every edge of $K_n$ carry a rational weight, and let vertex-deleted cards preserve the exact weight values. The weighted deck determines the graph at orders three and four. For every $n\ge5$, it also determines the graph, by a deterministic polynomial-bit-time decoder, whenever edges of globally unique weight form a graph of minimum degree at least two. Repeated weights can be arbitrary in this theorem. The proof also gives a directly checkable sufficient condition for decks with lower minimum unique-weight degree.

### Recovering vertex labels from card deficits

An edge of any weight occurs in exactly $n-2$ cards. Summing occurrences and dividing by $n-2$ identifies every globally unique weight. Write $U$ for the set of these weights. Let $L(v)\subseteq U$ be the set of unique weights incident to vertex $v$. Each card supplies $L(v)$ for its deleted vertex: it is exactly the complement in $U$ of the unique weights present on that card. Thus the deck gives the whole multiset of these labels. If they are distinct, use the labels themselves as the global vertex set. Distinct labels intersect in at most one weight, and two labels intersect exactly when their vertices are joined by a unique-weight edge. This recovers the unique-weight graph $H$, including its labels, without needing to solve graph isomorphism.

In the card deleting $v$, a surviving vertex $u$ has the observable unique-weight signature $L(u)\setminus L(v)$. This signature is known for each labeled global survivor. If both endpoints of an edge have unique signatures among these survivors, read its weight directly from the given card. In particular, the conditions that all $L(v)$ are distinct and that every pair of surviving vertices can be separately identified on at least one card are sufficient and testable from the deck in polynomial time. Sorting rational weights, comparing signatures, and reading the edge entries use polynomial bit time.

### Minimum degree two suffices

When $H$ has minimum degree two, the global labels are distinct, since any two different vertices share at most one incident unique edge. The graph $H-v$ has minimum degree one, and equal signatures of two vertices occur exactly when they form a connected component consisting of their single shared edge. For a given endpoint $u$ to become ambiguous after a deletion, its original degree in $H$ must be two, and the removed vertex must be one of its two neighbors. Both of these deletions make $u$ ambiguous only if $u$ belongs to a triangle component of $H$. Indeed each of the two neighbors must then have degree two and be adjacent to the other neighbor and to $u$.

For $n\ge7$, an edge's two endpoints forbid at most four candidate deletions, whereas $n-2\ge5$ deletions avoid the endpoints. For $n=5$, minimum degree two rules out a triangle component because the complement would have at most two vertices. Thus each endpoint forbids at most one deletion and $n-2=3$ suffices. For $n=6$, if no triangle component exists the same argument works. Any triangle component forces the remaining three vertices to form another triangle. All weights inside these two triangles are already known from $H$. To recover edges joining the two triangles, regard their cross weights as a $3\times3$ matrix with labeled rows and columns. Deleting one row vertex leaves the columns fully identifiable by their two distinct incident unique weights. The two surviving row vertices may swap, but their label pair is known, since the deleted vertex is identified by the card deficit. Hence for each pair of rows the deck gives the unordered multiset of their two row vectors. This determines each row vector with its label. For example, for any vector value and fixed row $i$, add its multiplicities in the two pairs containing $i$, then subtract its multiplicity in the pair not containing $i$. The result is twice its multiplicity at $i$.

### Orders three and four

At order three every card consists of one weighted edge, and the multiset of the three weights suffices. At order four the deck gives the multiset of individual weights and the multiset of unordered pairs of weights on adjacent edges. Each adjacent pair occurs in exactly one triangle card, whereas opposite edges never occur together in a triangle. Subtract these counts from all unordered edge pairs to obtain the multiset of the three opposite weight pairs, with multiplicity.

The full action preserving the partition of the six edges into opposite pairs consists of arbitrary permutations of the three pairs and independent swaps in each pair. The vertex permutation group $S_4$ acts faithfully on the six edges. Its image has index two in this group of order $48$. More explicitly, choose an ordering of the two edges within each opposite pair so that the triangle edge sets are precisely the four ways of taking one edge per pair with an even number of second choices. A given ordering of the three pairs does not affect this parity. Swapping the two positions in an even number of pairs preserves the triangle sets and defines a subgroup of order $24$, which must equal the image of $S_4$.

Consequently the three opposite weight pairs leave at most two vertex-isomorphism possibilities. If a pair has equal weights, swapping its edges identifies the two possibilities. Otherwise let the ordered weights on the three pairs be $(a_i,b_i)$. Use a commuting indeterminate $x_w$ for each distinct weight. The difference between the two proposed triangle-deck generating polynomials is
$$
\prod_{i=1}^3(x_{a_i}-x_{b_i}),
$$
since the two triangle decks correspond to the two parities. This product is nonzero over $\mathbb Z$ because $a_i\ne b_i$ for every pair. Thus the actual triangle deck selects exactly one candidate.

This theorem concerns preserved numerical weights. It supplies a broad constructive sufficient condition for all orders and does not settle the unrestricted weighted conjecture.
