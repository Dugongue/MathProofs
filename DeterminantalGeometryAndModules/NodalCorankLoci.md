# Linear determinantal representations of rational nodal plane curves

This note gives an exact criterion for every prescribed nodal corank locus and constructs families over number fields for which the existence of a linear determinantal representation fails the local–global principle. Both results have written proofs with classical algebraic geometry and arithmetic inputs.

## Theorem 1. Prescribed corank loci

Let $k$ be a perfect field, let $C \subset \mathbb P^2_k$ be a geometrically integral plane curve of degree $d$ with only ordinary nodes and with geometric genus zero, and let $P \to C$ be its normalization. Thus $P$ is a smooth conic, possibly split. Let $S$ be any subset of the closed nodes. Set
$$
r=\sum_{p\text{ a closed node},\,p\notin S} [\kappa(p):k].
$$
A linear determinantal representation is a $d$ by $d$ matrix of linear forms over $k$ with determinant a nonzero scalar times a defining equation of $C$. A representation with corank two exactly at $S$ and corank one everywhere else on $C$ exists precisely in the following cases.

- If $k$ is infinite, either $P(k)\ne\varnothing$ or $r$ is odd.
- If $k=\mathbb F_q$ with $q\ge3$, every $S$ is possible.
- If $k=\mathbb F_2$, every $S$ is possible except when $r=1$ and the single remaining node has two $\mathbb F_2$-rational branches.

When the criterion holds, equivalence classes under constant invertible row and column transformations are the $k$-points of an explicitly given open subset of a product of tori, as described below.

For infinite $k$, ordinary linear determinantal representability is equivalent to the existence of a zero-cycle of degree one on $C$. If $P$ is split, the normalization provides a $k$-point away from the nodes. If $P$ is anisotropic, both conditions hold exactly when $C$ has an odd-degree closed node. In the anisotropic case with even arithmetic genus, the least possible degree of a corank-two locus is the least degree of an odd-degree node, whenever a representation exists.

### Proof of Theorem 1

**Linear matrices and sheaves.** Put $i:C\hookrightarrow \mathbb P^2_k$. A matrix as above gives a resolution
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(-2)^d
\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(-1)^d
\longrightarrow i_*F\longrightarrow 0. \tag{1}
$$
The adjugate annihilates the cokernel by the equation of $C$. At the generic point of $C$ the determinant has valuation one in the ambient discrete valuation ring, so $F$ has rank one. At any closed point in its support its depth is one by (1) over the smooth ambient surface. Thus $F$ is torsion-free on $C$. The resolution gives $H^0(C,F)=H^1(C,F)=0$.

Conversely let $F$ be torsion-free of rank one with $H^0(C,F)=H^1(C,F)=0$. Its Hilbert polynomial is $dt$ because the leading coefficient is the degree of its support, with generic rank one, and its constant coefficient is $\chi(F)=0$. Then $i_*F$ is $1$-regular, so its twist by one is generated by $d$ sections and has no higher cohomology. Write $K$ for the kernel of the resulting map $\mathcal O(-1)^d\to i_*F$. It is locally free of rank $d$, by the depth-one property of a torsion-free sheaf on a curve and the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula on the ambient surface. The defining map gives $H^1(K(1))=0$ and $H^2(K)=0$, so $K$ is $2$-regular. Consequently $K(2)$ is generated by its $h^0(K(2))=3d-2d=d$ sections. The surjection from a trivial rank-$d$ bundle to $K(2)$ is an isomorphism. This proves (1). Its determinant defines $C$ with multiplicity one. The two bases used for the evaluation and kernel maps account for constant invertible row and column transformations, so the equivalence classes correspond to the isomorphism classes of $F$.

**Partial normalization.** At a geometric node, the completed local ring and its normalization are
$$
A=\overline k[[u,v]]/(uv),\qquad
B=\overline k[[u]]\oplus\overline k[[v]].
$$
A torsion-free module of rank one on both branches is isomorphic to $A$ or $B$. To see this, embed it as a fractional ideal, and rescale in the total quotient ring so that its $B$-span is $B$. Its image contains the conductor $\mathfrak c$ since $\mathfrak cB=\mathfrak c$ and $\mathfrak c B M\subseteq M$. Its image in $B/\mathfrak c=\overline k^2$ projects onto both coordinates. A one-dimensional image can be rescaled by a unit of $B$ to the diagonal, giving $A$, and a two-dimensional image gives $B$. The fiber dimensions over $A$ of these two modules are one and two. Applied to (1), these are exactly the two matrix coranks.

Let $\pi:C_S\to C$ normalize the nodes in $S$ and leave the other nodes in place. The preceding local computation shows that a sheaf $F$ with nonfree locus $S$ is $\pi_*N$ for a unique line bundle $N$ on $C_S$. Indeed $\operatorname{End}(F)$ is canonically a subalgebra of the rational function field, and the completed local computation identifies it with $\pi_*\mathcal O_{C_S}$. As a module over this algebra $F$ is invertible, which can be checked after the faithfully flat geometric completed local base change. Conversely a line bundle on $C_S$ pushes forward to a rank-one torsion-free sheaf of the required local types. This argument also handles nonsplit nodes. Cohomology is preserved by the finite pushforward. For the normalization $\nu:P\to C_S$ and $M=\nu^*N$,
$$
\chi(N)=\chi(M)-r=\deg(M)+1-r.
$$
Thus we need exactly $\deg(M)=r-1$ and $H^0(C_S,N)=0$.

**Existence and parametrization of the gluing.** A line bundle $M$ of degree $r-1$ exists on $P$ for every $r$ if $P$ is split and precisely for odd $r$ if $P$ is anisotropic. The conic has a line bundle of degree two, and any odd-degree line bundle could be twisted to degree one. Riemann–Roch would then give an effective degree-one divisor and a rational point. The degree map on $\operatorname{Pic}(P)$ is injective, since a degree-zero line bundle has a nonzero section by Riemann–Roch and this section has empty vanishing divisor.

Fix $M$ of the required degree. For each remaining node $p$, let $L_p=\kappa(p)$ and let $E_p$ be the étale quadratic $L_p$-algebra of its two branches. In the free rank-one $E_p$-module given by the restriction of $M$ to those branches, choose an $L_p$-line spanned by an $E_p$-generator. Given these lines, define $N$ as the subsheaf of $\nu_*M$ whose values there lie in the chosen lines. This is a line bundle by the local node calculation, and every line bundle pulling back to $M$ is obtained this way. Automorphisms of $M$ are multiplication by $k^\times$ and fix all the lines.

After trivializing these $E_p$-modules, the gluing parameters therefore form the $k$-points of
$$
T=\prod_{p\notin S}\operatorname{Res}_{L_p/k}
\bigl(\operatorname{Res}_{E_p/L_p}\mathbb G_m/\mathbb G_m\bigr).
$$
Each quotient over $L_p$ is the open set of lines spanned by units in the projective line of the two-dimensional $L_p$-space $E_p$. The choices are independent from node to node. Over $\overline k$, the vanishing of $H^0(N)$ asks whether the $r$ equations
$$
s(a_j)-\lambda_j s(b_j)=0,\qquad 1\le j\le r,
$$
on $H^0(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O(r-1))$ are independent. Here $a_j,b_j$ are the two preimages of the geometric node and $\lambda_j$ is the gluing ratio in chosen bases. The determinant $D(\lambda)$ is multiaffine. Every coefficient is nonzero since evaluation at any choice of one preimage from each pair gives an isomorphism on the $r$-dimensional section space. For $r=0$, $M$ has degree $-1$, has no sections, and the determinant is the empty determinant $1$.

It follows that the good gluings form a nonempty open subset of $T$ over $k$. If $k$ is infinite, each projective-line open above contains an open of $\mathbb A^1_{L_p}$, whose Weil restriction is an open of $\mathbb A^{[L_p:k]}_k$. So $T(k)$ is dense and there is a good gluing. This proves the infinite-field criterion and the claimed parametrization.

**Finite fields.** A smooth conic over a finite field is split, so $M$ always exists. Choose fiber bases over $E_p$ and order the geometric branches along Frobenius orbits. For a degree-$e$ split node, the geometric gluing ratios may be written as $t,t^q,\ldots,t^{q^{e-1}}$ with $t\in\mu_{q^e-1}$. For a degree-$e$ nonsplit node they have the same expression with $t\in\mu_{q^e+1}$, since the $e$-th Frobenius power exchanges the branches and inverts the ratio. Here $\mu_n$ denotes the $n$-th roots of unity in $\overline{\mathbb F_q}$. Substitution in $D$ gives one variable per closed node. Because its exponents are sums of different powers of $q$ with digits zero or one, separate subsets give distinct exponents. The maximum exponent in the degree-$e$ variable is $1+q+\cdots+q^{e-1}$. A polynomial vanishes on the product of the gluing groups if and only if its remainder modulo the corresponding monic polynomials $t^n-1$ is zero. This follows from interpolation in each variable.

For $q\ge3$, the exponent bound is strictly less than both $q^e-1$ and $q^e+1$. The remainder is the original nonzero polynomial, so some gluing is good. For $q=2$, the bound remains strict for nonsplit nodes. For a split node of degree $e\ge2$, the only two exponents colliding after reduction are $0$ and $2^e-1$. If there is no split node of degree one, choose any intermediate subset of branches in each split variable and any subset in every nonsplit variable. Its coefficient remains nonzero after reduction.

There can be at most one remaining split rational node over $\mathbb F_2$, since its branches use two of the three rational points of $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_2)$. Its gluing ratio is fixed to one. If it is the only remaining geometric node, the unique degree-zero gluing is trivial, so there is no good gluing. If some other node remains, put the rational branches at $0,\infty$. For every other split node use only intermediate subsets of its branch pairs, thereby avoiding a collision of coefficients in its variable. Nonsplit variables permit all subsets. For a selection $c_1,\dots,c_{r-1}$ of one branch from each other geometric node, the corresponding coefficient after substituting the forced gluing is zero if and only if $\prod_i c_i=1$. In fact the section vanishing at the selected points is a scalar times $\prod_i(x-c_i)$, whose values at $0,\infty$ agree exactly under that condition. Each $c_i$ is finite and nonzero. If there is a nonsplit node, two allowed selections differing in a single branch pair give different products. If all other nodes are split, their degrees are at least three. A split degree-two node would use four $\mathbb F_4$-points distinct from $0,\infty$, and $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_4)$ has only three such points. In a set of at least three pairs one can change one choice while keeping the subsets intermediate. Again the products differ. In either case at least one allowed coefficient survives and some gluing is good. This proves the finite-field claims.

For example, over $\mathbb F_2$ the plane cubic $X^3+XYZ+Y^2Z=0$ has one split rational node at $[0:0:1]$, with tangent lines $Y=0$ and $Y+X=0$. It is irreducible by viewing the defining form as a primitive degree-one polynomial in $Z$ over $k[X,Y]$, and has only that singularity by the derivative criterion. The theorem requires corank two at that node in every representation, and
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}
Z&X&0\\
0&Y&X\\
X&Y&Y
\end{pmatrix}=X^3+XYZ+Y^2Z
$$
exhibits such a representation.

**Index and minimum corank-two locus.** If $P$ is anisotropic, every smooth closed point of $C$ has even degree, by the degree calculation on $P$. The pushforward of a degree-two hyperplane section of $P$ gives a zero-cycle of degree two. Thus the index, the gcd of the closed-point degrees, is one if the curve has an odd-degree node and two otherwise. An odd number of geometric nodes can be left unnormalized exactly when there is such a closed node. When the total number of geometric nodes is even, the sum of the degrees of the normalized nodes must be odd, so normalizing one odd-degree node of minimum degree attains the minimum. This completes the proof.

## Theorem 2. Local–global failure with a fixed conic normalization

Let $k$ be a number field and let $P/k$ be an anisotropic conic. For every $d\ge6$ with $d\equiv2\pmod4$, there are geometrically integral nodal plane curves of degree $d$ with normalization $P$ satisfying all of the following.

1. They have a linear determinantal representation over every completion $k_v$.
2. They have no linear determinantal representation over any finite extension of $k$ of odd degree.
3. Over every extension splitting $P$ they have a representation of corank one everywhere on the curve. At a completion where $P$ remains anisotropic, a local representation can be taken to have corank two at exactly one rational node.

Such curves are obtained from a Zariski-dense set in the parameter space of ordered triples of sections of the degree-$d$ line bundle on $P$. The family contains infinitely many curves up to projective equivalence over $\overline k$.

### Proof of Theorem 2

Let $V=H^0(P,\mathcal O_P(d/2))$, where $\mathcal O_P(1)$ is the hyperplane line bundle of the conic, of degree two. Put $A=V^3$. Over $\overline k$ the line bundle in this expression is $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(d)$. A base-point-free triple defines a morphism $P\to\mathbb P^2$.

We first exhibit a nonempty open $U\subset A$ where each map is birational and immersive, all double points have distinct image tangent directions, and there are no triple points. Base points have codimension three at each specified point. Failure of immersion at a point where the value is nonzero has codimension two in the evaluations of the value and first jet. Three distinct points mapping to one has codimension four in their nonzero values. At two distinct points, coincidence of images has codimension two. If both derivatives are nonzero, equality of tangent directions imposes one further independent condition on the first jets. These counts follow by using affine coordinates on the target around the common image. Values and jets at the specified points are independent since the total length considered is at most four and $\mathcal O(d)$ on $\mathbb P^1$ separates such subschemes. Allowing the points to vary adds respectively one, one, three and two dimensions, so the bad loci are not dense.

These conditions also define an open locus. After removal of the base-point and nonimmersion loci, the universal map is separated and unramified over the target plane family. Its diagonal in the fiber square is both open and closed. Thus the distinct ordered collision locus is proper over the parameter open, as is the pairwise-distinct locus of the analogous fiber cube. The tangent-coincidence condition is closed in the distinct pair locus and the triple-point condition is the image of the distinct triple locus. Properness gives the requisite closed images. An immersive map with transverse double points and no triple points is birational onto its image in characteristic zero, since a generically multiple cover would have two preimages of a general image point with the same tangent direction. The map from the smooth conic is the normalization, its image has degree $d$, and its singularities are precisely the transverse two-branch identifications. The number of geometric nodes is
$$
g=(d-1)(d-2)/2.
$$

The universal scheme of ordered pairs of distinct points with coincident images is geometrically irreducible before restriction to $U$. Over $P\times P\setminus\Delta$, evaluation of the triples at the two points is a surjective bundle map. Coincidence of their nonzero images means that the resulting $2$ by $3$ matrix has rank one with both rows nonzero. This is a geometrically irreducible locally trivial rank-one locus, and its inverse image is locally a product with an affine space. Restriction to $U$ preserves geometric irreducibility. The ordered collision scheme is finite étale over $U$. Indeed it is proper by the diagonal argument, and near any collision the two affine image-equality equations have independent derivatives in the two point coordinates precisely because the image tangents are transverse. The same holds for the quotient by exchanging the points, which is a finite étale scheme $Y\to U$ of degree $g$. It is geometrically irreducible and its fibers parametrize the geometric nodes with their Galois action.

Only finitely many places $v$ have $P(k_v)=\varnothing$. This follows, for example, by good reduction away from finitely many primes, existence of a point on a smooth conic over the residue field, and Hensel lifting. For each place in this finite set, put $K=k_v$ and choose a separable quadratic closed point of $P_K$, with geometric pair $a,b$. One may use any line meeting the conic transversely. A degree-two intersection of an anisotropic conic has this form. In the $K$-linear subspace of triples where the last two sections vanish at $a,b$, we can find a triple in $U(K)$ identifying $a,b$. To verify this, work first over $\bar K$ and require the first section to be nonzero at $a,b$. The last two sections belong to the space of sections of the line bundle twisted by $-a-b$, of geometric degree $d-2$. Away from $a,b$ they still separate every jet scheme of length at most four, since $d\ge6$. The preceding codimension counts therefore apply to bad configurations supported away from $a,b$. The condition that any further point map to $[1:0:0]$ has codimension two with a one-dimensional choice of point. At $a,b$ the first derivatives of the last two sections can be freely specified, so the tangents there can be made nonzero and distinct. The resulting open in the constrained linear subspace is nonempty. Since $K$ is infinite, it has a $K$-point.

The unordered pair $\{a,b\}$ gives a $K$-point of $Y$ above that triple. The map $Y(K)\to U(K)$ is locally open at the point by the implicit function theorem for étale maps over the completion, so there is a nonempty local open where the specialized node scheme has a $K$-point. At each anisotropic place impose this open condition.

Apply Hilbert irreducibility with weak approximation to $Y\to U$. It gives a Zariski-dense set of specializations in $U(k)$ with the fiber of $Y$ the spectrum of a field and satisfying all the finitely many local open conditions. For this form of the theorem, the parameters with disconnected fiber lie in a thin set. One can see this by taking the finite étale covers that parametrize proper nonempty subsets of a fiber, using all sizes from $1$ to $g-1$. Every connected component of each such cover has degree greater than one because the generic action is transitive. The approximation statement then follows from Serre's Theorem 3.5.3 and weak approximation for affine space, by avoiding the thin set at additional places away from the given ones. One can also avoid any fixed proper Zariski-closed subset.

A resulting plane curve has a single closed node of degree $g$. This degree is even for $d\equiv2\pmod4$. Theorem 1 rules out a representation over $k$. If $L/k$ has odd degree, $P_L$ remains anisotropic, because any $L$-point would map to a point of odd degree on $P$. Each field factor of the node algebra tensored with $L$ has even degree over $L$, since it contains the original node field of even degree over $k$. Theorem 1 gives the same obstruction over $L$.

At a completion splitting $P$ the choice $S=\varnothing$ is allowed. At any other completion there is a rational node by the approximation condition, so choose $S$ to consist of this one node. Then $r=g-1$ is odd and Theorem 1 gives the required local matrix. The same theorem applies over every extension field splitting $P$, since the infinite-field argument used here applies to every characteristic-zero field. This proves all three properties.

To check the projective-equivalence claim, projectively equivalent images give parameter triples related by a projective target transformation and an automorphism of the normalization, by the universal property of normalization. Over $\overline k$, these arise from the actions of $\mathrm{GL}_3$ on the triples and $\mathrm{GL}_2$ on the homogeneous binary forms of degree $d$. Each orbit has dimension at most $13$, whereas $\dim A=3(d+1)\ge21$. A Zariski-dense set of triples therefore gives infinitely many projective-equivalence classes.

The degree classes for this mechanism are sharp. A plane model with anisotropic conic normalization has even degree because the pullback of the plane hyperplane bundle has even degree on the conic. For $d\equiv0\pmod4$ its arithmetic genus is odd, so an everywhere corank-one representation exists by Theorem 1. In degree two the curve is the conic itself. There the local–global principle is Hasse–Minkowski. Degree six is therefore the first possible failure in the geometrically rational nodal class.

## Literature and scope

The sheaf approach builds on the classical determinantal correspondence. Beauville treats smooth hypersurfaces and plane curves, and Kerner–Vinnikov treat determinantal representations of singular hypersurfaces. The newly derived claims here are the arithmetic corank criterion, including the small finite fields, and the local–global family with a fixed normalization and one global even-degree node. The primary papers consulted did not contain these claims. [Beauville, *Determinantal hypersurfaces*](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9910030) · [Kerner–Vinnikov, *Determinantal representations of singular hypersurfaces in P^n*](https://arxiv.org/abs/0906.3012)

Ordinary linear determinantal representations were previously known to fail the local–global principle for some smooth plane cubics and for a smooth plane quartic. Ishitsuka's conditional positive-proportion result among all smooth plane cubics has a different scope. The construction here proves failure within a different class, with a fixed anisotropic normalization and all admissible failure degrees. [Ishitsuka, *A positive proportion of cubic curves over Q admit linear determinantal representations*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.05167) · [Ishitsuka–Ito–Ohshita, *The arithmetic of a twist of the Fermat quartic*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05902)

For the approximation version of Hilbert irreducibility used in Theorem 2, see [Serre, *Topics in Galois Theory*, §§3.4–3.5](https://indico.math.cnrs.fr/event/11410/attachments/4760/7351/SerreTopicsGaloisTheory.pdf) and [Dèbes, *Density results for Hilbert subsets*, Theorem 3.4](https://pro.univ-lille.fr/fileadmin/user_upload/pages_pros/pierre_debes/A27-HITDensity.pdf).
