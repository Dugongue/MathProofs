# Two-pebble identification for coprime extensions


Grochow and Levet's Question 5.3 asks for constant-pebble identification of coprime extensions of an Abelian group by an Abelian group in Hella's second bijective game. The following cases allow both Abelian groups to have unbounded order and rank. The comparison group can be arbitrary. [Grochow–Levet, Question 5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.13725v7#S5)

**Theorem.** Let $G=N\rtimes H$, with $N,H$ finite Abelian and $\gcd(|N|,|H|)=1$. Suppose at least one of the following holds.

1. On each $r$-primary part of $N$, the action is a direct sum of scalar-character actions over $\mathbb Z/r^e\mathbb Z$, where $r^e$ is that part's exponent. It is sufficient that the action on each primary part have exponent dividing $r-1$.
2. $H$ is elementary Abelian of exponent dividing $3$, with any action on $N$.

The two-pebble, one-round version II of Hella's second bijective game identifies $G$ among all finite groups. By Grochow and Levet's Theorem 7, four pebbles and two rounds suffice in version I. Case 2 includes nonsplit quadratic constituents. [Grochow–Levet, Theorem 7](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.13725v7#S3.SS1)

## 1. What the game supplies

Suppose $G'$ has the same order and a bijection $f:G\to G'$ preserves the marked isomorphism type of every ordered pair. This means that $(x,y)\mapsto(f(x),f(y))$ extends to an isomorphism of the generated subgroups for every $x,y$. Any bijection failing this condition can be defeated immediately by pebbling the offending pair. It suffices to construct an isomorphism given a pair-preserving bijection.

Write $\pi$ for the set of primes dividing $|N|$. The $\pi$-elements in $G$ are exactly $N$ because their images in the $\pi'$-group $H$ are trivial. The bijection preserves orders, so their images are all the $\pi$-elements $N'$ of $G'$. Any two images commute, and products of commuting $\pi$-elements are $\pi$-elements. Thus $N'$ is an Abelian normal subgroup of order $|N|$. The property that a commutator has order supported on $\pi$ is a marked word condition on each pair. It holds on all pairs in $G$ and hence all pairs in $G'$. Thus $H'=G'/N'$ is Abelian and of order $|H|$. Schur–Zassenhaus supplies a complement isomorphic to $H'$.

The condition $xy^{-1}\in N$ is also a word-order test. Therefore $f$ induces a bijection $\theta:H\to H'$ of quotient sets. It preserves quotient element orders, since the order of a coset with lift $x$ is the least positive $j$ for which $x^j$ is a $\pi$-element. In particular $H$ and $H'$ are isomorphic as abstract Abelian groups by their element-order counts. Since the kernels are Abelian, the action on them is independent of the choice of quotient lift. For $v$ in the kernel and $h$ in the quotient, any word identity in $v$ and a lift of $h$ is preserved by $f$. We will use scalar-power action, fixing conditions, and the action of $1+h+h^2$ on the kernel written additively.

## 2. Scalar action and lifting a quotient isomorphism

Let $N_r$ be the $r$-primary part and $R=\mathbb Z/r^e\mathbb Z$, where $r^e=\exp N_r=\exp N'_r$. Let $\mu(R)$ be the prime-to-$r$ torsion of $R^\times$. Reduction gives an isomorphism $\mu(R)\to\mathbb F_r^\times$, since the reduction map on all units is surjective with an $r$-group kernel.

A nonzero $v\in N_r$ has a globally invariant cyclic subgroup precisely when every quotient element sends it to an integer power of itself. If $v$ has order $r^j$, the resulting character into $(\mathbb Z/r^j\mathbb Z)^\times$ has prime-to-$r$ image and lifts uniquely to a character $\chi:H\to\mu(R)$. For each such character let $N_{r,\chi}$ consist of all $v$ with $hv=\chi(h)v$ for every $h$. The nonzero vectors with globally invariant cyclic subgroup are partitioned into these blocks by their reduced-scalar signatures $h\mapsto\overline{\chi(h)}$. Every signature value is read from the marked pair tests of $v$ with all quotient lifts. Thus $f$ matches the nonzero scalar blocks bijectively and preserves each block's element-order distribution. Matched signatures agree along $\theta$.

For any action of the quotient, the operator $P_\chi=|H|^{-1}\sum_h\chi(h)^{-1}h$ has image $N_{r,\chi}$ and is the identity there. Distinct characters differ by a unit at some $h$, so their idempotents are pairwise orthogonal. The same holds for $H'$. In case 1 the source is the direct sum of its scalar blocks. Their matched sizes and $|N_r|=|N'_r|$ imply that the target has zero complementary summand too. Abstract block types match by order distribution. If the action factors through a quotient of exponent dividing $r-1$, scalar averaging on that quotient is complete. Its characters separate points by the finite Abelian cyclic decomposition, and their sum is zero on each nonidentity by the unit-difference argument.

We use the following lifting fact. Suppose $C,D$ are isomorphic finite Abelian groups and $q:C\twoheadrightarrow Q$, $q':D\twoheadrightarrow Q$ are surjective. If
$$
q(C[p^a])=q'(D[p^a])\qquad\text{for every prime }p\text{ and integer }a\ge1,
$$
then some isomorphism $u:C\to D$ satisfies $q'u=q$. Here $C[p^a]$ is the subgroup killed by $p^a$. Choose $C=C_0\oplus C_1$, with $C_0$ a direct summand of maximum order inside $\ker q$, and similarly $D=D_0\oplus D_1$. The images of the indicated subgroups are unchanged on the second summands. The cyclic prime-power decompositions give homomorphisms $a:C_1\to D_1$ and $b:D_1\to C_1$ over $Q$, lifting each cyclic generator's image to an element of order dividing its own order. The endomorphism $ba$ is over $Q$. Its nilpotent part in the Fitting decomposition is a direct summand contained in $\ker q$. In this finite setting sufficiently large powers have stable kernel and image, and restrict to automorphisms on the stable image. Hence the two stable subgroups are complementary. By maximality of $C_0$ the nilpotent summand is zero. The same reasoning applies to $ab$, proving that $a$ is an isomorphism. Finally $C_0\simeq D_0$ by cancellation in the cyclic prime-power decomposition, so $a$ extends to an isomorphism over $Q$.

For case 1 take $q$ to be the joint map on $H$ of all nonzero scalar-block characters reduced modulo their respective primes. Use the matched target characters for $q'$. They have the same image $Q$, since they agree along $\theta$. The lifting hypothesis holds by preservation of quotient element orders. The resulting isomorphism $u:H\to H'$ matches every scalar character, since equality modulo $r$ implies equality in $\mu(R)$. Any matched Abelian block isomorphism is then equivariant, as the actions are scalar. Their direct sum intertwines the entire action and gives $G\simeq G'$.

## 3. Nonsplit blocks for elementary Abelian 3-complements

Suppose that $H$ is elementary Abelian of exponent dividing $3$. So is $H'$ by the quotient order test. The trivial complement case is covered by case 1. Since $r\ne3$, either $r\equiv1$ or $r\equiv2\pmod3$. In the former case the $r$-primary actions split by scalar character averaging. Each nontrivial scalar character is an oriented nonzero functional on the $\mathbb F_3$-space $H$, after choosing a primitive cube root in $\mathbb F_r$, with the same root choice on both sides. The matching of scalar blocks preserves these functional values along $\theta$.

For $r\equiv2\pmod3$ and $L\le H$, let $A_L=|L|^{-1}\sum_{h\in L}h$ act on $N_r$. The trivial block is the image of $A_H$. For every hyperplane $K$ take $N_{r,K}=\operatorname{im}(A_K-A_H)$. These give a direct sum decomposition together with the trivial block. Indeed, $A_KA_L=A_{K+L}$, so the hyperplane differences and $A_H$ are mutually orthogonal idempotents. They sum to the identity in $R[H]$: writing $d=\dim H$, there are $(3^d-1)/2$ hyperplanes and any nonidentity element belongs to $(3^{d-1}-1)/2$. These counts give coefficient one on the identity and zero on every other element.

For each $N_{r,K}$ the action factors through $H/K$. If $h\notin K$, then $1+h+h^2$ acts as zero there because $h$ generates that quotient and $A_H$ acts as zero. Such $h$ fixes no nonzero element in the block, because on a fixed element the same operator acts as multiplication by the unit $3$. Letting $\zeta$ denote the action of a chosen generator of $H/K$, each block is a module over $R[\zeta]/(\zeta^2+\zeta+1)$. This is a local principal ideal ring. Its residue modulo $r$ is a field, and every nonzero element is a power of $r$ times a unit by taking the lesser valuation of its two coefficients. The cyclic elementary divisors of a finite module over this ring are the quotients by $(r^j)$. Each contributes two cyclic groups of order $r^j$ to the underlying Abelian group. Thus the module type is determined by the Abelian group type. Reversing the quotient generator twists the action by the ring automorphism $\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{-1}$, which preserves these module types.

A nonzero $v$ belongs to one block, including the trivial block, exactly when for every $h$, either $hv=v$ or $(1+h+h^2)v=0$. Pure vectors pass. If two distinct blocks support $v$, choose $h$ inside the fixing subgroup of one of them and outside that of the other. This is possible for distinct hyperplanes and when one block is trivial. Then $hv\ne v$ on one component, while $(1+h+h^2)v$ is nonzero on the other. This fails the test. The test uses only word identities on pairs. The signature of a pure vector is its subgroup of fixing elements, which is $H$ for the trivial block and $K$ for a nontrivial block. Therefore $f$ pairs the nonzero blocks, sending each fixing subgroup to its counterpart under $\theta$ and preserving each Abelian type by the order counts.

## 4. Matching all hyperplanes and orientations

Keep one indexed nonzero functional $a_i$ on $H$ for each matched nontrivial block. For split blocks use the orientation encoding the actual character value. For nonsplit blocks choose either defining functional for the block hyperplane. Write $a_i'$ for the paired functional on $H'$. The bijection $\theta$ preserves intersections of any indexed set of kernels. Their cardinalities determine the ranks of the associated column subsets. Thus the two functional lists represent the same ternary matroid, allowing parallel columns.

Here is the elementary projective uniqueness argument. Normalize a selected basis of columns to the identity in each span. The support of each remaining column is determined by the basis-exchange tests. The two trailing matrices thus have the same support bipartite graph, with each nonzero entry a sign. Around a chordless cycle the product of entry-sign discrepancies is $+1$. Indeed the square submatrix on the cycle has exactly two nonzero determinant terms, and equality of its vanishing status in the two matrices forces this product. Vanishing is known from the matroid by adjoining the basis columns at rows outside the cycle. Splitting cycles along chords yields the same discrepancy condition for every cycle. Scale rows and columns by signs to match along a spanning forest. The cycle condition matches all remaining entries, and basis columns can be rescaled to absorb the row scales. This is the Brylawski–Lucas projective uniqueness theorem in the needed form. It gives a linear isomorphism $T:H\to H'$ such that $a'_i\circ T=\epsilon_i a_i$ for signs $\epsilon_i$. If the functionals do not span the whole dual space, extend the dual isomorphism on complementary subspaces arbitrarily. [Brylawski–Lucas uniqueness, as stated and used by Oxley](https://www.math.lsu.edu/~oxley/o_87.pdf)

For split indices the additional identities are $a'_i(\theta(h))=a_i(h)$ for all $h$. Put $\psi=T^{-1}\theta$. Then $\psi$ preserves every zero set, and $a_i(\psi(h))=\epsilon_i a_i(h)$ for split indices. If a matroid circuit contains two split indices $i,j$, choose an $h$ whose evaluations on the circuit's other columns vanish and whose evaluations at $i,j$ are nonzero. Such a vector exists: the image of evaluation on a circuit is the hyperplane defined by its unique linear dependence, with all coefficients nonzero. On $\psi(h)$ the other columns still evaluate to zero. The same linear dependence forces $\epsilon_i=\epsilon_j$.

Two distinct elements of a connected component of a matroid occur in a common circuit. Thus all split indices in each component have the same sign. The component spans form a direct sum, since any minimal dependence meeting two components would be a circuit joining them. Scale each component on the dual space by its common split sign, or by $1$ if it has no split indices, and extend this correction to an automorphism of the dual space. Composing $T$ with the corresponding primal automorphism matches the exact functionals of the split blocks and the hyperplanes of the nonsplit blocks.

We can now match the split and trivial blocks by their Abelian types. The nonsplit blocks also match equivariantly because their module types match and the cyclic quotient map can be reversed by the indicated ring automorphism. Taking direct sums gives an intertwiner and hence $G\simeq G'$. This proves case 2.

## Relation to the open questions

This proves the stated families of cases of Question 5.3 in the second Hella game. The full case of coprime Abelian-by-Abelian extensions and the broader bound in terms of the complement's pebble complexity remain open here. The proof uses a common bijection to test two new elements in one round and does not establish the ordinary one-ary claim. I found no resolution of the separate superlogarithmic-round Question 8.4. For the ordinary versus twisted CFI-group pairs, Collins and Levet give $O(\log\log n)$ rounds in ordinary dimension three, version I. In particular such pairs cannot establish a superlogarithmic lower bound at dimension three or above. [Grochow–Levet, Questions 8.1 and 8.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.11487v7#S8) · [Collins–Levet, Count-Free Weisfeiler–Leman and Group Isomorphism](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11247)
